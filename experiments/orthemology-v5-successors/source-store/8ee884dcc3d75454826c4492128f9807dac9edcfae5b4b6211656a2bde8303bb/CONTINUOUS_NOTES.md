## Windows 001–003: NB-S001-L0001–0039

Complete displays were recorded with every ending marker present; no truncation.

### Source sequence and voices

- Opening sets two tasks: distinguish prophetic signs from other extraordinary occurrences, and explain their evidential relation to prophecy. Ibn Taymiyya first reports rival positions before giving his own classification. The assertion attributed to Abu Ishaq al-Isfarayini/Abu Muhammad Ibn Abi Zayd is expressly questioned as possibly a misreport; do not flatten the reported school classifications into uncontested history (L8).
- One group denies all extraordinary events outside prophecy, reclassifying events involving Mary or a prophet's birth as preparatory signs. The author replies that subsequent followers' wonders can equally be derivative confirmations. Another group allows every extraordinary event on any agent's hands but singles out the claim/challenge/no-opposition combination as authoritative. His objection is internal: unrestricted divine voluntarism has not explained why the conjunction is truth-discriminating (L8–15).
- The author also criticises philosophical reduction to psychic power and moral purity. He ranks prophetic signs, followers' graces, and opponents' wonders, then distinguishes authentication of a religion from infallibility of each follower's utterance. A follower's wonder does not make all his claims binding (L14–15). That is an important restriction on the very supernatural evidence the author affirms.
- L16–20 proposes signs of knowledge and signs of power beyond the relevant created kinds. Some extraordinary performances are ordinary capacities of other creatures or familiar to specialists. These include substantial claims about jinn, angels, sorcery and historical miracles; they are source theses, not independently established empirical findings here.
- L21–27 separates extraordinary ability from righteousness, current spiritual status from eventual status, probable from known judgements about a particular person's outcome, and religiously good, neutral and wrongful uses. The Umm al-Ala/Uthman b. Maz'un report checks overconfident testimony about an individual. The author positively permits knowledge through convergent causes, but does not approve assertions beyond what is known.
- L28–33 sharpens the generic argument: unusualness is relational, unstable and common to prophets and nonprophets; absence of a present rival is not inability of every relevant kind to replicate. Skill in grammar, medicine or astronomy is not prophecy merely because an audience cannot match it. Significance depends on the right comparison class and mode of acquisition, not spectator surprise.
- L33–36 addresses the apparent circularity of identifying signs by previously recognised prophets. In a world with known prophecy, one may compare a new claimant with an already known class, use shared testimony from earlier communities, and distinguish broadly known class facts from specialist testimony about a named future person. He does not require every individual member of a community to testify. He also distinguishes failure to know from comprehensive knowledge of absence and warns that no one has inspected every copy of every earlier book.
- L34 rejects the convenient identification of a Meccan verse's witness with Abd Allah b. Salam on chronological grounds. L35 explicitly allows conditional language to state an impossible or nonactual antecedent; it does not establish that the speaker actually doubts.
- L36–39 turns to the Qur'an's polemical configuration: partner/offspring denials, Moses against denial of the maker, and divine self-sufficiency. The claim that coequal partners imply mutual need is asserted here in compressed form. It is not a newly expanded independent argument closing all the role-versus-power questions. The source connects imposed nonvoluntary generation and need for a helper/successor with the denial of divine offspring.

### Argument reconstruction before appraisal

A truth-discriminating prophetic sign must be more than an unusual event or an unopposed challenge. Its relevant provenance/type and surrounding profile must distinguish a truthful prophet from rival kinds; ordinary followers' derivative signs do not entail their own universal infallibility. Genre recognition using already known cases and cross-community testimony is a legitimate proposed route after prophecy is known. The present passage combines conceptual discrimination with substantive theological and historical premises about what various classes can do.

### Initial reflections and preserved questions

The criticism of audience-relative novelty and unmatched expertise is strong: those features alone plainly do not identify divine attestation. The positive multi-feature approach is more promising than a single miracle-shaped trigger and contains a real scope safeguard for followers. Yet the passage's universal incapacity claims about relevant created kinds need their own warrant; defining a sign as exclusive to prophecy is not enough to establish a particular occurrence belongs to that type. Likewise L35's compressed transition from class to particular must include established relevant membership features, not merely the existence of a class. The source's recognition-through-pattern route deserves assessment in its full later development.

Textual observations to preserve: L15's compressed last opposition clause, L25's philosopher/Abu Hamid comparison, L31's opening fragment, and L32's من يقوم are awkward as received. No repair has been made and no decisive premise is extracted from their suspected corruption. Specific printed controls will be selected after the continuous pass.

## Windows 004–007: NB-S001-L0040–0086

Complete displays were recorded with every ending marker present; no truncation.

### Source sequence and voices

- L40–45 completes the class-recognition discussion. For an initial prophet, the first sign establishes the type and particular together; later prophets can be recognised against an already known type. This is his direct answer to the bootstrap objection, although identifying the first discriminating sign still needs an account. He appeals to characteristic words, actions and profiles, rather than the bare common fact of extraordinary power.
- L46–53 stresses ends, practices and provenance: justice/reform versus deception/wrongdoing, divine service versus demonic aid. Agreement of prophets is distinguished from competitive opposition. Claims about the universality of belief in angels, Aristotle's ignorance, ancient histories, conversion patterns and the motives of opposing communities are polemical/historical assertions, not independently established by this reading. The sequence explicitly faces the objection that prophetic figures historically preceded Aristotle and that every nation was sent a messenger; the replies distinguish existence from surviving/accurate knowledge.
- L51–53 positively proposes knowledge through reports and broad investigation of lives, doctrines and outcomes. Waraqa and the Negus are examples of prior competence recognising a new instance. The hypothesis is that Muhammad's profile more fully realises the already-recognised prophetic type. This is more than a stipulative definition, but its historical and comparative premises must be evaluated separately.
- L54–57 differentiates kinds of divine support. Deliverance and an opponent's humiliation are not the same as total destruction; Abraham's departure and mercy differ from other narratives, and the worldly order is explicitly not a place of complete final recompense. The author notes that the Qur'an does not say Abraham's people were exterminated and uses the distinct purposes of different suras. He infers from the Lot narrative's lack of a polytheism charge that their defining offence was the other wrong described. That inference from narrative focus to their theological status is an authorial interpretation, not historical verification. The broad comparison should not become a crude inference from military success to divine truth.
- L58–62 makes a precise condition-versus-sufficient-ground distinction: being alive is required for reading or knowing but does not itself entail either. Likewise extraordinary occurrence as a necessary condition of a kind of sign is not automatically the feature explaining its force. He challenges theological arguments that unexperienced incapacity proves an effect cannot be within creaturely power. The competing account's nonconcurrent ability theory is not simply his own view.
- L63–64 quotes al-Baqillani at length: nature of sorcery, claim/challenge, divine removal of power or production of an opponent, and the imagined superiority of preventing a previously usual performance. L65 introduces the author's explicit critique. Do not attribute the quoted proposal that magnets or flint could authenticate a prophet to Ibn Taymiyya.
- L65–74 gives objections: the proposed restriction of ability becomes idle if the same kinds can occur without prophecy; stipulating that God always prevents a liar's successful challenge is not a reason within a theology allowing every possible act; claiming an event as a proof does not confer evidential force. Reported false claimants supply empirical counterexamples in his argument, but their stories are not independently authenticated here.
- L73–76 quotes the rationale from avoiding divine incapacity, then attacks its exclusivity and dialectical circularity. An appeal to consensus cannot establish a premise needed to establish prophetic authority if consensus itself depends on that authority. Other paths to knowing a prophet's truth remain open. God can give necessary/noninferential knowledge without eliminating the duty to acknowledge, worship or obey; obligatory acquisition through one specific speculative route is rejected.
- L77–78 is a pivotal adopted clarification, after explicit conditional agreement with the opponent's formal point. A genuine total truth-sign cannot occur with falsehood; that is not the same as saying God lacks power to produce a qualitatively similar extraordinary event. He says the latter is within power but not done as misleading attestation because of wisdom. The relevant divine aim is truth-attestation; the complete sign includes that aim/context. He connects the epistemic inference from orderly work to knowledge with purposive arrangement for an end. On his criticism, denying all final purposes also undermines the opponent's claim that order evidences divine knowledge.
- L79–80 argues that a pure non-opposition account makes the extraordinary event itself dispensable: even normal action becomes miraculous if others are restrained. These are reductios of the opponent's combination, not endorsements of that standard.
- L81–86 begins a broader account of rational religious knowing. The author opposes both uninformed repetition and an imposed speculative method claiming monopoly over knowledge. He distinguishes a sequence's kind from any finitely bounded collection of its members. A successful proof need not be the only possible proof, and someone may know through one sufficient path without having met every other available sign. He criticises both the soundness and the purported universal obligation of the accident/body proof, separately.

### Argument reconstruction and reflection

The strongest affirmative proposal is a purposive, truth-directed attestation account embedded in a rich recognitional profile. It separates the ability to create an event-shape from fitting divine use of it and makes wisdom do genuine inferential work. This gives a source-internal reason that causal origin alone cannot settle attestation. It also avoids requiring everyone to complete a theological proof before any real knowledge.

Its scope remains important. The impossibility of a total truth-indicating sign occurring with falsehood is conditional on that sign's genuinely truth-indicating status. Identifying the divine intention in an actual episode still needs the recognitional and background grounds developed across the book. The author does supply those grounds; they should not be replaced by either a verbal entailment or a demand for one compulsory neutral syllogism.

L62–78 also shows why the book is not a simple endorsement of every anti-deception argument: it rejects a popular proof while preserving an independently motivated conclusion through wisdom. A contributor's successful objection and the truth of their remaining positive theory are repeatedly separated.

Textual observations: L49 has شيرا; L63 القدرة على الدخول في بقرة is part of a quoted example and not silently altered; L67 نختص; L74 احلناه/الكادب; L75's clause إن كان الامر... and فهذا هذا are compressed/damaged; L76 نسلن; L77 contains a difficult progression in the capacity dilemma; L83 عنا and فلا تكون مثلها look damaged. The clear surrounding arguments are preserved; no speculative repair is used.

### Source sequence and voices

- L87–95 traces doctrinal consequences of the accident/body proof across the author's account of Jahmis, Mu'tazila, Ibn Kullab/Ash'aris, Karramiyya and philosophers. It preserves an important independent existential dichotomy: the observed reality either is original/necessary or requires what is; the criticism is of ways of stripping that reality of every actual qualification. The history and accusations are the author's account, not neutral independent historiography.
- The historical Ibn Khuzayma/disciples narrative concerns a conciliatory formula masking different accounts of divine action and speech. Real attributes, renewed voluntary action, and created effects must be distinguished. The text warns that refuting one theological scheme can leave someone mistakenly thinking only an opposing philosophical scheme remains. The eternity of a kind of successive activity is separated from the eternity of any particular created object (L92).
- L93–96 contrasts will, love and approval; ontological permission/creation is not automatically normative love. It also separates valid rational inquiry and desire from blanket school labels. The author criticises overgeneralising against a whole class of rational or devotional activity because of bad instances.
- L97–105 explains why a scripturally taught argument can also be independently rational: human origination, agriculture and rain are observed, rather than known only because a messenger reports them. A 'scriptural argument' can be one scripture teaches, not merely an appeal to its authority. Practical judgements also can have rational grounds. The atomist and philosophical theories are then criticised for allegedly obscuring the real origination of substances rather than merely attributing novel accidents to an unchanged substrate.
- L106–116 presents rival analyses of material transformation, abstract shared quantities and supposed persisting matter. Abstract commonality is not a numerically shared external constituent. The author gives real creation-from-material a stronger role than rearrangement and links the issue to resurrection and the 'eaten person' difficulty. He sometimes ranks an opponent's account as locally better than another's before rejecting its remaining premise (L112).
- These transformation arguments include substantial premodern physical and biological assumptions. His claim that no old matter/substance/accident remains in the new entity should not be silently validated as modern physics or chemistry. The existence of new organised beings and the precise metaphysics of material persistence are distinct questions.
- L117–119 develops material dependence as an indication of creaturely need and separates mental possibility from a real power or receptivity. Mental non-exclusion is not already externally realised capacity. The author asserts that each external novelty is preceded by another external novelty; the argument does not require a first temporal event, and the history/type distinction matters. This positive account is not a generic prohibition of all infinite succession.
- L119–121 explicitly warns that adherents bearing the label 'Sunna' can transmit fabricated reports, deny wisdom, or append an unsound proof. Success in one dispute does not guarantee truth in another. The same applies to the label 'reason'. The proposed remedy is inquiry into the actual proposition and distinction, not rejecting everything on the other side.

### Argument reconstruction and reflection

The epistemology is not simply 'scripture says so'. It distinguishes taught rational signs, true testimony, material transformation and explanatory inference. This supports meaningful rational agency and a real-bearer picture without requiring the critic to accept every historical scientific illustration. Its pressure against a purely nominal or empty creator is substantial. Its particular substrate argument remains a separate metaphysical/physical thesis; recognising observed change does not automatically endorse every statement about what persists through that change.

The broad lesson is methodological: criticism of one proof does not defeat all knowledge; one correct school commitment does not validate the package; a common term can hide several different objects. These controls directly bear on later appraisal of power, wisdom and attestation.

Textual observations retained: L90's repeated فانكروه and لن يكن; L93's الآله/الآله repetition; L100–104 compressed/corrupt constructions; L104 تشفي غليلا ولا تروي غليلا; L115 says المشهود...إحداثه...من غير مادة despite a surrounding argument for creation from material; L116 لا أن الأجسام... also appears compressed. L118 contains a substantial modal-to-prior-material inference that must be assessed as an argument, not silently corrected or collapsed into mere logical possibility.

### Source sequence and voices

- L122–124 distinguishes corrupted doctrinal claims from altered commands and obligations. The author again separates the truth of a route from a claim that it is universally obligatory. The hadith of four reader/believer profiles yields a distinction between truthful verbal form and an inward believing condition; good words and good states need not always coexist.
- L124–131 develops love, worship and ends. Desire for a wage or fear of a powerful person's harm can motivate obedience without making the person intrinsically loved. The author's positive religious account includes final love as well as reward. He argues that vision/love of God belongs within the promised garden, rather than requiring contempt for other goods. Devotional anecdotes criticise unrealistic boasts of indifference to pain or created need; their exact histories are not independently authenticated.
- L130 interprets surrender, sincerity, humility and wholehearted orientation as complementary aspects, rather than mutually exclusive definitions. L131 connects bodily facing with inward orientation; the universal psychology should not be silently enlarged beyond the source's intended devotional context.
- L132 answers the alleged requirement of likeness for love. Parentage, species identity or equality are not necessary; a lovable quality is. This is a positive perfection-giver comparison, not merely negative theology: the giver of good and the union of power with mercy are offered as grounds of love.
- L133–136 gives the philology of al-Wadud and defends genuine divine loving, including love following repentance and obedience. He distinguishes it from an eternally fixed love based only on one's eventual end, and from treating love solely as a separate created benefaction. The discussion uses context and morphology, cited exegetes and hadith; narrated chains are part of its source apparatus, not new authenticity verification by this reader. The giver-of-love priority is explicitly argued at L136. The pastoral consequence is that sinners should not regard repentance as meeting only a harsh, unresponsive source.
- L137–138 separates the will to produce something from love for an existing object/end. Production aims at a future result, but what is produced may continue to be loved when it exists. Building a house is a means to its use; the final object is not reducible to the act of bringing it into existence. The passage then argues that a produced thing is originated, independently of whether voluntary production has yet been established. The final sentence continues in the next reader’s contiguous window 014, so that join must be retained.

### Argument reconstruction and reflection

These pages provide positive content for purposive agency and source-owned love while preserving real relations to created events. They are not a generic inference from any newly achieved end to externally supplied constitutional deficiency. They also distinguish doing, the object done and the reason for doing, so later sourcehood and attestation arguments must respect those types.

Textual observations: L122 ومنهم من لم ير may contain a troublesome negation; L130 لفظ الاسلام يتضمن الاسلام is tautological as received; L134's القول الأول follows several levels of reported alternatives and should not be used as an isolated endorsement of the first quoted denial of renewed love. L135's conclusion beginning فالصواب القطع بأن الودود is compressed. L138 العلة الغائبة/بالاختبار look corrupted. No raw text was emended. These local flags do not erase the clear context, but they prevent overconfident isolated quotation.
