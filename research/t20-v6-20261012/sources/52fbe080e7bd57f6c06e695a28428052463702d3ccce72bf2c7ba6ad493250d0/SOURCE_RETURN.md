# Later ignorance and continuing practical obligation

9 October 2026 UTC. Source return to the preserved communication report from Ibn Taymiyyah’s Majmūʿ al-Fatāwā 14:142–167, especially 149–160. The initial electronic division was followed through the actual chapter ending in the already-acquired scan.

## What changes

The earlier distinction between successful original delivery and later failure of effective receipt now has direct source support. Ibn Taymiyyah expressly holds that the messenger removed burdens while later people remained burdened because they did not know the relief and their teachers or rulers misidentified the law. The original law’s continuing validity, individual knowledge of it, its practical benefit, and an interpreter’s current duty therefore come apart within his own argument.

This strengthens the earlier obstruction and sharpens its obligation clause. **A genuine current duty to follow or state the result of qualified inquiry does not entail that the agent knows the source’s actual ruling.** The proposed obligation-to-access inference remains available only for an obligation whose specified performance requires adequate warranted uptake of the actual source-attributable content. Merely establishing a continuing practical duty is insufficient. The immediate continuation also explicitly recognizes cases of beneficial obscurity, while maintaining that the truth is one; this is an additional source-owned limit on an unrestricted disclosure norm.

## The argument in its continuous setting

The selected electronic division opens at 142 with the answered prayer at the end of Sūrat al-Baqarah. At 143 the author rejects treating prayer as causally idle merely because its outcome has been decreed: causes operate with conditions and absent impediments. At 144–147 he gives commands and prohibitions genuine wisdom and distinguishes benefit in the act from benefit in the command, including obedience and resolve. At 147–149 he returns to how answered prayer can remain useful for individual participants.

The distinction at 149–150 is explicit: a benefit secured for the community collectively need not be secured for every individual. Its individual enjoyment can involve conditions of obedience. This is a claim about the specified promises and benefits, not a preservation theorem about every item of revelation. It blocks an automatic distributive inference from the community’s good to each person’s reception; it does not establish either total communal loss or perpetual individual access.

At 151 he raises a harder case. Relief from liability for mistake and forgetfulness is part of the law and applies even to a sinner. Someone who forgetfully eats while fasting is not disqualified from that legal relief merely because they sin elsewhere. This prevents interpreting the later deprivation as an unnoticed repeal of the source’s original permission or exemption.

At 151–152 the explanation instead concerns knowledge: a person may not know the relief, may lack someone who gives the permissive ruling, or may trust only scholars whose mistaken judgment imposes liability. The deprivation is “لعدم العلم لا لنسخ الشريعة”, due to lack of knowledge rather than abrogation. The law can therefore remain genuinely permissive while a particular recipient’s effective guidance incorrectly treats it as restrictive.

At 153–155 he extends this to permitted goods, transactions and travel practices. At 155 the central formulation is especially direct: “وإن كان الرسول قد وضعها لكنهم لم يعلموها”. The messenger removed the burdens, but those people did not know that. He adds cases where an official, mufti, endowment supervisor or ruler imposes restrictions through a false belief about the law. The leader can cause harm through ignorance rather than intentional injury. An attribution by an authoritative intermediary is consequently not identical to the original source’s act of prohibition.

This is stronger support for the diachronic distinction than a bare imagined later-loss model. It supplies the author’s own account of continuing original guidance coexisting with real local deprivation through erroneous mediation. Its mechanism is ignorance of the legal relief or its evidential grounds, not an established loss of the original text. The passage directly establishes absent knowledge and deficient effective guidance, sometimes including the absence of a suitable adviser; it does not establish that every possible warranted route was unavailable. Thus it is not itself a historical witness of total epistemic inaccessibility. Extending it to globally irrecoverable wording would still require a separate argument.

## The practical obligation is real but differently targeted

At 153–154 the author does more than describe a psychologically compelling false belief. He says the mujtahid must state what their inquiry leads to: “فإن المجتهد عليه أن يقول ما أدى إليه اجتهاده”. He describes the resulting restriction as sharʿī in the apparent situation while explicitly saying that God and the messenger did not prohibit the underlying permitted thing.

The passage therefore distinguishes:

1. **The actual source ruling:** the thing is permitted, or the original burden has been lifted.
2. **The agent’s evidential judgment:** their inquiry, limited evidence or trusted mediation instead supports restriction.
3. **The operative practical requirement:** they can be required to judge according to their ijtihād rather than assert a conclusion their inquiry does not support.
4. **Experienced consequence:** they and those who follow them can consequently lose the benefit of the original permission.

The third is not merely an inert error. Neither does it convert the first into an actual prohibition by the original source. The source distinguishes practical normativity under the agent’s circumstances from correctness of the attributed first-order ruling.

This yields an exact test of the earlier bridge. Let Oᵢ be the qualified duty to respond according to one’s inquiry, and let Kₛ(C) mean adequately warranted knowledge that C is the original source’s ruling. The passage allows Oᵢ while Kₛ(C) is absent and the operative first-order judgment is mistaken. Hence **Oᵢ ⇒ Kₛ(C)** is not source-supported. This does not by itself refute Oᵢ ⇒ A where A means an adequate opportunity that the agent has not used: opportunity and actual knowledge remain distinct. Rather, the passage identifies why Oᵢ has a different normative target, so a source-truth-access conclusion needs its own bridge. No new general mathematical theorem is involved.

The stronger proposed bridge used O꜀: a determinate obligation whose required mode of compliance is successful, responsible uptake of the actual content C as the source’s teaching. Whether O꜀ is presently imposed, unexcused and supported by an adequate opportunity must still be established. The new passage does not show that such a demand can coherently require knowledge with no adequate opportunity. It shows why one cannot infer O꜀ simply from the presence of Oᵢ, continuing objective law, or an officially enforced restriction.

This also qualifies an overly simple response to loss: the recipient’s practical obligations need not all disappear or become normatively inert when their understanding of the primary rule is wrong. They can have a different, circumstance-relative duty. That is an actual rival account of normative guidance, not a verbal relaxation of every obligation.

## The immediate continuation recognizes beneficial obscurity

At 156–158 the author continues the prayer’s interpretation and returns to historical instances of altered practical burdens. At 159–160 he then qualifies the deprivation account in a way that matters directly here. Disagreement in rulings can be mercy if the obscurity does not produce great harm, although the actual truth is one. Some facts or rulings can mercifully remain unknown because their discovery would bring severity. In contrast, ignorance of a dispensation can bring punishment. Even removing doubt, he adds, can have either effect.

His examples include the night of decree remaining unspecified so that people strive throughout the ten nights, and goods whose wrongful origin is unknown to a recipient who is consequently not liable as one who knows would be. These are the author’s examples, not independent adjudications of their historical or legal particulars.

This is a substantive rival to the premise that fuller or easier disclosure is always required by wise guidance. A communicative or normative good can concern the agent’s appropriate inquiry and action rather than possession of every true detail. The intended task can therefore have a coarser informational target than complete historical or factual recovery. This is not epistemic relativism: the author expressly maintains one actual truth.

It does not vindicate falsely attributing an invented message to the commissioned source, nor show that a teaching specifically promised as accessible can be left unavailable without consequence. Beneficial obscurity must fit the actual purpose and the legitimate conditions of the recipient’s obligation. These examples provide no general licence to manufacture ignorance, conceal relevant evidence from others, avoid warranted inquiry or evade recognized duties. The new text supplies examples and an articulated possibility, not a blanket defence of every apparent communication failure. It also directly defeats a converse inference from present ignorance to punishment: the same author expressly describes some ignorance as mercy.

The remaining continuation, 160–167, discusses beneficial knowledge, obedience, inward and external reward, and rejects reducing the revealed account of the afterlife to an exclusively inward condition. It does not turn the qualified discussion of beneficial obscurity into general indifference to truthful revealed teaching. No separate afterlife or doctrinal verdict is drawn here.

## Culpability cannot be read backward from difficulty

The author gives the selected deprivations a substantive moral-causal explanation: failures of obedience can result in loss of beneficial knowledge, and he explicitly interprets several examples that way. This is part of his account and should not be suppressed.

It does not license this research to diagnose a particular person’s ignorance, suffering, legal disagreement or burdensome practice as proof of personal guilt. A claim that wrongdoing can cause epistemic deprivation does not establish that every deprivation has that cause or identify who bears responsibility in a particular transmission chain; the author’s explicit mercy cases at 159–160 now supply a direct textual reason against that universal converse. Nor does a teacher’s mistaken ruling alone show intentional deception, as the author’s own distinction between ignorance and intended injury confirms. The explicit point at 151 about the sinner’s retained legal exemption also blocks turning moral blame into an automatic alteration of the original law.

Accordingly, the earlier culpable-loss exception gains a source-owned causal setting, but no named recipient has been shown to fall under it. For an actual application one must establish the relevant earlier opportunity, failure and dependency; an unexplained present difficulty cannot substitute for those facts.

## Bounded connection to information gain

The inherited Bregman information-gain result concerns a fixed truth-score and target. The present source distinguishes that epistemic improvement from a change in the agent’s applicable practical requirements when knowledge changes. Better information under the fixed score therefore cannot simply be transported into lower total practical burden or universal normative benefit: the practical evaluation and potentially its action constraints differ. This preserves the inherited theorem and its scope; it introduces neither a new theorem nor a recommendation to remain ignorant.

## What remains positively established

The new unit affirms the beneficial original law and its real removal of burdens. It does not retract the earlier Darʾ account of truthful explanatory guidance. Its constructive lesson is to locate a failure at the right stage: original ruling, evidence or interpretation, current professional judgment, enforced practice, and individual reception are distinguishable. Correcting a mistaken attribution can restore access to an already valid permission without positing a new law or a defective original messenger.

The access criticism remains forceful if it establishes a continuing, appropriately scoped undertaking of adequate access to actual C, and a failure of all warranted routes within that undertaking. But individual deprivation does not by itself disprove original delivery, a collective promise or every continuing form of practical guidance. The new source makes those distinctions substantive parts of the account being tested.
