## 1. The connected argument

The range begins in an already-running debate about miracles. Its main burden is to defeat an explanation that reduces prophetic signs to unusual psychic powers without embracing the opposite error of denying created causal efficacy. That double refusal structures the apparently digressive discussions of will, human action, natural powers, angels, epistemology and prophecy.

First, the author rejects mere occasional conjunction as a complete account of eating, drinking and other familiar effects (SF-S001-L0239–0240). He then disputes the extension from admitted natural powers to every prophetic sign. Staff-transformation and other cases are said to differ from an unusually strong instance of ordinary psychophysical action (L0243–0245). The problem is thus not “there are causes” but “these are the exhaustive and adequate causes of these particular events”. The relevant standards are causal capacity, receptive conditions, scale or kind of effect, knowledge and intention, temporal availability, and the quality of evidence that establishes the event itself.

The excursus through kalām is integral. A generic will equally related to alternatives cannot itself explain determinate specification (L0257–0263). Equally, identifying God's creating with the created product generates a false dilemma between genuine human action and divine creation (L0270–0273). Once act and product are distinguished, the author affirms both. Created powers really contribute, but none independently suffices; they need collaborators and are defeasible by impediments (L0275–0276). This establishes the account with which subsequent psychic, magical, angelic and prophetic cases must be read.

The ensuing numbered objections are cumulative. The philosophical account lacks evidence for actuality and exhaustiveness (L0293–0298); it cannot use experience selectively while excluding other claimed evidence in advance (L0299–0303); it improperly extrapolates limited powers into capacities neither demonstrated nor admitted (L0317–0321); and it treats several distinct informational routes as one (L0330–0335). Long narratives of jinn and magic are not distractions from the structure: they are the author's proposed counterexamples to the exhaustive classification. They are, however, assertions and testimonies presented by the treatise, not independently verified observations available to this study.

The author then distinguishes audiences. An interlocutor accepting prophetic truth may be challenged with what prophets say about external speaking angels; an interlocutor denying prophethood first needs evidence establishing it (L0341, L0394). Scriptural descriptions and an argument from alleged independent concordance across accounts jointly resist reducing angelic communication to an internal image. The literal truth, knowledge and honesty of prophets are set against the claim that beneficial public representations can be false in fact (L0365–0366). The author also uses scripture to distinguish different modes of revelation and command rather than flatten them into a single faculty (L0368–0373).

A further metaphysical strand opposes necessary emanation. Angels obey, receive new commands, fear and seek permission; they are not fixed emanations whose effects follow without renewed direction (L0378–0389). The generation/partnership argument at L0391–0392 builds on cooperative causality among creatures and the inability of an eternally complete cause to account for newly arising effects. It is deliberately an argument against a specified necessitating account, not a statement that voluntary divine creation is itself a two-origin natural process.

The ninth reply is especially discriminating. An individual soul cannot intentionally perform what happens before its existence, without its awareness, or after its death (L0395–SF-S002-L0003). Yet personal excellence and prayer can genuinely contribute to divine response (SF-S001-L0399–0400). The tenth reply accepts gifted capacities in prophets but rejects their sufficiency as the whole definition or cause of prophecy (SF-S002-L0004–0013). Prophecy involves particular divine election and address, not merely acquired readiness for a general outflow.

After a provisional closing statement at L0021, the range broadens into intellectual history, semantics, the distinctness of the Creator and the authority of prophets relative to saints. A necessary existent is not yet a Creator distinct from the world (L0043–0046). Inward inspiration can be genuine but fallible; presumed immediacy does not make it superior to reliable prophetic mediation (L0065–0072). The final section explains an alleged inversion of authority in philosophical mysticism and ends with a restrictive kalām proof-chain the author is preparing to criticise (L0129–0135). That final critique is not complete within this range.

## 2. Particular necessary bearer and mental universal

Two related but distinct criticisms must be kept apart.

At SF-S001-L0284 the author denies an extramental absolutely simple universal. A universal considered without a restrictive stipulation nevertheless exists outside the mind only in determinate particulars; its mental universality is its applicability to many particulars. The received wording concerning shared predication is defective or at least suspicious enough to require scan control. The general anti-reification point is also explicit at SF-S002-L0043.

At SF-S002-L0044 the author makes a further logical point: proving some self-necessary existent is compatible with taking the world itself as necessary. Consequently, that proof does not yet establish the distinct Maker. The intended conclusion requires a discriminating premise. He criticises obtaining it by the denial of attributes and composition, because on his account that route leaves only a mental abstraction (L0044–0046).

Thus this range is useful both against treating a mental universal as an external bearer and against treating an underdetermined existential result as already identifying the theological bearer. It does not provide here a fresh, fully reconstructed proof of every positive divine attribute. Nor does the criticism establish that no necessary-existent argument can ever contribute; its stated target is this route and its insufficiently discriminating conclusion.

## 3. Source, perfection and causal conditions

The clearest local comparative case is the embryo said to be more complete than the womb and its powers (SF-S001-L0278). It appears while criticising the attribution of differentiated, purposive formation to a nature that is only an accident in a body, without knowledge or volition. The author demands evidence for the proposed active intellect and substitutes intelligent voluntary agency under divine creation. This has genuine source/perfection significance, but a bounded one: it challenges a proposed local explanatory source for this organised effect.

The more explicit general claim is non-independence of created causes (L0276, L0391–0392, L0399–0400). Sunlight needs a recipient; fire needs combustible matter; prayer is a cause of a response, not its sole independent producer. Actual creaturely agency coexists with dependence on God. Generated human effects also depend on other causes, which the criticised Muʿtazilite account is said to neglect (L0267–0273).

These claims should not be collapsed into one unqualified “all causation transmits every perfection from every requisite” principle. The text differentiates a creator, an acting agent, a product, a recipient, a contributory cause, a condition and an impediment. It does not show that these relations are interchangeable or that every condition bears every positive quality of the completed result. The very fire/recipient example makes their different causal roles salient.

### Actual common-Creator hypothesis versus universal SourceMode

For the actual world as he understands it, the author expressly affirms one God who creates every thing and its causes, sustains their continuing dependence, and produces events by will and intended wisdom. This is an **actual common-Creator explanatory commitment**. The polemic compares it with local natural powers, psychism, ten intellects, necessary emanation and occasionalism, finding the latter accounts inadequate in distinct ways.

A **universal every-original/every-requisite SourceMode claim** would be stronger and differently quantified: for every original bearer and every relevant requisite, in all admitted cases, the requisite's source relation must confer or exceed each stipulated perfection. This range does not state that general schema, specify its variables, or prove that every receptive condition, cooperating cause and originating agent instantiates one source mode. The actual Creator's uniqueness and dependence of creatures cannot by themselves fill that gap. Nor may the comparative embryo example be promoted into the missing universal merely because it points in a compatible direction.

Conversely, the range does not refute every possible carefully restricted source/perfection argument. It supplies material requiring a narrower formulation, explicit causal roles and proof of the particular bearer. It yields neither a predetermined global victory nor a predetermined failure for a formal thesis not fully stated here.

## 4. Epistemic routes, signs and attestation

The epistemology is plural and audience-sensitive. Sense, rational inference, recurrent observation, truthful report, broad transmission, independent concordance, scripture, and inward disclosure all occur, but they do not have equal authority or identical conditions.

- The moon argument uses public recitation before questioning and hostile audiences to infer that a notorious falsehood would have provoked objection (SF-S001-L0246–0254).
- Detailed past speech and future particulars require an informational route not supplied by a general syllogistic capacity alone (L0255).
- A possibility judgement does not identify the cause that actually operated; absence of one proof does not demonstrate non-existence (L0293–0298, L0317–0319).
- The Abraham/Lot concordance argument depends explicitly on absence of borrowing or collusion and unusual agreement in detail (L0345–0346). Those are assumptions requiring their own historical support if the argument is reused outside the treatise.
- Multiple kinds of extraordinary event do not automatically provide the same religious attestation. Allegedly demonic transport or magical influence can coincide with falsehood, moral failure and invalid religious practice (L0321, L0336–0339).
- Inward inspiration is conceded but checked against a more reliable communicated standard (SF-S002-L0067–0072). Directness is not a sufficient reliability criterion.
- Uncertain inherited reports and a disputed reading prompt conditional argument or suspension, not obligatory affirmation (L0076–0078, L0089–0090).

The author often claims necessity or wide transmission for his evidence. A study of his argument can faithfully record these claims without pretending to possess the same observations, without validating every report and without erasing the antecedent conditions on which his inference depends.

## 5. Wider material that must remain visible

The range includes the theology of human speech and recitation; grammar and lexical distinctions; a dispute over female prophecy; discussions of angels, jinn, magic and pilgrimage; polemical geographical judgements; interpretations of bodily resurrection; intercession; the reception and disputed attribution of al-Ghazālī's works; saintly authority; preservation of scripture and Sunna; allegations of antinomianism; religious hierarchy; and political conflict in Egypt and Syria.

Its severe condemnations coexist with express allowances for forgiven error where capacity or established proof is lacking (SF-S002-L0018, L0110), concessions to genuine psychic or inspired phenomena, and differentiation among Sufi and kalām traditions. These qualifications are not optional decoration. Omitting them would produce a systematically harsher and more uniform position than the supplied argument supports.

The legal-punitive passages at L0096–0098 and the attributed dictum at L0115 are recorded as historical arguments of the source. They are not recommendations or present-day legal conclusions. Likewise, hostile reports about named individuals and communities must not be silently transformed into independent biographical evidence.
