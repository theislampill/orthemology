## Knowledge, guidance and sufficient cause

229–230 distinguishes knowledge as a governing standard from knowledge as a directing guide. At 231–235, knowing, intending, having power and removal of impediments are explicitly different: explanatory guidance is a condition without necessitating successful guidance, while the stipulated complete guidance includes enabling. This is a source-supported reason to keep information, motivation, capacity and realised conformity separate.

241–242 divides knowledge into productive prior knowledge of a voluntary actor's intended act and knowledge that follows an independently existing object without producing it. Both count as perfections. The author's explicit error diagnosis is judging universally from a partial instance. The examples of receptive knowledge concern our knowledge of prophets, nations, kings and other existents. This passage does not independently show that an uncreated being acquires a constitutive perfection from another original source. It does oppose equating every occurrence of knowing with efficient production of its object.

243–263 fairly reconstructs two positions on whether knowledge necessitates guidance. The author's own resolution begins at 263–264: incomplete disposition, absent condition and opposing impediment must be distinguished from a sufficient cause. Recognition can coexist with failure to follow. At 271–278 he adds a dynamic claim: rejection and impediments can subsequently obscure, weaken or erase knowledge. This later deterioration must not be retrojected to deny every earlier knowing refusal. A model using only monotone accumulation of information would miss this claimed feedback.

## A useful logical and interpretative control

At 246–247, the first party considers the hadith that whoever is intended good is given understanding. An objection correctly distinguishes the displayed conditional from its converse. The reply appeals to the utterance's function of designating understanding as a sign; that is an additional interpretative premise, not converse implication by propositional logic alone. The supplied marginal note repeats the objection, and the modern editor defends the author's response. Preserve the three voices: staged argument, marginal critic and editor. This is an excellent future example for occurrence-sensitive pragmatics: a theorem can verify the strengthened sign relation once supplied, but cannot derive the speech-act premise merely from the displayed implication.

## Source criticism and wider material

The supplied apparatus at 257 flags an impossible or doubtful chronology for the cited al-Miswar/Abu Jahl narration; at 258 it records disagreement about the nine-signs report; at 267 it flags the dating of wine prohibition in the al-Asha story; at 269 it notes interpolations in the poem. These examples are not freshly authenticated historical evidence merely because their argumentative use is intelligible.

259–262 separates recognising or reporting a messenger's truth from adherence, commitment and love. This supports separating assertion, recognition and normative undertaking, without presupposing that this reading authenticates any claimed messenger.

264–271 lists impediments including reception, envy, status, material incentive, family, home, concern for ancestors, hostility to earlier adherents, and habituation. Preserve these as the author's account, not a clinical diagnosis or an explanation of any current reader's disagreement. At 270–271 habituation is described as weaker intrinsically yet widely prevalent; theoretical causal strength and population prevalence are different measures.

276–278 distinguishes literal absence of information from calling someone ignorant because they do not use what they know, or because the expected fruit is missing. Lexical denial need not deny every underlying cognitive capacity. This bears on formalising ordinary source language without collapsing its contextual senses.

## Assessment

The passage strengthens the methodological case for typed causal and epistemic interfaces and contributes a concrete source-side example of the need for a pragmatic bridge. It neither independently supplies numerical source unity nor refutes the full source-mode argument. Its theological, historical and psychological claims remain at their actual source/evidence scope.

## Additional bounded reflection: 455–467 and 496–513

The [coverage record](READING_RECEIPT.json) records three further untruncated windows. Together with 229–278 these total 81 distinct electronic positions.

455–467 contextualises entrusted preservation of revelation. At 460 a limited act of delegation does not license the unrestricted title 'God's agent'; a scoped act predicate does not automatically yield a global status predicate. At 461–462 God’s delegation is expressly beneficent enabling, not delegation from need; likewise affirming friendship is distinguished from friendship through abasement. This is an affirmative source account of real mediated work under divine self-sufficiency, not permission to substitute independently original helpers. The relation's ground matters as well as its surface verb.

462–467 also demonstrates why a claim about trustworthy transmitters cannot bypass evidence criticism: Ibn al-Qayyim argues from the report about upright bearers of knowledge, while the supplied editor judges several transmission paths severely weak or fabricated and qualifies the Ahmad attribution's endpoint. Preserve the author's inference and the apparatus's dissent; this reading does not itself adjudicate the full hadith criticism. Uprightness is expressly distinguished from sinlessness at 463.

496–499 distinguishes being followed intentionally from a path's being followed without awareness, and an expression's lexical meaning from its consequence. The phonetic interpretation of 'umma' is attributed linguistic argument, not independently established etymology. Gratitude's three components are recognising the benefit, assigning it to its benefactor and using it fittingly; those are distinct epistemic and practical acts.

500–502 gives an unusually precise account of downstream credit: an agent's voluntary act is a genuine part of a composite cause, while the resulting thirst, fatigue or another's response is not itself that agent's direct act. Credit for the consequence is related to its contribution, without making the act a sufficient producer. This aligns with typed contribution/occurrence distinctions, but does not supply a quantitative attribution formula or solve all causal credit allocation.

503–508 considers increased responsibility from knowledge and mitigation by other goods together; the source does not make either consideration an unconditional global ranking. Forgiveness is not proof that the act was correct, and 'upright' need not mean every act is beyond criticism. 511–513 separately rejects the blanket claim that knowledge is always merely instrumental: some knowledge is an end, some instrumental; action likewise. This cautions against collapsing epistemic good into a single downstream utility score.

## Further direct control of equilibrium argument 892–910

All nineteen positions were read, with 902–906 filling the intervening context after the first display. The overall separate reread union is now 100 positions, not an additional full-volume pass.

The section starts with classifications of benefit and harm, explicitly distinguishes a good in itself from costs of obtaining it at 895, and at 896–898 argues that equal opposed benefit and harm cannot simply cancel. It does not overlook the objection that each cause encounters an impediment: that objection is written at897 and answered by a priority claim that a cause's producing its own effect is stronger than its preventing the other's effect. The answer then moves from prevention of an effect to the cause being ineffective or annulled. That priority/identity step carries the burden. If production and inhibition have different objects, saying a power prevents one effect but fails to produce another is not immediately a contradiction. The restricted reading involving simultaneously sufficient independent causes of contradictory outcomes is much easier to defend, but does not cover every partial opposed contribution.

The legal counterexamples899–908 matter for scope. The author permits a choice between equal alternatives and narrows his disputed claim to mutually cancelling effects; he is not denying all equality or all permissible nonunique decisions. The ethical examples retain historically specific legal classifications, not current instructions. In particular his ship example rejects sacrificing one equally protected person's life merely to preserve another; that constraint is not a numerical expected-value calculation.

909–910 expressly permits a sharp sword to be prevented from cutting without losing its sharpness. The author understands retained capacity under inhibition. A criticism must therefore identify the additional assumptions about mutual inhibition, not say he simply conflates every impeded effect with destroyed capacity. The accompanying dietary/medical causal generalisations are historical claims; necessity does not medically guarantee absence of harm.

This is a useful open formalisation target for distinguishing power, specific exercise, prevention and resultant effect. No new theorem, empirical refutation or complete philosophical refutation is claimed from this selected comparison.
