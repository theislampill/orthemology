# Full-source summary

Smith contrasts Rawls's reflective equilibrium and metaethical avoidance with Hare's prescriptivism and utilitarianism, finding problems in both. He proposes beginning with moral rationalism and a naturalistic account of agency. Agents acquire knowledge and realize intrinsic desires; ideal agents exercise these capacities maximally and robustly. Diachronic and interpersonal vulnerabilities allegedly require ideal agents, supported by similarly ideal companions, intrinsically to promote everyone's agency and refrain from interfering with its noninterfering exercise. Self-control restores these motivations in nearby nonideal worlds. Ideal counterparts' advice then grounds unconditional moral reasons to help and not interfere, alongside conditional nonmoral reasons to pursue personal ends. All three are pro tanto and can outweigh one another. Their contents and weights remain partly vague; suitably constrained reflective equilibrium, personal commitments, and social rules may precisify them. Moral wrongness does not by itself authorize sanctions. The resulting agenda includes nonmoral wrongdoing, civility, legitimate sanctioning, and how obligations change under radically degraded social conditions.
