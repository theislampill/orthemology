# Avicenna on intellectual production and non needy willing

9 October 2026. Bounded primary-source adversarial return. Own analysis only; no protected edits, integration, closure, recalibration, or historical research-duration claim.

## Result

**Avicenna's own account does not supply a knowing-but-involuntary original producer in the unqualified sense.** It explicitly ascribes intellectual will, knowing acceptance, love, generosity, and providence to the First; excludes an ignorant natural outflow; and makes the apprehended order of good productively determinative. It is therefore not a counterexample to every non-needy knowing-and-willing original source.

**The disagreement is nevertheless substantive.** Avicenna identifies divine intellectual willing with knowledge, expressly even in concept; denies an additional executive intention; treats the good order as derivatively loved through the First's self-love; denies that lower effects can be primary purposes of higher causes; and excludes new intrinsic states of the First. His strongest reason for the disputed purposive restriction is that any nonfutile intention allegedly obtains a perfection that its intender would otherwise lack. That premise, rather than the bare word *will* or the bare fact of necessitation, marks a genuine point of disagreement with intrinsically wise, non-needy practical agency.

The result refines the rival's identity. It does not establish the rival's metaphysical possibility, truth, equal credibility, or victory over the affirmative practical-perfection appraisal. It supplies no independent countermodel to original perfect agency. The earned affirmative appraisal should remain, with the dispute located more accurately.

## Primary source and exact reading scope

The principal source is Ibn Sīnā's **al-Shifāʾ, al-Ilāhiyyāt**, the critical text attributed on its scan cover to Georges C. Anawati and Saʿīd Zāyed, reviewed and introduced by Ibrāhīm Madkūr. The scan has 479 PDF pages; the checked printed page is PDF page minus one. The scan's precise reprint imprint is not established by the available cover/front matter. Rafed's separate electronic bibliographic entry identifies a Marʿashī Najafī printing, 1404 AH. These are not silently treated as proof that the scan is that exact printing.

Continuous main-text reading covered:

- VIII.6, printed 355–362, complete, on perfection, self-intellection, and knowing particulars.
- VIII.7, printed 362–370, complete, on intellectual production, will, attributes, and intellectual pleasure.
- IX.1, printed 373–381, complete, on first causality and the impossibility of a first temporal activation from an unchanged total cause.
- IX.3, printed 393–401, complete, on actions of higher principles and why lower effects cannot be their primary ends.
- IX.6, printed 414–422, complete, on providence and the entry of evil.
- IX.4, printed 402–403, **opening argument only**, through its account of knowing, accepted, intellectually productive emanation and before the detailed one-from-one derivation. No claim to reading the rest of that chapter.

Electronic text was read continuously, with apparatus preserved in source HTML but omitted from separately labelled working main-text extractions. Core claims were then checked visually against printed 363, 366–367, 395–398, 402–403, 415, and 420–421. The print check also verified the VIII.7 heading at 362. This is neither whole-work reading nor complete apparatus collation.

A directly hosted **Najāt** electronic text was read at its complete unity section, displayed pagination 205–206, with adjoining complete knowledge sections at 202–204. Its argument substantially reproduces the relevant Ilāhiyyāt wording. This confirms the quotation identified in Ṣafadiyya without relying on Ibn Taymiyya as its only carrier. Najāt is corroboration with that limitation, not a second independently established philosophical argument.

Chapter numbering must be edition-specific. Here providence is **IX.6**, the higher-principles argument is **IX.3**, and the emanated hierarchy begins **IX.4**. The Ḥasan-zāda Āmulī table of contents encountered during discovery splits chapters differently. Its numbering was not imported into this edition.

## 1 What Avicenna positively attributes

At VIII.7, 363, the analogy begins with an architect's conceived form: sometimes cognition derives from a pre-existing thing; sometimes the known form precedes and helps produce the thing. The First knows its own reality, what follows from it, how good is ordered in the whole, and that this knowing is productive of that ordered existence. The passage expressly rejects modelling this simply on light following a luminous body or heat following a hot body. The rejection is immediately followed by knowing the order **as good** and knowing its production from the First.

The same paragraph identifies the First's self-love as love of itself as source of good order; the order is loved derivatively (*bi-l-ʿaraḍ*). The First neither suffers an effect from the wanted object nor longs to acquire it. Avicenna calls this its will free of deficiency. The denial of need belongs inside a positive account of willing.

At 366–368, the account becomes more exact. The object is known as arising from its source, good, non-opposed, and following the goodness and perfection loved in the source itself. Such an object is called willed. The First is described as willing through a purely intellectual will; its knowing is also the source of its activity. At 367, will is said not to differ from knowledge either in reality or in concept. At 368, *willer* means the Necessary Existent, intellectually apprehending itself as the principle of the whole good order.

IX.4, 402–403, reinforces the positive point. Production by nature in the sense of occurring without knowledge or acceptance is explicitly denied. The First knows the whole's proceeding from it, contains no resistance or aversion to that procession, and knows it as following its beloved perfection. Its primary act is self-intellection as principle of the best fitting order; the intelligible actuality is knowledge, power, and will. The resulting existence is expressly **distinct from the First's essence**. Attribute identity does not authorize identifying God with the external world or identifying cognition-token with external product.

Primary locators: [VIII.7](https://lib.rafed.net/view.php?b_id=1128&page=362&type=c_fbook), [IX.4 opening](https://lib.rafed.net/view.php?b_id=1128&page=402&type=c_fbook).

## 2 Why the claimed identity is more than absence of bodily machinery

Avicenna does not argue that human cognition as such produces its object. VIII.7, 366–367, openly acknowledges that a human design is insufficient: a new desire-linked intention, motive power, organs, instruments, and matter are involved. If the intelligible form were itself a fully actual productive principle, he says, it would itself be power. The First's pure actuality does not require the added human sequence.

This supplies a historically articulated contrast in productive completeness. It is stronger than appending awareness to an independently operating mechanism. But the argument also relies on the disputed substantive claim that the First's evaluative intellection already is fully actual productive efficacy. Removing nerves, instruments, acquired desire, and unrealized potency does not by itself prove that a conceptually distinguishable non-needy practical orientation is impossible.

VIII.7's wider context matters. Pages 364–366 address how multiplicity of intelligibles relates to the simple First, canvass problems with treating forms as constituents or effects of further cognitions, and direct the reader to preserve noncomposition. IX.4, 402, adds that a human-style purpose would introduce a reason for intending, an intention, and a resulting benefit. These are part of the argument for identity, not evidence that the source has forgotten what its effects are. Conversely, claims of simplicity cannot be made to prove their own contested interpretation by fiat.

**Interpretive finding:** this is a proposed account of practical determination within intellect, not a neutral cognition plus an explicitly denied practical relation. Its theory may fail to provide everything required by the stronger practical-perfection account. That failure would have to be established, not read off from its identity thesis.

## 3 The strongest reason against primary intention of the effect

IX.3 is the decisive adjoining argument. Its immediate subject includes heavenly causes, so not every statement about heavenly souls should be transferred to the First. But the chapter explicitly reasons from the First as the exemplar and states its general higher-cause rationale.

An interlocutor proposes an altruistic analogy: a good person travelling for their own purpose chooses an equally serviceable route that also benefits another. Likewise, heavenly motion might pursue a higher end while selecting its direction for the good of lower beings. Avicenna first says that if aiming at lower benefit is possible in direction, the same rationale would permit it in motion itself. The proposed restriction does not solve the underlying problem.

At 395–397, he gives the core argument:

1. A nonfutile rational purpose makes the object's proceeding from its agent better **for the agent** than its not proceeding.
2. Whatever is genuinely better for that agent confers a perfection otherwise absent. Praise, displayed power, reputation, profit, safety, and happiness illustrate the analysis.
3. A lower effect, already receiving its perfection from the cause, cannot confer a perfection on that cause.
4. Goodness cannot require an added intention of that kind: either the goodness is complete without it, or the intention completes that goodness and is therefore a condition of its completion.

He accordingly permits benefit as a derivative consequence of a primary upward-looking object, while refusing to make the lower benefit itself the primary end. At 397–399, the First's perfection is not enhanced by the existence of what proceeds from it. The chapter uses analogies of necessary concomitants, then explicitly marks the higher case as knowing both its consequences and the way goodness is ordered in them. Thus it would be selective to quote only VIII.7's rejection of the light/heat model or only IX.3's concomitant analogies: the full view combines necessary consequence with intellectual knowledge and acceptance.

Page 398 supplies an important qualification: celestial motion is itself the relevant perfection, preserving a type through successive individual configurations where no one configuration can remain. It is not a journey that would cease after obtaining some external perfection. Avicenna therefore knows the distinction between activity constituting perfection and activity acquiring an external benefit. This passage concerns a derivative celestial bearer; it does not grant intrinsic succession in the First. His exclusion of that stronger case depends on the First's nonpotency, noncomposition, and necessity in every respect, not on ignorance of activity-as-perfection. A reply must address why those premises allegedly prohibit original successive activity rather than refute a position he never held.

**The point that needs answering:** step 1 moves from an intelligible reason to produce a good into a claim about a perfection acquired by the producer. A non-needy agency account can agree that arbitrary indifference and external completion are defective while denying that intrinsically valuing a created good must make its producer a beneficiary perfected by that good. The act may express an already perfect agent's wisdom, with its benefit terminating in the creature. This is the proper comparative challenge; merely asserting such an expression is possible is not yet a proof, but Avicenna's acquisition premise is likewise not supplied by the word *purpose* alone.

This distinction also prevents an overcorrection. Avicenna does not teach lack of all positive regard for produced good. He teaches a determinate order apprehended and accepted as good, loved derivatively through the self, while denying its being an independent primary final cause of the First's activity.

Primary locator: [IX.3](https://lib.rafed.net/view.php?b_id=1128&page=393&type=c_fbook).

## 4 Foreknowledge is not intrinsic love of every consequence

IX.6, 415, defines providence through the First's intrinsic knowledge of the order of good, intrinsic causality of good and perfection as far as possible, and acceptance of that order. It appeals to ordered natural structures as evidence against sheer chance, then explains how the envisaged order proceeds.

The whole chapter, not only the definition, is needed to determine the content of acceptance. It distinguishes privation, damaging causes, relative harms, and goods requiring material natures. Fire's general useful capacities can carry incidental destructive consequences. At 418–421, suppressing the whole class of goods to avoid those harms would allegedly sacrifice a greater good; demanding that the same natural kind retain its characteristic action while never having the relevant harmful interaction is rejected as asking for a different kind.

At 420–421, goods are explicitly said to be primarily willed, while evil is willed accidentally as a foreknown necessary accompaniment. This is fully compatible with VIII.7's saying that the created order is loved derivatively relative to the First's primary self-love. The comparisons differ: good versus its incidental evil, and the created order versus the source itself. They should not be collapsed.

Therefore neither complete foreknowledge nor inclusion within providence makes each outcome intrinsically loved for its own sake. The detailed claims about the quantity of evil, material necessities, and the best realizable order are Avicenna's arguments here; their truth was not independently established by this reading.

Primary locator: [IX.6](https://lib.rafed.net/view.php?b_id=1128&page=414&type=c_fbook).

## 5 The modal and temporal difference

IX.1, 373–381, denies that an unchanged fully sufficient cause can first acquire an effect without a relevant new determinant. Avicenna expressly includes will, intention, knowledge, natural disposition, an instrument, recipient readiness, and a suitable time among possible alleged new determinants; giving a change the name *will* does not exempt it from explanation. The First is taken to be necessary in every respect, so a newly acquired intrinsic state is excluded. The chapter argues for beginningless motion dependent on its cause, not for an original idle period followed by an unexplained start.

This does **not** make necessity equivalent to involuntariness. In the account just read, the necessity runs through the First's own knowing and intellectual willing. What distinguishes it from the stronger agency account is the conjunction of that causation with the exclusion of successive intrinsic determinations and the proposed identity of knowing, willing, and power.

There is also a necessary modal caution. VIII.7, 366, explicitly allows the First considered with a particular causal relation, such as causing Zayd, to be possible in that relational respect while necessary in itself. The works distinguish the necessity of the First from the dependent status of its effects. This bounded reading should not be turned into an independently proved formal theorem that every truth or the whole history is necessary in precisely one modern modal sense. Nor does allowing possibility considered intrinsically establish alternatives under the identical fully specified productive actuality.

Primary locators: [IX.1](https://lib.rafed.net/view.php?b_id=1128&page=373&type=c_fbook), [VIII.7 relation discussion](https://lib.rafed.net/view.php?b_id=1128&page=366&type=c_fbook).

## 6 Particular knowledge is part of the rival's own claim

VIII.6, 355–362, does not simply admit that the First misses particulars. It expressly claims that no individual thing escapes its knowledge. The proposed mode is unchanging knowledge of the complete causal order, including particular configurations and their temporal relations, rather than new indexical judgements acquired as events occur. The eclipse illustration distinguishes knowing which eclipse occurs, with its specified causal and temporal relations, from acquiring a newly present-tense perceptual judgement.

This is a claim requiring assessment, not a concession of ignorance. A critique may challenge the sufficiency of that account for exact particulars, indexical knowledge, or successive original action; it must target the actual proposed mode. Adding exact particular knowledge to a hypothetical rival is not automatically a charitable innovation over an Avicenna who supposedly renounced it.

Primary locator: [VIII.6](https://lib.rafed.net/view.php?b_id=1128&page=355&type=c_fbook).

## 7 Disposition of the adversarial test

Three claims must remain separate:

- **Every original perfect source knows and willingly produces:** Avicenna's text supplies no straightforward counterexample. It positively describes such intellectual willing and rules out ignorant natural outflow.
- **Knowledge and will must have irreducibly different practical roles or concepts:** Avicenna is a real rival. He says the divine reality and concept coincide, while relying on evaluative content, acceptance, and full productive actuality to explain what he means.
- **Wise original action permits intrinsic successive determinations and genuine primary intention of produced good without needy acquisition:** Avicenna's causal and final-causal restrictions oppose this stronger account. The opposition has arguments, especially the purpose-to-acquired-perfection premise and necessity-in-every-respect premise. Those are the premises the affirmative source account must answer.

The positive appraisal of informed, effective, intrinsically wise agency is not displaced by the historical availability of this package. The refined result is that Avicenna must be assessed as a competing analysis of divine willing with restrictive final-causal and temporal commitments. Calling him an unqualified knowing-but-nonvoluntary original would conceal his own affirmative agency language and assign the rival counterexample credit it has not earned.
