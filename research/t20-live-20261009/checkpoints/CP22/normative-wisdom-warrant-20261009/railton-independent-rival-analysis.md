# Independent naturalist rival: Peter Railton

## Result

## Source capsule (135 words)

Peter Railton, “Moral Realism,” *The Philosophical Review* 95(2), 1986, 163–207, proposes naturalist realism with objective, relational truth conditions. An individual's good depends on natural facts underwriting fully informed self-advice; moral rightness concerns impartial consideration of everyone affected. He offers causal feedback and explanatory usefulness as support, rather than deducing authoritative norms from actual preferences. His account expressly permits moral obligations without categorical reasons for every rational agent. Actual desire, justified appraisal, and motivation therefore separate. The proposals are reforming definitions, answerable to evaluative adequacy and empirical explanatory success, not proofs against skepticism. Their unresolved demands include idealization, interpersonal comparison, and reliable social learning. The paper directly addresses objectivity without a cosmic foundation, while candidly declining universal desire-independent practical authority. It is stronger than merely observing moral sentiments and weaker than proving naturalism supplies categorical reasons.

## Own analysis

### 1. Fix the disputed proposition before scoring the rival

At least five claims must be kept separate:

1. A judgment has a truth value.
2. Its truth does not depend on the current evaluator approving it.
3. Its truth can be warranted rather than merely believed.
4. It gives any agent a reason regardless of that agent's contingent ends.
5. The reason actually motivates, or overrides every competing consideration.

No implication from (1) or (2) to (4) or (5) is automatic. Conversely, failure of motivation does not show absence of a reason; failure to establish a universal reason does not show that an assessment is false. A comparison that calls all five “objective normativity” cannot discriminate the theories fairly.

The strongest available objection to Ameri's reported transition is therefore a missing exclusion argument: why must every nonconventional truth about harm, justice, or proper treatment require a legislating mind? Being independent of present approval is not equivalent to being independent of every fact about living beings. A relation can be objectively instantiated even though its relata include persons and their capacities. Nor does lack of a physical shape establish a separate immaterial legislator. Those conclusions require additional premises about normativity, explanation, and agency.

The strongest available objection to the naturalist is a missing adequacy argument: why is the proposed natural relation the rightness at issue, rather than one practically useful ranking among others? Showing that a criterion measures something real does not yet establish the authority disputed by the theist. Calling it a definition makes the proposal intelligible; it does not force an opponent to accept its preservation of moral content.

### 2. Desire and reasons: the precise unpaid bridge

A person may have a very strong motivation for an act that is unjustified. A reason can also exist while the person is inattentive, weak-willed, or unmotivated. We should not give the naturalist credit for the missing justification merely because an idealization produces a desire, and should not give the theist victory merely because a vicious agent remains unmoved.

The difficult test is a clear-eyed agent who understands the suffering of others but has no relevant concern for them. Ask separately whether the act is bad, whether that agent has any reason to avoid it, and whether the agent must give that reason priority. If the target is categorical authority, idealizing information alone is insufficient unless an argument shows why rationally adequate ends must change. If the target is objective appraisal, the agent's indifference by itself does not defeat the appraisal.

A parallel problem remains for a legislator account: the existence, power, or creative role of a commander does not without argument yield an obligation to obey. A threat can produce prudential motivation without constituting moral authority. A grounding in perfect goodness may improve the explanation, but the content of goodness and the authority of that grounding must still be made explicit. This is a symmetry of questions, not an assertion that the two accounts have equal answers or equal plausibility.

### 3. What survives for an evil objection?

There are two different routes, requiring different evidence.

**Victim-centered evaluation:** severe suffering can frustrate a being's capacities, projects, and conditions of flourishing regardless of whether its inflictor cares. The further conclusion that infliction is morally wrong requires a defensible bridge from that loss to moral assessment. The even further conclusion that every conceivable rational agent has a categorical reason to prevent it is an additional burden. Rejecting the last claim does not by itself cancel the first two.

**Internal assessment of theism:** temporarily assume the proposed creator is perfectly good, knowledgeable, and powerful. Ask what that asserted goodness commits the hypothesis to concerning suffering. A challenge can use the hypothesis's own benevolent commitments without first proving a freestanding categorical ethic binding every possible rational agent. This does not establish atheism: the challenge still needs a justified assessment of the relevant suffering, its relationship to the asserted goodness, and whether sufficient justifying goods or constraints are absent or evidentially unlikely.

Do not automatically extend a human-naturalist criterion to God. Equally, do not answer a conditional criticism of perfect benevolence merely by demanding the critic first supply an external cosmic law. The relevant inference may concern the content and expected manifestations of the claimed divine attribute. That is also why “evil needs God” can be question-begging if it assumes the very necessity conditional under examination.

### 4. Epistemic access is not genealogy

A causal origin of belief becomes evidentially useful only when the mechanism is appropriately sensitive to its proposed truth conditions. Survival, social stability, confidence, consensus, and strength of feeling are not interchangeable with accuracy. A naturalist may propose an account of correction under better information, but must show where it tracks the criterion, where it fails, and why the criterion itself is normatively adequate.

An especially discriminating case is the powerless sufferer: an oppressed, isolated, future, or nonhuman being whose interests cannot generate effective bargaining pressure. A successful moral epistemology must explain how its correction methods can recognize that being's significance without treating social influence as the measure of worth. This is a conceptual reliability test, not a request for a new empirical campaign.

The same distinction applies to a theistic appeal to an innate moral disposition: its naturalness, universality, or felt certainty would not alone establish truth or identify its theological ground. The grounding argument and the access argument need separate warrants.

### 5. Remaining naturalist debts and bounded verdict

The decisive unresolved questions are whether idealized self-advice is determinate without importing the desired moral answer; whether information changes the person whose welfare is being assessed; whether interpersonal comparison is sufficiently stable; why equal consideration rather than domination or selective concern supplies the moral criterion; and whether explanatory success warrants the normative identification rather than only a neighboring descriptive theory. Answering the authority question may require a stronger account of reasons than this rival attempts.

These debts make the rival defeasible. They do not entail its inconsistency, and they do not identify God as the unique remaining explanation. Conversely, the existence of a sophisticated rival does not establish that its proposed identity is true. The warranted result is a narrower necessity-claim test: Ameri must exclude objective, relational, naturalist moral assessment, or show why the evil objection indispensably presupposes the stronger authority that this particular naturalist does not provide.
