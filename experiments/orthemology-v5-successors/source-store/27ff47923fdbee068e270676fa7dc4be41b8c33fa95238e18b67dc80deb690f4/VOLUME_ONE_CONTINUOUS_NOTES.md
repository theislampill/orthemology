## Opening and programme 001 lines 16–108

The invocation already puts the central affirmative thesis strongly: genuine monotheism affirms the realities of divine names and perfections in the exclusive manner due to God; denying them imports defects, while assimilating God to servants also fails. This is an affirmative-explanatory conception of perfection, not merely refusal of contradiction. The introduction treats recognition of God's names, attributes and actions as the foundation of worship, moral direction and final happiness. Its threefold programme is knowledge of the object of worship, the revealed path to him, and the destiny of travellers. Detailed salvific knowledge is claimed to exceed unaided human reason; prophecy is thus needed more urgently than sensory goods. That is the author's theological premise, not a conclusion licensed simply by the existence of a necessary being.

An important dependence example is the reading of al-Shāfiʿī's praise: thanking for a gift itself requires a new gift, so gratitude never makes the servant independent. The text treats this as support for both attributes and providence. The application is to the servant's act of thanks, not automatically to every conceivable bearer of a causal role. The grammatical alternatives in Qurʾān 12:108 let the followers share both insight and the work of calling; this is one of the opening's language arguments, not expendable decoration.

The main prolegomenal inference is communicative. If revelation and the messenger perfectly guide in the most important matter, and teach even minor practical details, they cannot deliberately leave divine knowledge misleading in its usual meanings while requiring speculative specialists to supply the opposite. The author's historical corroboration says the first generations were intensely concerned with knowing God, understood the meanings, and were neither simple repeaters of unintelligible words nor deficient relative to later speculative theologians. The passage beginning “our shaykh” at line 58 and again at 78 attributes the attack on supposed superiority of later thinkers to Ibn Taymiyya. The reported verses, deathbed confessions and distressed philosopher's prayer are reported rhetorical corroboration, not independently authenticated historical evidence in this reading.

The 24-chapter programme concerns interpretation, language, intention, doctrinal and legal disagreement, and four large epistemological theses. It expressly allows correct interpretation, so its target is not every act called taʾwīl. The four contested theses are the alleged inability of verbal evidence to yield knowledge, figurative reduction of attribute texts, non-certainty of sound reports, and giving reason priority when opposed to revelation. The ordering in this opening must be retained even if subsequent physical surviving sections do not cover all four.

## Chapters 1 and 2 001 lines 109–192

Three senses of taʾwīl are distinguished. First is the extra-mental actuality or fulfilment to which a report, promise or command points. Second is explanation of meaning as in early tafsīr. Third is the later technical redirection from apparent/real meaning to a non-apparent figurative one. The first two are admitted; this already blocks reading the book as flat opposition to interpretation. Khidr's explanation supplies a further teleological example: the action's hidden intended end is its taʾwīl. The distinction between external fulfilment and mental understanding must be preserved when later propositions concern divine attributes.

The ʿAbdallāh b. Rawāḥa/ʿAmmār verses are discussed as an attribution and chronology problem. Ibn Hishām's objection is answered conditionally, not by assuming the attribution settled. The interpretation that those who brought ʿAmmār into battle “killed” him is rejected by parity with bringing Ḥamza to battle. The discussion of ʿĀʾisha and ʿUthmān completing travel prayer canvasses multiple legal explanations, rejects their strength, and nevertheless treats sincere reasoning from a perceived proof as a recognisable form of interpretation. These political/legal examples matter to the book's internal standards.

Ten defects of false interpretation are then identified: lexical impossibility; inflection such as dual hand forms; incompatibility with syntax/context and contrastive lists; importing later technical senses; transferring a sense that is valid in one construction to an incompatible construction; neglecting settled usage and reinforcing contextual markers; defeating a text's operative purpose; imposing an esoteric meaning on common language; lowering a supreme predicate into a trivial comparative one; and lacking contextual indications for deviation from the apparent meaning. Examples range across hands, vision, speech, creation of the throne, marriage guardianship, and the meaning of “one.” The marriage example is a cumulative argument from generality, syntax, apt rationale and repeated invalidation, not just lexical literalism.

The important positive case at lines 184–190 rejects reducing divine “above” to merely greater worth than humans or mastery of the throne: absolute highness is treated as a distinctive lordly perfection, and domination of one's own throne is too trivial to explain repeated solemn praise. The argument thus appeals to the degree and kind of perfection fitting the speaker's communicative aim. Its validity still depends on identifying the appropriate literal sense and on the metaphysics of highness; it is not a free-standing deduction of unshared sourcehood.

## Chapters 3 and 4 001 lines 193–240

Interpretation is truth-evaluable reporting of another speaker's intention, not creation of a meaning by the interpreter. “Carrying” an expression can mean a lexical report, a report of what a speaker intended, or a newly stipulated usage. Only the first two can establish what someone else's speech means. A defender says the literal sense is impossible and the figurative must therefore be intended; Ibn al-Qayyim answers that this is still a report needing truth, and that a perfectly guiding speaker would signal the intended redirection. He expressly admits that ambiguity or indirect speech may be intended where lawful concealment is the purpose. Hence the argument's scope is guiding communication, not every utterance by every speaker.

Commands require sufficiently knowable interpretation if obedience is required. This grounds the existence of knowledgeable interpreters rather than universal equal comprehension. Legal disagreement can be excused by lack of access, forgetfulness, counterevidence or genuine ambiguity; sometimes both readings are true, sometimes only one of incompatible readings. Cases include guardianship, touching women, ritual impurity, relatives' entitlement, Qurʾānic listening, the widow's waiting period, dead sea animals, and inheritance. The author contrasts that disagreement with his asserted consensus on divine attributes. The consensus assertion is retained as his evidentiary claim, not independently established here.

The text differentiates understanding an attribute's basic meaning from knowing its essence or modality. Ambiguity is often relational to a reader rather than an intrinsic permanent absence of meaning. “Mutashābih” can mean mutually consistent/resembling throughout scripture or a locally ambiguous passage; neither sense establishes that all attribute texts are inscrutable. This distinction is crucial before using the book to equate positive predicates, known external realities, and fully specified modes of bearing them.

## Chapters 5 through 8 001 lines 241–449

The distinction between explanation and distortion is restated, with changes to vowel case, implicit subjects, and figurative reinterpretation treated separately. The text's accusations against Jews and rival Islamic schools are polemical claims in its historical setting, not neutral descriptions to endorse. Its own express conclusion is that real knowledge is correct taʾwīl, and the learned are interpreters; it rejects distortion, not explanation.

Chapter 6's principal argument is parity. If real knowledge, will, life and power can be predicated without their creaturely modes, why cannot mercy, love, anger, rejoicing, face and hands? Several opponent replies receive individual answers: rationally proven versus merely reported predicates; consensus on seven predicates; and supposedly divisible limbs versus indivisible qualities. The reply to the first distinguishes reason's silence from reason's negation and points to kindness, selection and punishment as intelligible signs of mercy, love and anger. The reply to the last says separation from the divine bearer is impossible in both classes, and not every distinction of meanings constitutes impermissible composition. The argument here is not that all attributes are identical, nor that an attribute is an independent bearer. Lines 285–289 preserve this scope explicitly.

The catalogue of rival methods includes a criticism of Jabriyya who allow no efficacy whatever to the servant's power and liken human movement to wind or trees (299). This is a real anti-occasionalist/anti-compulsion control within a wider anti-selective-exegesis argument. Chapter 7 says relocating a predicate to a different positive attribute repeats the very analogy problem invoked to remove it. Some intelligible continuity with observed meanings is required to think of the unseen at all; sameness of general intelligible meaning is not asserted to be identity of mode or essence.

Chapter 8 rejects the opponent's grotesque composite picture of multiple eyes and hands on one flank. It argues from actual syntax, plural possessors, singulars with generic force, and the absence of an exhaustive-count implication. The “side of God” is specifically not adopted as a bodily predicate: regret concerns neglect of God's right and obedience, and a servant's act/omission is separate from the divine essence. The “shin” verse is exceptional: the text acknowledges a dispute among early authorities whether the verse refers to severity or a divine predicate, and says the attribute's proponents rely on a separately transmitted hadith. This qualifies the broad earlier no-disagreement claim and prevents turning every anatomically sounding expression into a divine attribute by a mechanical rule.

The treatment of hands differentiates “our hands made” from “I created with my two hands” by the subject, preposition and dual form. The latter is presented as a special honour of Adam, so reduction to generic power would erase the special predication. The author accumulates reports concerning creation, giving, justice, predestination and the resurrection as corroboration. They are a cumulative transmitted case; this pass does not authenticate every report. The rhetorical comparisons of faulty anthropomorphic affirmation with total denial explicitly claim affirmation may err less grievously; they do not themselves license construing the author as endorsing the imagined body.

## Chapters 9 through 14 001 lines 450–608

The interpreter owes four things: linguistic possibility in this construction, evidence selecting that non-apparent sense, a sufficient defeater of the apparent sense, and answers to evidence favouring the apparent sense. The text adds that an alleged meaning must fit the speaker's known perfection and stated aim, so not every usage found in poetry establishes divine intention. Its general positive proof invokes knowledge, communication and sincere guidance; its special proof invokes the rational fitness of perfections and a claimed unavoidable distinction between separately subsisting external realities. The latter argues that something neither in nor outside the world cannot be a distinct self-subsisting real; that spatial/disjunctive premise is asserted as self-evident and must be evaluated, not silently replaced with the weaker proposition that an external cause exists.

Chapter 10's long polemic says destructive reinterpretation combines a bad initial resemblance, denial, misrepresentation of the speaker, and violation of textual constraints. It extends the authority problem to political government, legal analogy, ascetic experience and charismatic religious leaders. The criterion is claimed revelation judging these other authorities, not their identity or prestige. The language is sometimes a moral denunciation rather than a new demonstrative step. Chapter 11's communication model has two necessary conditions: the speaker supplies an intelligible indication and the hearer is able to understand it. If a message habitually signifies the opposite of its guiding intention without correction, leaving the hearer without it could be better. The named legal examples are Khaybar's sharecropping interpreted as tribute and minimum breastfeeding interpreted as mere latching; both are rejected through historical/linguistic constraints.

Chapter 12 is a major argumentative locus. A reported Ibn Taymiyya debate partitions the defender's options by speaker's knowledge, power to express the truth, and sincere intention to explain it. Perfect guidance plus stable misleading presentation produces contradiction. The opponent asks to descend to the level of theoretical debate; the author takes this as avoidance. Ibn al-Qayyim then reports his own conversation with a scriptural scholar (549–557): if Muḥammad falsely claimed divine authorisation while taking power, changing laws and harming God's allies, God's sustained effective support would entail ignorance, inability, or foolish/unjust governance. The interlocutor grants prophetic truth but says followers of earlier prophets are happy too; the reply says accepting this prophet while rejecting the scope of his message is inconsistent. This is an explicit morally grounded attestation argument. Its force requires both the moral principle and the factual description of the sign/support, including the claimed trajectory of success; it cannot be certified by a conditional theorem alone. Its general significance is greater than a mere appeal to the power of any successful ruler, since the described public claim, alleged divine response, and intended guidance matter.

Chapter 13 treats ease of scripture as ease of memorisation, comprehension and obedience. Its ensuing survey of metaphysical disputes covers essence/existence, attributes and modes, universals/particulars in knowledge, unity and differentiation of willing, inner speech, sensory cognition, atomism, persistence of accidents, time/place, acquisition, and the relation of act to agent. These are not all proven false by being enumerated; they illustrate the author's criticism of complex speculative premises displacing clear guidance. In particular the act-agent issue is explicitly whether an act inheres in its agent, and how its temporal relation is explained, not merely whether a product is caused.

Chapter 14 supplies a social anthropology of language: human life needs cooperation, communication of intentions needs accessible signs, and spoken movements of the tongue offer economical expressive differentiation. It contests a poetic line used to locate speech entirely in the heart, reporting an alternative “expression” wording and its source. The larger argument is functional: systematic assignment of opposite, unmarked meanings would defeat language's interpersonal purpose. A deliberately lying tongue is said to add harm beyond loss of use, unlike simple muteness. This is a teleological account of communication, not proof that language admits no figurative or context-dependent uses.

## Chapter 15 first part 001 lines 609–650

The author describes religious schism, coercion and doctrinal fragmentation as effects of uncontrolled reinterpretation; the account also identifies persuasive mechanisms: prestige, ascetic appearance, eloquence, insider status, and attacks on rivals. Claims about origins of Jewish and Christian practices, Paul, councils and sects are attributed polemical history, not newly established facts. The strongest internal challenge is parity: if very abundant attribute reports may be reinterpreted without denying the messenger, why cannot bodily resurrection and then practical obligations be reinterpreted too? It presses proponents to supply a non-arbitrary distinction, rather than proving every proposed difference impossible by rhetoric alone. The positive accessibility principle is that the greater the human need, the more readily wisdom supplies means to meet it, illustrated by air, water and food; divine knowledge is ranked above them. Its role is normative/teleological support for accessible revelation, not a bare law derived from necessary existence.

## Chapter 15 remainder and chapter 16 001 lines 651–731

Iblīs's reasoning is reconstructed as an enthymeme: a superior should not bow to an inferior, and fire is supposedly superior to clay. The text treats the first unstated premise as a false standard controlling a command. Adam's mistake receives several rival explanations, which the author rejects individually: restriction to one tree token, merely advisory prohibition, and a prohibition of joint rather than separate eating. The preferred explanation is reliance on the tempter's emphatic oath plus a mistaken comparison of the promised abiding good with a reparable disobedience. This is a substantive account of motivation, trust and wishful practical reasoning, not simply “Adam interpreted.” The historical catalogue includes Khālid's action at Banū Jadhīma, withholding alms, civil wars, persecution of jurists and Ibn Taymiyya; its casualties and causes remain source reports, not verified history.

Chapter 16 distinguishes unambiguous statements, defeasibly apparent statements, and statements requiring explanation. Stable usage across contexts can make an initially defeasible indication effectively unambiguous. Explanation may occur elsewhere, so the doctrine is not atomistic isolation of individual words. A series of examples shows scripture correcting likely misreadings: capacity limits, the commander's responsibility and exhortation, descendants' elevation without reducing parents' reward, proper speech, fasting's horizon, human willing dependent on divine willing, wisdom preventing arbitrary willing, and promise-keeping. The passage states that God may reward without a recipient's earned deed but does not punish without the recipient's earning (715–716). Human will is affirmed, its independence denied, and divine will is tied to wisdom and knowledge (719–724). These are distinct affirmative controls, not interchangeable modalities.

The inherited electronic marker “end of part one” occurs at 001:731. It is a genuine source-text marker, not a web-subsection boundary; the final boundary review establishes this as an inherited earlier part break at printed 183/184, not the new physical-volume 604/605 seam. The continuous display covers 001:16–731 without gaps. It does not thereby cover every modern preliminary or apparatus page of any print volume.

## Chapter 17 and chapter 18 001 lines 732–819

The argument against destructive interpretation is extended to medicine, mathematics, grammar and ordinary coordination. These practices require stable recoverability of an author's intent even where their authors are fallible. The author distinguishes propositional monotheism (perfections and creator-creature distinction) from practical monotheism (exclusive worship, love, fear, trust and reliance), claiming they support one another. A long Ibn Rushd quotation (748–776) is retained as quoted argument, not converted into Ibn al-Qayyim's unqualified own metaphysics. It uses a premodern cosmology of enclosing surfaces to distinguish direction from bodily place, argues for an extra-cosmic nonbody, and partitions people into untroubled public, genuinely knowing experts, and intermediate dialectical doubters. Its medicine analogy portrays successive reinterpretations as replacing ingredients in a carefully composed remedy. The author explicitly omits further material about al-Ghazālī, so this is a selective authorial quotation already within the original.

The five interpretations of attribute language are set out with their differences intact: reinterpretation of literal content; salutary imaginative depiction with no corresponding literal reality; ignorance of all meaning; creaturely assimilation; and the author's affirmation of actual predicates without creaturely equivalence. The imagination theory's prohibition on disclosing reinterpretation to the public is not the same as the interpreter's aim to discover an intended hidden sense. The fifth way says knowing a meaning does not require knowing its full nature or how it is instantiated. Soul and otherworldly food are analogies for this epistemic distinction. The “highest likeness” is then the truthful conception in believers' minds, combining intrinsic independence, agency, knowledge, speech and response; illustrations by vastly accumulating all creaturely strength, knowledge, wisdom or beauty stress that even the imagined sum does not approximate the divine measure. This is far stronger than mere membership in a maximally large comparative class, but its exclusivity is part of the argued divine conception rather than already licensed by a minimalist necessary-being premise.

## Chapter 19 and chapter 20 001 lines 820–1040

Six causes of credulous acceptance are examined: ornamental wording; repellent relabelling of the rival position; attribution to prestigious persons or the Prophet's family; misplaced transfer of expertise from mathematics, medicine or engineering to theology; the pleasure of novelty and exclusive insight; and preparatory claims discrediting textual knowledge. Scientific competence in a specialised field is explicitly acknowledged and can greatly exceed that of religious scholars; the inference to equal authority in theology is rejected. The text lists mechanics, water management, agriculture, medicine and prosody. This domain-specific-expertise argument matters independently of the book's polemical target. Its use must also be applied reciprocally rather than granting its author universal competence.

Chapter 20 first presses symmetry between sects' rival reinterpretations; importantly, it pairs texts that human actions are God's creations with texts that they genuinely belong to servants and occur by their power and will (867). It next claims reason and sound revelation agree, diagnosing apparent conflict by defective reasoning, unauthentic transmission, wrong interpretation, or confusion of reason's inability to discover with its proof of impossibility. This last distinction is not optional.

The long positive scriptural argument sequence has been read in full. Qurʾān 34:22–23 excludes independent ownership, partnership, help and unpermitted intercession; intrinsic needlessness is opposed to creaturely need. Qurʾān 17:42 is read in relation to acknowledged dependent alleged gods seeking their lord, not simply as a conflict-of-two-independent-causes proof. Qurʾān 23:91 invokes real creative-beneficial agency in an ilāh, refusal of equal partnership, separation of products or supremacy/subjection; order is evidence of one governor. Source 001:904 reads “worship and divinity” where the separately known 2020 p 232 reads “end and divinity”; this discrepancy remains open for exact scan collation. Qurʾān 13:16 explicitly argues complete overcoming entails unity because sharing contradicts complete overcoming. These are the full positive claims. The step from maximal source-role predicates to one bearer must be assessed under the author's unshareable supremacy and non-partnership premises, not weakened to a mere ability to act alone and not assumed true by definition.

Further proofs concern failure to create even a fly, the Qurʾānic challenge, character and content as mutually reinforcing evidence of prophetic truth, resurrection by first-creation parity, higher-to-lower capacity, conversion of opposite material qualities, and effortless willing rather than needy instrumental production (938–965). The resurrection argument distinguishes ability from wise purpose: ordered human creation is also said to make ultimate abandonment without command and recompense unfitting. Premodern physics of heat, moisture and material elements is part of its argument and not modern scientific evidence. The criticisms of incarnation invoke food-dependence and bodily waste; the material also contains historical gender hierarchy claims. These are the author's claims, not present-day judgments endorsed by these notes.

Abraham's exception regarding divine willing is read as epistemic humility about God's unknown purposes, not fear of independently powerful idols. His challenge concerning the sun is treated as requiring consistency from a claimant who equivocates over giving life, rather than abandoning the first proof. Creation is tied to knowledge of particulars, and the “created without a creator / self-created” division supports a creator. The final gratitude argument (1016–1024) says originating a person is the basis of subsequent benefit, so rational moral perception requires gratitude and worship; it explicitly rejects denial of rational moral evaluation. This is a genuine further normative bridge beyond causation alone, and deserves assessment rather than dismissal or silent inclusion in the word “source.”

## Chapters 21 through 23 001 lines 1041–1388

Misinterpretation is analysed by two speaker-side factors (inadequate explanation, bad purpose) and two listener-side factors (defective understanding, bad purpose). The desired fit is between intended and understood meaning. Lawful concealment is again admitted: proposal allusion, Abraham's “sister,” the migration narratives, euphemistic sexual language and ironic poetry are differentiated as kināya and taʿrīḍ. Thus truthful meaning, full disclosure, and preventing every false hearer-inference are not simply conflated. The limits of that allowance must be carried into any broad formulation of a no-deception principle.

The account of disagreement is nuanced after the earlier denunciations: one kind is culpable factionalism; another includes correct judgments and excusably mistaken sincere inquiry; a third is diversity among permitted alternatives. It urges accepting truth even from an enemy and rejecting error even from a friend, and says different capacities and wills make some disagreement inevitable. Examples cover inheritance, divorce, ritual practice, interest and manumission. Cooperation, deliberation and debate can remain charitable. This constrains a reductive reading of chapter 15 as saying all difference is culpable distortion.

Chapter 23 preserves two large attributed contributions: Ibn Ḥazm via al-Ḥumaydī (1112–1156), then Ibn Taymiyya beginning 1157, with Ibn al-Qayyim's intervening comments. It treats memory, locally distributed transmission, ordinary work commitments, travel, weak chains, variant narrator assessments, changing reliability, restrictive admissibility rules, vocabulary, subsumption, abrogation and apparent conflict. No single scholar is credited with knowing all revelation. Possession of collected books is specifically distinguished from mastery of their contents (1183–1185). This is directly relevant to the present study's reading-integrity rule: acquisition or parsing is not reading or exhaustive knowledge.

The legal catalogue is retained substantively: shortening prayer, wiping footwear, grandmother's inheritance, widow's residence and pregnancy, dower, burial/travel, intoxicants, divorce under mental closure, waiting periods, fraudulent temporary remarriage, disguised interest sales, unlawful partners, banishment versus imprisonment, and apparent versus legally valid prayer. It separates conflict between two proofs from conflict concerning a premise or the indication of one proof. The extended correction of claimed consensus is especially important: not knowing a dissenter is not knowing universal agreement. It surveys testimony by enslaved persons, inheritance by partially freed persons, money taken by a parent, renewed waiting periods, travel distances, oath refusal and counter-oaths, homicide compurgation, hunting/hair violations during pilgrimage, conditional divorce, exceptions from divorce counts, retreat vows, interfaith inheritance, recitation posture, moon testimony and menstruation intervals.

The long divorce discussion argues for recognised disagreement about oath-conditioned divorce, triple divorce in one utterance, and divorce during menstruation. It gives chains and school attributions, not just an unsupported assertion of minority existence. Particularly, the author says Imāmī scholars' errors elsewhere do not make all their reports lies or all their reasoning worthless (1349–1351); this is an explicit fairness constraint within a fiercely polemical book. The immediate purpose is to rebut false consensus as a reason for rejecting clear evidence, with a defence of Ibn Taymiyya's positions, rather than a freestanding practical legal advice manual. This study does not independently authenticate all attributed reports or supply present-day legal advice.

## Chapter 24 first ‘ṭāghūt’ beginning 001:1389–1569 and 002:16–32

The ten alleged preconditions for certain verbal evidence are presented before critique. The author distinguishes knowing what a speaker intended from knowing that intention's propositional content is true. The former can follow from usage, context and reliable transmission; the latter also requires the speaker's knowledge/truthfulness. The ongoing exchange here is often explicitly addressed to someone already accepting prophethood (1472), not offered as a fresh independent proof of it. This is a crucial dialectical-scope control for importing the argument into a foundational audit.

The rebuttal appeals to ordinary and specialised communication, teaching, children, animal signalling, preserved communal practices and competence in an established linguistic setting. It denies that all understanding requires consciously considering every speculative alternative. It carefully distinguishes the negation of universal certainty from the universal negation of certainty (1469), and holds that one person's uncertainty does not prove everyone else's inability to know (1495–1499). Some matters are inferential for one person and immediately known to another; expertise and acquaintance differ. This supports rejecting a global sceptical claim, while not itself validating every particular claim of certainty.

The examples include metaphysical schools' disputes about atomism, universal forms, identity of intellect/intellection, temporal agency, existence/essence and inner speech. Reported last words, poems and al-Rāzī's retrospective statements are used as a comparison with scripture's alleged clarity; they are quoted historical/polemical evidence, not independently verified accounts. Technical semantic drift is a recurring problem: body, accident, event, composition, intoxicant, neighbour and legally valid spouse shift meanings between ordinary/revealed usage and later theory. A change in term can smuggle a disputed premise. The word “composition” does not automatically name every distinguishability within one reality in ordinary Arabic, according to the source. Section 002 begins without a conceptual break at argument 29, then addresses whether each proof requires all ten preconditions, instinctive grammar versus explicit grammatical theory, and the impossibility of allowing unbounded hypothetical defeaters to dissolve all understanding.

## First major polemic completed 002 lines 33–465

The reply to linguistic scepticism now canvasses the Qurʾān's subject-matter broadly: divine predicates, created signs, first creation, resurrection, final destinies, earlier nations, admonitions, commands, the adversary's deceptions and the messenger's own remit. It differentiates widest scope, particular address and intermediate classes. Generality is purposeful; one should not infer restriction merely because a verse arose from an individual case. Early exegetes can give examples rather than exclusive definitions. The catalogue includes contentious readings of Ṭā-Hā, Yā-Sīn, Nūn, “enchanted,” aid, Pharaoh's body, sacrifice, farmers/unbelievers, the lamp-niche, and praise distributed among named Companions. The author rebukes both favourable and unfavourable narrowing when it defeats the text's general moral scope. Its chronological assertions about particular sūras and named persons remain attributed judgments and are not independently verified by this pass.

A substantive causal qualification occurs at 002:74. Threats attach to wrong acts as causes, yet repentance, compensating good, expiation, intercession or mercy can prevent punishment; this need not abolish the general causal rule. A complete cause cannot lack its effect, but a contributory cause can fail through a missing condition or an obstacle. The author resists both making divine forgiveness impossible and making all warnings epistemically empty. Here too “cause” is not used with one unqualified sense across all claims.

Ellipsis has three cases: unjustified invention that destroys communication, obvious contextually supplied material, and a potentially ambiguous case governed by the speaker's known aim and available indications. The argument affirms meaningful context and pragmatic knowledge, not exclusively explicit isolated wording. The lengthy syntax discussion distinguishes changes that obscure the proposition from changes that preserve it but change focus: agent, act, object, interrogation, denial, and insinuation. Qurʾānic examples and al-Farazdaq's difficult verse are used to show why ordinary recoverable syntax differs from arbitrary reordering. Implication in an accusation can have legal significance even where the literal words deny wrongdoing by the speaker.

The subsequent reply emphasises the act/product distinction expressly (002:216–224): human deeds are genuinely the servants' actions by choice, and are made by God, without thereby being God's own acts in the same bearer sense. It also refuses to identify creating with the created product. Species versus individual act is needed in the discussion of beginningless capacity and actual agency. This particular electronic passage contains suspect wording at 219 concerning the absence/presence of a newly arising reason; it is recorded for scan checking rather than silently corrected.

The certainty argument acknowledges probabilistic judgment in some practical cases, while insisting revelation can genuinely teach knowledge. It distinguishes an expression's stable referent from theoretical dispute over etymology or grammatical explanation. Four modes of explanation are allowed: bare expression, attached qualification, another passage, or the messenger's authorised act/utterance. Thus no universal claim that every isolated sentence is immediately certain follows. Repeated attestation and repeated exposure can turn an initially weaker judgment into settled knowledge; conversely linguistic unfamiliarity and inattention matter. The universal claim that all verbal proof depends on ten uncertain premises is the target.

002:355–362 explicitly limits a generic necessary-existence proof: an existentially necessary something has not thereby been identified as the particular knowing, choosing, beneficent God described by the messengers. The source contrasts generic syllogism with concrete signs and actual speech. That criticism must be given its full force even when the larger study has provisionally accepted necessary existence. The proof's formal shape and the positive identification of its object are different tasks. The polemic ends by distinguishing texts that simply report from texts that direct reason to a displayed argument; treating both as “merely verbal” is accused of erasing real rational content. The inherited part 2/3 marker lies at 002:465, within printed 471 of the newer layout; it is not a change of web subsection or new 2020 volume.

## Second major polemic opening 002 lines 466–594

The introductory answer promises a later clarification of muḥkam/mutashābih; the surviving-edition notice means that promise is not evidence the missing ending has been read. The second polemic takes up the alleged necessary priority of reason over transmitted revelation. Its first reply is explicitly neutral about source category: two decisive proofs cannot genuinely conflict; decisive evidence outranks probable evidence; among probable arguments weight decides. Reason is not rejected as a faculty. This passage has transcription damage at 002:482 and is queued for direct scan control.

The next distinction separates what makes something true from what lets a person know it. Revelation's reality is not caused by our knowledge of it. Nor is every rational judgment needed to recognise a prophet, so rejecting some speculative premise need not destroy the rational basis of all transmission. The text's model is a witness identifying an expert: valid identification does not give the identifier epistemic priority on every question of that expert's subject. Medicine supplies the analogy, with medical error acknowledged. These are epistemic dependence and authority arguments, not ontological derivations of prophetic content.

Some arguments assume already established truthfulness and actual communication. Given those premises, a supposedly decisive contrary conclusion shows a problem in one alleged proof, because truth cannot contradict truth. The author separately grants the case of a forged report or a misunderstood authentic one. Statements that revelation takes priority are therefore paired with the earlier certainty/probability partition and the claim that sound reason never opposes authentic correctly understood revelation. The source does not grant all historical reports automatic authenticity.

The broader exposition contrasts revelation's moral and ultimate purpose with specialist study of astronomy, elemental physics, geometry and formal logical classifications. Its attacks on philosophers' biographies or motives are polemical assertions; an argument's origin alone would not invalidate its structure. Yet the author independently presses substantive issues: non-spatial existence, accidents, agency, unity of different propositional contents and the interaction of knowledge, will and life. The roles of correct expertise and fallible identification remain distinct.

## Second polemic continuation 002 lines 595–850

The source rejects fabricated anthropomorphic stories and discusses alleged reports about other religions; these are neither accepted divine predicates nor independently verified depictions of those religions. It ranks agreement and textual reasoning, while treating unbroken early consensus as a substantive historical claim. The earlier chapter 23 warning about overclaimed consensus remains a relevant internal standard, not something to forget when the polemic makes its own claims.

The natural-science discussion acknowledges that Ptolemaic astronomy includes sound demonstrations as well as disputed assumptions and transmitted observations vulnerable to error. It is therefore more nuanced than a blanket assertion that all non-theological specialised knowledge is false. Accounts of philosophers' confusion, al-Ghazālī's final turn, Ibn Wāṣil's overnight hesitation and moral decline remain reported evidence. The recurrent argument from disagreement cannot by itself select the true party; the author supplements it with purported positive proofs and religious authority.

The analogy of light joins correct reason, nature and revelation rather than treating them as inherently hostile. The source repeatedly presents conditional arguments whose antecedent is rationally certified prophetic truth. At 002:721–731 it explicitly says someone who denies prophethood must first be addressed on its establishment. It then distinguishes whether the prophet is truthful, whether he actually uttered the content, what he intended, whether the intended content is true, whether he knew it, and whether he could communicate it. This multi-stage structure prevents jumping from an accepted necessary being to certainty about an unidentified revelatory claim.

The discussion of perception includes sensory illusion, reason's correction of it, testimony, combined channels of knowledge, and immediate versus inferential certainty. Hearing is said to have wider scope, sight stronger directness; these are different merits. Numerous miracle reports are surveyed continuously as an argument against making habit the measure of possibility. They include first creation, flood, wind, transformed staff, sea crossing, life restored, altered bodies, food and water increase, celestial signs, speaking creatures and movement of objects. This pass reads the author's case without claiming contemporary verification of those events or authenticity of every report. The comparison claims that accepting such miracles while rejecting every non-creaturely attribute is inconsistent, not that a miracle list alone logically proves every attribute.

Practical and doctrinal authority are paired: examples include usury, slaughtered/dead animals, changing prayer direction, and using decree to excuse disobedience. The scope of mission is differentiated between recipients and content. The portrait of opponents is itself qualified at 839: among inheritors of the disputed ideas are ignorant imitators, sincere but mistaken inquirers, and wilfully hostile partisans, rather than one uniform motive. Face numbering visibly jumps from 47 to 50 at 842–843; no silent renumbering is used.

## First-volume endpoint cluster 002 lines 851–1009

Face 54 presents the strongest affirmative claim encountered in this volume: complete positive perfections are rationally fitting to the world's creator and governor, and scriptural predicates identify that unique fullness. It derives knowledge from making, superior possession of life and cognition from giving them, and real responsive agency from the contrast with powerless idols. It says perfection is exclusively divine in the unrestricted sense, and insists that volitional action is a perfection rather than a defect of change. This is not just a denial of partners or a maximal-power assumption. It identifies positive worshipful and evaluative content as well as causal content. The appropriate appraisal is of the inference from given perfections and worldmaking to unrestricted completeness and unshareability, not rejection of a weakened caricature.

The passage treats devotional practice as epistemically and morally significant: divine names frame prayer and supplication, life grounds predicates, and sustaining independence gathers action under a unified conception. The ensuing classification of denial, beneficial fiction, reinterpretation and refusal to understand revisits the contrast between knowable meaning and unknowable modality. Refusal to assert an interpretation is judged less harmful than a false assertion, even while its wholesale version is rejected.

The terminology section is especially important for bearer scope. It says multiple eternal attributes are not multiple gods: one described divine reality has distinct perfections, and the object's proper name includes those predicates rather than naming bare propertyless existence. Being pleased and being angry need not be identical merely because they belong to one bearer (960–961). Criticism of “composition” distinguishes existential essence analysis, essence with attributes, hylomorphism, atomism, and assembling formerly separate pieces (971–978). The last three are not freely interchangeable with internal distinguishability. Real dependence on an external assembler cannot be inferred just by changing labels. Conversely the author does not here establish that every proposed plurality of original source bearers is merely an attribute plurality. That remains a separate classification question.

“Body,” “direction,” “change,” “purpose,” “justice” and “unity” receive explicit disambiguation. A containing place and an assembled creaturely body are denied; creator-creature distinction and voluntary agency are retained. True creaturely actions are affirmed against Jabriyya even as universal divine power and decree are defended against Qadariyya. Mediators of transmitting a divine message are allowed; independent or coercive intermediaries needed to secure divine attention are denied. This is a substantive distinction of roles, not a generic ban on all mediation.

The final faces 58–59 concern limits of a cognitive faculty. Prophetic apprehension is compared to reasoning relative to sense, with developmental stages of sense, discrimination and rational principles. Dreams, human generation, fire spreading from a small source, and silkworm metamorphosis illustrate how lack of prior familiarity cannot prove impossibility. Their premodern biological/psychological details are source premises requiring their own evaluation. The volume's last line 1009 closes the comparison of reason to revelation; the next line 1010 starts face 60 and the second-volume-equivalent body. The scan/layout control ties this seam to printed 604/605, while the exact two-imprint qualification remains in [edition and preservation](EDITION_AND_PRESERVATION.md).
