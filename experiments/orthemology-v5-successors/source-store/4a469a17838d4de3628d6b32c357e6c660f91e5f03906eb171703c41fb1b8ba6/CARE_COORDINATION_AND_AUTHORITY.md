# Care, coordination and the limits of source authority

Tenth Orthemology v5 research candidate v2, 3 October 2026 UTC. A focused continuation of the independently reviewed organised-reliance result. No canonical amendment, actual divine address, universal authority theorem or essential-integrity conclusion is claimed.

## Result

A source's responsibility for epistemic dependence can make its protective coordination practically significant for the recipient. It does not make the recipient's will the source's property. The positive intermediate result is a **bounded reason to cooperate with an actually adequate protective arrangement**. A source which must arrange continuity can identify and institute a particular workable plan; once other permissible actions are fittingly coordinated around that plan, the recipient can have reason to follow its otherwise optional details. That reason can outweigh a preference for another equally good plan considered in isolation. The arrangement, rather than the bare fact that a command was issued, changes what the recipient should do.

There is a corresponding substantive limit. **An organiser cannot justify control over a recipient by making that control necessary through its own unjustified refusal of an equally adequate, reasonably available, less intrusive discharge.** The comparison must include the source's actual alternatives, not only the alternatives it chooses to offer. Refusing an unnecessary obedience condition is not thereby refusing adequate care or waiving a claim to it. A recipient can reject the condition while still owing care to affected third parties and while needing to stop an independently unsafe activity.

This produces different decisions in cases that share the same source, expertise, proper function and special responsibility. It is more than a distinction between truth and authority. It tells the recipient when a protective instruction can settle an operational detail, when a refusal leaves the source's duty in place, and why obedience extorted by threatened withdrawal does not acquire legitimacy through that threat. It does not establish a power to settle all the recipient's reasons by issuing a directive. Such a power requires a further justified allocation of decision-making, not merely a duty to protect.

## 1. What has already been earned

The reviewed Tenth result concerns an intelligent G that actively organises R's legitimate bounded reliance on a provision I, understands the exposure, and controls a relevant route of response. Its new normative consequence is a special, defeasible reason for proportionate care, potentially including continuity, planning, substitution or adequate release. The responsibility can precede a fresh assurance and survive G's change of purpose. Mere authorship, ability, passive noninterference and recipient preference do not establish that relation. [B1]

That result already denies that responsibility confers jurisdiction or forces a competent R to accept assistance. It already recognises informed refusal, effective risk allocation, multiple organisers and independently binding obligations concerning others. Repeating those conclusions is not the present increment.

Sixth already distinguishes the facts fixing a true or faithful representation, evidence for those facts, and the authority to change a public claim. It also distinguishes an intended function from a morally good end and from a creature's duty to comply. Its positive source account joins apprehension, fitting end, means and effective institution without assuming that their co-location gives every source intention binding force. [B2]

The attribute and motivation arguments already make recipient goods relevant to an informed source's choices, defend intelligent beneficence as an eligible excellence, and leave comprehensive practical governance to a further warrant. Ninth preserves the distinction between apprehending a reason, fixing a function and grounding normative authority. Those general resources are inherited. [B3–B5]

The question here is what happens on R's side when G acts on the new responsibility. Does the source merely owe an action, or can its choice of a response alter the recipient's reasons? And if it can, what further fact would make a direction binding because it is that source's decision?

## 2. The authority test must allow a real change of decision

An expert can give R decisive evidence that a channel is unsafe. R may then have powerful reasons not to rely on it, even if the expert has no right to decide R's other projects. A source can also announce what it will actually do, thereby changing the conditions under which a project can succeed. Neither case should be dismissed as normatively idle merely because it is not a command power.

For the stronger claim, use a more demanding test. G has directive authority in a domain if its authorised choice can give R a duty to act as specified, with some ability to settle the relevant matter against R's otherwise permissible competing judgment. Its scope, any reasons it displaces, and its defeaters must be identified. It need not be unlimited, infallible or irresistible. But the proposition that R should do what G says whenever that action is independently best is not enough to establish it.

Two contrasts are especially useful:

1. **Coordination-sensitive change.** Before a plan is announced, either of two procedures is acceptable. After a fitting common arrangement is established, using the other procedure disrupts the arrangement. The recipient's reason changes even though the abstract technical merits do not.
2. **Settlement-sensitive change.** Hold the relevant safety, coordination, other people's reliance and permissible interests fixed. Ask whether a harmless deviation becomes wrongful merely because G validly chose otherwise. If so, a decision-making power is doing additional work. If not, the demonstrated result is a reason arising from the arrangement, not yet that further power.

The second is a diagnostic, not an assertion that all legitimate authority must prohibit every harmless deviation. A genuine authority might expressly permit it. The question is whether there is any independently warranted force of G's decision beyond the particular substantive or coordinative reasons already identified.

## 3. The positive bridge: a fitting arrangement can require cooperation

Consider an epistemic service supporting a shared, time-limited observation. G has deliberately organised the legitimate reliance and now owes proportionate continuity care. A necessary transition can be performed through either procedure A or B. Both preserve the fixed target and the required evidential record. Neither requires R to endorse a proposition, surrender an unrelated project, waive a wrong, or accept a new personal risk. A synchronized pause and resume sequence is needed; uncoordinated mixing of A and B would lose part of the shared observation.

G arranges A, gives adequate notice and explanation, accommodates relevant recipient constraints, and actually prepares the support and the other participants for A. The choice of arrangement is independently permissible in light of the alternatives available when it is made. Merely expending resources or recruiting other people's reliance does not establish that permissibility. R understands this. R slightly prefers B but has no countervailing protected interest, superior safety evidence or workable non-disruptive way to use B. R can complete or disengage on the stated fair terms.

R now has a reason to use A that it lacked when the two procedures were merely unselected possibilities. If the shared stakes sufficiently outweigh the slight preference, R should use A. Insisting on B can needlessly disrupt other people's permissible activity or impose an avoidable repetition of an otherwise adequate protective performance. It is no answer that B would have been equally good before the arrangement was made.

Three elements make this a substantive normative inference.

First, R's underlying interests and the other participants' legitimate interests are assessed independently of G's choice. The organiser's preference does not make an unsafe plan safe or a wrongful project permissible. Second, the actual arrangements connect R's act to those interests. G is not merely announcing that cooperation would be convenient while leaving everyone able to proceed equally well. Third, there is a reason not needlessly to frustrate an adequate, fair discharge of a claim one continues to invoke, especially when the frustration also burdens others. That is a modest practical reciprocity in a particular action, not a debt for having received existence or an unsolicited benefit.

The third element is a defended normative addition to the due-care result. Its rationale is that a claim to fitting assistance protects a real interest; it does not give its bearer an unrestricted claim to dictate all equally adequate means regardless of the effects on others. To insist on another means needs a relevant reason once a fair, usable arrangement is actually in place. An arbitrary preference can be a reason, but need not outweigh the avoidable disruption. This does not define R's original reliance as legitimate only if R obeys; legitimacy was fixed before the coordination question arose. Nor does denying R an unrestricted entitlement to dictate means give G a right to select them. N1 supplies no freestanding duty to accept help or obey a provider: its recipient-facing force still requires independently fair formation and a relevant avoidable frustration or burden of the kind specified in this case. If neither is present, the care claim does not fill the gap.

G's special responsibility matters here in a definite way. It explains why G should do the work of making the protective plan, rather than waiting for an uninvolved expert to do it. The resulting enacted plan can make a specific response fitting for R. An equally informed outsider H cannot displace it merely by announcing B. If H supplies a genuinely better alternative, accepts the coordination, or exposes a serious defect in A, the assessment can change. Source history grounds G's planning responsibility; the actual quality and formation of the arrangement ground its recipient-facing practical force.

No formal human contract is needed for this result. R need not have bought the service, promised obedience or even known G before the transition. But R must have adequate access to the relevant plan and reasons before a culpable refusal is attributed. The historical duty can exist while G remains unidentified; recipient cooperation with a particular instruction requires more identification and access than the mere existence of that duty.

This bridge does not transfer G's entire protective burden to R. G must still take fitting precautions, communicate usable instructions and consider reasonable accommodations. Even a wrongful refusal does not automatically release every remaining duty, particularly duties to others or duties which can still be performed without disproportionate cost.

## 4. Why that result is not yet a power to command

Now alter only one fact. R can use B through an independently verified translation which preserves the shared observation, imposes no additional relevant burden and does not mislead anyone about which procedure is used. G knows this. The sole objection is that G selected A and wants that choice obeyed.

The previous reason for R to use A has disappeared. The due-care relation and G's expertise remain, but no corresponding obligation to follow A has been established by them. R's use of B does not prevent protection, exploit another participant, deny a legitimate claim of G, or compromise the task. A separate decision-making right might alter the conclusion, but it would need its own ground.

The change does not prove that G lacks every possible authority. It shows exactly which recipient reason this argument earned and where it stops. The same inference would work if a jointly usable convention, a coordinating recipient, or a temporary helper established the equally fitting plan. Their claims to act may differ; the coordination reason does not depend on original sourcehood as such.

The conservative description is therefore **protective coordination standing**: G has a reason to propose and implement a fitting plan, and R can have reason to accommodate its actual operation. This includes more than advice about a pre-existing fact, because the performance arrangement helps create the relevant practical situation. It includes less than a warranted power to bind by choosing. It should not be reported as a discovery that organised reliance confers general practical authority.

## 5. The no-leverage limit: assess alternatives on both sides

The hardest abuse is not an openly unrelated command. It is an organiser making its own compliance with due care conditional on recipient submission, then citing the resulting danger as the justification for authority.

Suppose G can perform the transition with independent verification intact, at no greater relevant legitimate cost. Instead it says: surrender the independent check, accept my future declarations without review, or lose the support around which I organised your task. R cannot safely exit during the present exposure. The imposed choice is not an adequate release. Nor does R's refusal establish that it refused the care itself.

The reason is not that a recipient may reject every protective measure. It is that the alleged need for control disappears once G's own equally fitting discharge alternative is admitted. The exposure supplies a reason for G to provide care; it does not supply G with a reason to withhold that care in order to enlarge its jurisdiction. Allowing the latter would let the size of a prior responsibility become a measure of the leverage its bearer may extract from its beneficiary.

The operative comparison is exact:

- Hold fixed the recipient, supported target, real stakes, legitimate risk allocation and independently warranted claims.
- Identify what cooperation is actually needed under the fitting responses G can reasonably provide, including alternatives G has not chosen to offer. Examine the alternatives when the restrictive arrangement was selected as well as those available at the later decision point.
- Ask whether the contested condition protects one of those respects or merely adds a demand for control.
- Assess any real costs, effects on others, incompatible obligations and values of personal choice. An apparently less intrusive alternative need not be required when it sacrifices a relevant greater good or an independently legitimate interest.

There is a dynamic version of the abuse. G initially has an equally adequate, reasonably available, less intrusive option, with no relevant offsetting loss. It unjustifiably forecloses that option and deliberately arranges innocent participants around the restrictive plan. Switching later would now impose real costs. Those sunk costs do not retrospectively justify G's selection or create the authority it lacked. An independently fair formation cannot be inferred from the fact that people have become dependent on the chosen plan.

Current and earlier judgments can therefore differ. R may now have reason to cooperate temporarily to protect innocent participants, although G had no right to manufacture that situation. G's fitting responsibilities to remedy or reconfigure the arrangement remain assessable under the currently available measures and legitimate burdens. R's temporary cooperation does not by itself endorse the original selection or cancel those responsibilities. Nor does the earlier availability of a costless alternative guarantee that costless restoration is still possible. The wrong can persist as a reason for fitting remedial effort without making an impossible remedy obligatory.

This is not a universal least-interference optimisation rule. Two responses can involve incomparable legitimate costs. Nor does every possible accommodation have to be furnished. The decisive controlled case contains an equally adequate, reasonably available alternative with no relevant offsetting loss. In that case the source cannot make a gratuitous condition authoritative by declining the alternative.

Notice a further important separation. Wrongful leverage can still create urgent reasons for R to comply. If third parties will otherwise be harmed, R may have powerful reasons to take the demanded step while objecting to G's conduct. The source's creating that emergency does not make those third-party interests unreal. But a reason to avert the harm is not a vindication of G's right to demand the step. Recipient action and source entitlement can come apart. No claim that resistance must always prevail is made.

This limit is stronger than simply saying that coerced consent is invalid. There may be no consent claim. The source may instead argue that its duty gives it a right to select the necessary means. The response is that a means is not rendered normatively necessary merely by the duty-bearer's unjustified refusal of another fitting means.

## 6. Refusal, revised criteria and reliable warnings

### A refusal need not be a waiver

If competent R refuses the unnecessary obedience condition in §5, the original exposure and G's fitting ability to address it remain. The continuing care claim therefore need not disappear. By contrast, R may freely reject every adequate, fairly offered response concerning a risk it is entitled to accept. That can alter what G should do. Between these cases lie ordinary accommodations, misunderstandings and burdens: calling them all refusal obscures what was actually refused.

R's scope of control also matters. R can sometimes accept a risk to its own inquiry; it cannot thereby authorise avoidable harm to other recipients. A plan may remain justified for others even when R withdraws. The positive cooperation reason must identify those actual interests rather than calling every dissent disruptive.

### A source may correct criteria without acquiring jurisdiction

Suppose G gives good evidence that a prior calibration rule now systematically misidentifies the fixed target. R has epistemic reason to revise that rule. Permission to revise criteria is not permission to preserve a false attribution by redefining fidelity. This is the inherited target-preservation discipline. [B2]

Conversely, the fact that G instituted the faculty does not prove that its preferred test is now adequate. If G asks R to remove the only relevant check, the proposed replacement needs a truth-linked warrant and the practical change needs appropriate permission. Authenticated provenance alone does not supply either. The source's duty to support understanding gives additional reason to request a usable explanation or alternative; it gives no licence to force belief.

R is also free, within its legitimate responsibilities, to change or abandon an optional inquiry. That differs from claiming to have repaired the original inquiry while secretly changing its target. G's authority to describe what its own service supports does not automatically become authority to forbid R's other permissible pursuits. A newly introduced service restriction also cannot retroactively remove already organised exposure.

### A source's breach does not make continued reliance safe

G may wrongfully withdraw support while truthfully warning that the old channel will no longer be reliable. If R has adequate independent grounds to credit the warning, R should adjust its reliance. It can retain its complaint and any claim to care or repair. Rejecting G's supposed jurisdiction is not evidence that the old channel remains safe.

This is a practically important combination: decline the unwarranted demand, do not treat the refusal as a waiver, and still refrain from the independently unsafe irreversible act. Neither obedience nor continued unsupported reliance is forced by the conclusion that G acted wrongfully.

## 7. What would support stronger authority?

The present result neither assumes that consent is always necessary nor prohibits a substantive source argument for authority. It locates the additional burden.

One route is a valid, bounded allocation of decision-making. A competent recipient may entrust a specified operational choice to G, within the recipient's own powers and without coercion. The allocation must actually cover the decision, its permissible error tolerance and any revision or exit conditions. Permission to supply information, for example, is not permission to choose the recipient's life project. The organiser's historical responsibility can explain why G is a sensible candidate, but it does not itself execute that entrustment.

A second route is a noncontractual justification for letting G settle a particular class of decisions. A serious case would show why unified direction improves conformity to independently applicable reasons, why competing individual determination would undermine that benefit, why this is a matter appropriately settled that way, and why G is a fitting accessible bearer of the role. The account must address the possibility of competent dissent, manipulation, alternative coordinators and less intrusive provision. These are objects of argument, not boxes filled by writing legitimate next to the source.

For a temporary rescue of an incapacitated recipient, intervention may be justified without present consent. That possibility cannot be generalised from superior source expertise to recipient incapacity. An adult can lack technical knowledge while retaining the right to choose which permissible risks or projects to undertake. A general power over a subject requires considerably more than evidence that one maintenance procedure is safer.

The strongest service-oriented objection is that the coordination result is already authority once an actually worthwhile role is sufficiently established. The terminology may be defensible in that limited sense. The substantive further question remains whether G's decision has any justified settling force which survives a harmless alternative or an otherwise permissible disagreement. Sections 3–5 establish and test the arrangement-dependent reason; they do not settle every theory of directive authority. They also do not insist that a further authority must come from human appointment.

Our argument does not take a position on every part of that theory. It uses a narrower source-governance comparison to prevent responsibility, competence and actually justified decision-making from being treated as interchangeable. Any stronger positive application must defend the additional normative allocation in ordinary argument and exhibit its recipient-facing consequence. ## 8. Original source without a human contract

None of the positive cooperation reasoning requires G to receive its being from a supplier, fear sanctions, gain a benefit from R or have entered a human legal agreement. The relevant goods are the actual recipient's understanding and agency, the relevant actions are protective arrangements, and the added normative relation is fair accommodation of their real effects. A source can voluntarily institute those arrangements without asking R to promise anything.

But original productive scope does not by itself instantiate the coordination premises. If G can provide equally adequate care while leaving R's disputed choice free, the practical need to direct that choice has not been shown. Increasing the source's capacity can remove a finite maintainer's constraint instead of increasing its right to control. This is a conditional comparative result, not a deduction that an omnipotent source must accommodate every preference. Real third-party goods, logical incompatibilities and the fixed task can still limit alternatives; they must be identified.

The target matters. A source cannot make one unchanged assertion both faithfully attribute 17 and faithfully attribute 71 to the same unaltered inscription merely by permitting different criteria. That is a target-relative correctness limit, not a restriction imposed by a rival source. Conversely, a limitation of one contingent implementation must not be carried into the original-source case as though every adequate implementation had it. The practical authority case must establish the relevant constraint at the scope it uses.

There is also no need here to establish global unity, necessary existence or essential T0 first. A bounded recipient-facing conclusion can be warranted about a sufficiently identified organiser and plan. Essential integrity would be a stronger claim about all eligible exercises; it is not the price of crediting a local fair arrangement. Conversely, lack of essential integrity does not make ordinary defeasible evidence of this plan worthless. Actual attribution, competence, truth-linked information and the applicable defeaters still matter. [B4–B6]

The conditional whole-origin comparison adds no second absolute supplier. Other participants and their actions can remain received within G's granted productive field. Their real agency and interests are not erased by that reception. Their existence can provide reasons within a common plan without establishing any particular theory of the ultimate grounding of those reasons.

## 9. Strongest challenge and current disposition

The strongest conservative challenge is that all the recipient-side force is ordinary noninjury or fair cooperation; organised-reliance responsibility adds nothing to authority. The challenge succeeds against the strong thesis. This paper has not derived a new command power from responsibility.

It does not erase the narrower increment. The earlier result tells us who must arrange care before an assurance. The present application identifies how a fitting performance of that responsibility can create a specific recipient reason before any independent authority is established, and how to prevent that process from becoming coercive self-authorisation. The historical relation explains why G has the planning burden; actual fair coordination explains why R may now need to choose A; the counterfactual alternatives test explains when R can refuse while the duty persists. These are different parts of one practical explanation, not three names for the same premise.

The new result is thus an **application-level bridge and limit**, not a new general theory of authority. It earns a bounded positive recipient consequence and a sharper negative consequence under matched facts. It leaves stronger authority genuinely open rather than declaring it impossible.

The next decisive work is a case-specific defence of a source's normative right to settle a decision after the independent informational and coordinative reasons have been accounted for. That work should identify the recipient, decision class, permissible disagreement, error tolerance, alternatives available to the source, and the source's warranted role. A generic creator title, an assurance of benevolence or the bare existence of an epistemic faculty will not supply those facts. An independently defended source-nature or role argument could; a human contract is not stipulated as the only possible route.
