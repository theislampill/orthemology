## 1. Information, policies, and the lower bound

Candidate lines 10–23 correctly fix a passive iid receipt process and a common causal policy. The action at index \(n\ge1\) uses exactly \(n\) receipts; an initial action is a separate contract. The objective counts ordinary action mistakes, not regret weighted by the gap.

For a seed space with common law \(\eta\), any deterministic policy driven by that seed can be unfolded to a function \(a_n(r,y_1,\ldots,y_n)\). Dependence on earlier actions or internal memory causes no additional information issue: those actions and memory are themselves functions of the same seed and earlier receipts. Since receipts are passive and the seed is independent of tag and receipts, the distribution of the seed conditional on a fixed receipt word is the same \(\eta\) under either hypothesis. Therefore

\[
 d_n(y)=\int 1\{a_n(r,y)=1\}\,d\eta(r)\in[0,1]
\]

is common to both tags. This justifies the candidate's wordwise error inequality at lines 53–66. Fresh independent randomization can be included in one seed containing all its coordinates. No Markov, finite-memory, stationarity, count-only, or deterministic restriction is imposed by the lower-bound argument.

At each word, the error-mass sum is the affine function
\(P_0(y)d_n(y)+P_1(y)(1-d_n(y))\). Its minimum on \([0,1]\) is exactly \(\min(P_0(y),P_1(y))\). Summing over words gives \(e_0(n)+e_1(n)\ge B_n\). For every finite \(N\),

\[
\max(C_0(\pi),C_1(\pi))\ge\tfrac12\sum_{n=1}^N B_n.
\]

Taking \(N\to\infty\) is legitimate in the nonnegative extended reals. This gives the lifetime lower bound without exchanging an infimum with a sum or assuming that one tag maximizes risk at every stage.

The argument would fail if the seed or additional feedback conveyed the hidden tag, or if policy choices changed the receipt experiment. Both are excluded by the contract. It also does not cover a different objective that stops charging after the policy declines to act.

## 2. The deterministic tie rule and simultaneous attainment

At non-tied words the likelihood ratio selects empirical majority. At length \(2m\), each tied word has probability \(p^mq^m\) under either tag. Of the \(\binom{2m}{m}\) tied words, exactly \(\binom{2m-1}{m-1}=\binom{2m}{m}/2\) start with 1. Thus choosing the first observed bit assigns exactly half the tied mass to each action, separately under both tags.

No independence of the tie choices at different times is needed. The objective is the expectation of a nonnegative sum, so the per-index half-mass statement is sufficient even though all tie choices use the same first bit. The rule is defined on every nonempty history, is causal, and commutes with bitwise complement. Complementing a tag-0 history yields its tag-1 probability, and complements the chosen action. Hence the two tagwise error probabilities agree at each stage and equal \(B_n/2\).

The same single policy therefore attains the lower bound simultaneously for every finite horizon and for the infinite-horizon sum once that sum is evaluated. This is the essential Bayes-to-minimax step: Bayes optimality alone would not suffice without the verified equal-risk witness. The candidate supplies both at lines 68–87.

Always choosing 0 at ties remains Bayes optimal, but its tagwise risks are unbalanced. The candidate correctly gives its larger worst-tag cost at lines 175–182. An unbounded ordered-history policy is allowed here; no finite-state implementation follows.

## 3. Occupation identities

Write \(d=q-p=2\delta>0\), with right-step probability \(p\) and left-step probability \(q\). For \(0<\delta<1/2\), both probabilities are positive.

### Finite absorption and hitting

On \([-M,k]\), the proposed \(h_M(x)=(r^{x+M}-1)/(r^{k+M}-1)\), \(r=q/p\), has the correct boundary values and first-step harmonic equation. Absorption is almost sure: from any interior state, taking only left steps reaches the lower endpoint within at most \(M+k\) steps and has probability at least \(q^{M+k}>0\). Repeating blocks bounds nonabsorption geometrically. Consequently the bounded harmonic solution gives the actual hitting probability; no unproved transience assumption is involved.

The events \(\{T_k<T_{-M}\}\) increase to \(\{T_k<\infty\}\), because a path that hits \(k\) in finite time has a finite minimum beforehand. The limit from zero is \((p/q)^k\). Similarly, absorption on \([0,M]\), followed by the limit of the upper-hit probability from 1, proves almost-sure hitting of 0 from 1. Translation gives hit probability \(p/q\) from −1. These steps justify lines 101–124, including the limit events.

### Returns and Green functions

The probability of a positive-time return from 0 is \(f=p+q(p/q)=2p<1\). If \(N_0\) counts visits including the initial visit, the strong Markov property at finite successive returns gives
\(\Pr(N_0\ge m+1)=f^m\). Thus \(E N_0=1/(1-f)=1/d\). The argument proves finiteness directly before any potentially circular renewal equation.

On the event of first hitting a positive level \(k\), the expected number of visits from that hit onward is the same \(1/d\) by translation and strong Markov. Therefore \(G(0,k)=(p/q)^k/d\). Tonelli then gives positive-state occupation

\[
\sum_{k\ge1}\frac{(p/q)^k}{d}=\frac{p}{d^2}.
\]

The zero-state occupation after acquisition is \(1/d-1\): precisely one initial visit must be removed. There is no corresponding time-zero term in positive occupation. This checks lines 128–153, including the easily missed indexing term.

Combining these occupations with the per-index balanced tie masses gives

\[
\frac p{d^2}+\frac12\left(\frac1d-1\right)
=\frac{2pq}{d^2}
=\frac1{8\delta^2}-\frac12.
\]

No assertion that the tie bit is independent of the walk is needed or used.

## 4. Separate initial-action convention

With no receipts, any common randomized action has error probabilities \(\alpha\) and \(1-\alpha\), whose sum is one. Combining that fact with the wordwise lower bound at every later index gives total worst-tag cost at least \(1/2+V_{post}\). It is not justified merely by adding maxima; the valid operation is adding the two tagwise costs first.

A fair initial bit, followed by the balanced deterministic policy, makes both tagwise costs exactly \(1/2+V_{post}=1/(8\delta^2)\). Later choices can ignore that bit. The candidate's claim and its proof at lines 169–173 are correct.

The candidate appropriately does not assert that randomization is necessary for every initial-inclusive minimax implementation. For example, at \(\delta=1/4\), choose initial action 0 and subsequently break all ties toward 1. The two post-acquisition costs are 2 and 1, and the initial costs are 0 and 1, producing total costs 2 and 2. Conversely, at perfect separation below, a deterministic initial action does have a strictly larger minimax cost than a randomized one. Initial-action and randomization conventions must therefore remain explicit.

## 5. Requested boundary checks

### Perfect separation: \(\delta=1/2\)

Here tag 0 always produces 0 and tag 1 always produces 1. The first receipt identifies the tag exactly, so the post-acquisition value is 0. The displayed formula also equals 0. The proof using \(r=q/p\) must not be evaluated at \(p=0\); this endpoint is established directly instead.

If a zero-receipt action is charged and independent randomization is allowed, its minimax contribution is exactly 1/2 and later cost is zero. Thus the initial-inclusive value is 1/2, also agreeing with its formula. If that initial action must be deterministic, its worst-tag cost is 1, and no later nonnegative cost can cancel it. This last deterministic convention is a different optimization problem.

### Vanishing gap and zero gap

For positive \(\delta\),
\(\delta^2V_{post}(\delta)=1/8-\delta^2/2\), so the exact leading divergence as \(\delta\downarrow0\) is \(1/(8\delta^2)\). The initial-inclusive value has the same leading term.

At \(\delta=0\), the two receipt laws coincide. For every common policy the two error probabilities sum to one at each stage. Through \(N\) receipts the worst-tag cost is at least \(N/2\); the balanced reflection-equivariant policy attains that finite-horizon value. Letting \(N\to\infty\) proves the lifetime minimax value is infinite. This is a separate argument, not substitution into a formula with zero denominator.
