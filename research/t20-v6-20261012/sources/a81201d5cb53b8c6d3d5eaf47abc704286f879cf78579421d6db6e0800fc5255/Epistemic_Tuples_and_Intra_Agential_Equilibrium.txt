Epistemic Tuples and
Intra-Agential Equilibrium
A formal thesis on evidentiary profiles, cognitive faculties, mixed epistemic policies,
and equilibrium




   Central thesis. Evidentiary weight is often better represented as a tuple than as a scalar.
   Once such evidence is understood as genuinely epistemic – that is, as apprehended, discriminated,
   integrated, defeated, retained, compared, and acted upon by a differentiated cognitive architecture
   – the relevant interaction need not be between historians or external agents. It can be intra-agential:
   functionally distinct cognitive faculties can jointly regulate belief. When their admissible responses
   are finite and mutually conditioning, mixed response policies generate the finite-dimensional
   compact convex mixed-strategy space of a finite game. Ordinary Nash equilibrium follows only
   after the additional assumptions required for Nash-representable utilities are supplied. If the
   evidentiary values remain genuinely vector-valued or partially ordered, a vector, Pareto, team, or
   constrained-optimality solution concept may be more faithful than scalar Nash.

1 Executive statement
The ordinary question “which source should carry more weight?” is frequently ill-posed. It presupposes
that evidentiary force lies on a single common scale. In many real comparisons, especially across
heterogeneous source classes, one source is stronger in one respect and weaker in another. A better
representation is therefore a profile

                                       e = (e1 , e2 , . . . , ek ) ∈ E ⊆ Rk ,

whose coordinates track distinct evidentiary virtues or liabilities.
This changes the structure of epistemic comparison. A scalar asks for a total ranking. A tuple can preserve
incomparability, trade-offs, and partial dominance. The crucial next step is not to imagine the tuples
as inert mathematical objects and then append a historian from outside. They are epistemic because
they occur within a faculty-mediated system of apprehension. Perception, memory, source-monitoring,
coherence-checking, defeater-recognition, inferential integration, and action-guidance can each constrain
what responses are admissible, and their outputs can condition one another.
Thus, the relevant “players” in a game-theoretic encoding need not be separate persons. They may be
functionally differentiated cognitive processes within one subject:

             faculty/process ←→ player            admissible epistemic response ←→ strategy .

If each process has finitely many coarse-grained admissible responses, and mixtures over those responses
have a coherent cognitive interpretation, the product of mixed response sets is a compact convex strategy
space. If, in addition, each process has a Nash-representable real-valued payoff over joint response profiles,
the standard finite-game equilibrium result applies. But the existence of such an equilibrium is not the
discovery of a uniquely correct historical weighting. It is the existence of a stable joint policy profile
under the chosen encoding.
The deepest lesson is therefore not “Nash tells us whether hadith beats inscriptions.” It is:

    Structural lesson
    Heterogeneous evidence may be epistemically stable without being reducible to one scalar ranking.
    The stable object can be a joint policy for handling several incomparable evidentiary dimensions
    at once.


2 Evidentiary weight as a tuple
2.1 Why scalarisation is often premature
A scalar weight

forces every evidentiary consideration onto one axis. That may be legitimate after a justified aggregation
rule has been established, but it should not be assumed at the outset. A tuple instead records the profile
directly:
                                          e = (p, t, i, s, d, c, . . .),
where, for example, the coordinates might encode provenance quality, temporal proximity, independence,
semantic specificity, defeater exposure, coherence, or other case-specific dimensions.

1. Which coordinates matter for the inquiry?

2. Which coordinates, if any, may trade off against one another?

3 What makes an evidentiary tuple epistemic
An evidentiary tuple is not epistemic merely because the word epistemic is written beside it. It is
epistemic insofar as its coordinates enter the operations of a cognitive system: they are apprehended,
discriminated, integrated, retained, compared, defeated, revised, and used in judgment or action.
This means cognition is endogenous to the object under study. The more faithful schematic is not

                                tuple −→ external observer later added,

but
               evidence −→ faculty-mediated apprehension −→ joint epistemic regulation.

3.1 Differentiated cognitive loci
Let
                                           F = {F1 , F2 , . . . , Fn }
be a set of functionally differentiated cognitive operations. They need not correspond one-to-one with
traditional philosophical faculties; they may be computational or functional decompositions. Examples
include:

• perception or material-evidence assessment;

• memory and retention;

• source-monitoring and provenance assessment;

• testimony assessment;

• coherence and consistency checking;

• defeater and undercutting-defeater recognition;

• inferential integration;

• confidence calibration;

• action-guidance or decision thresholding.

The important feature is not the labels but the interaction. If the response of Fi depends upon outputs
of other processes, then
                                               Fi (e, s−i )
is more accurate than
                                                    Fi (e).
The architecture is mutually conditioning rather than merely parallel.

4 From cognitive architecture to game structure
4.1 The functional encoding
Suppose each process Fi has a finite set of admissible coarse-grained responses

                                              Si = {s1i , . . . , sm
                                                                   i }.
                                                                     i



Examples might include retain, discount, quarantine, revise, seek corroboration, treat as defeated, or pass
downstream with a confidence interval. These are illustrative response types, not a universal vocabulary.
The functional game-theoretic encoding is:

                                  Fi ↔ player i,        Si ↔ strategy set of i.

A joint response profile is
                                      s = (s1 , . . . , sn ) ∈ S1 × · · · × Sn .
The epistemic consequence of one response may depend on the entire profile. A source-monitoring process
that would ordinarily retain a report may instead discount it after a defeater process identifies common
dependence; a coherence process may revise an inference after memory retrieval supplies a conflicting
datum. This profile-dependence is the interaction term.

4.2 Why this is genuinely intra-agential
Nothing in the formal vocabulary of game theory requires that a “player” be a biologically independent
person. A player is a locus to which a strategy set and preference/payoff structure are assigned. Treating
functional sub-processes as players is therefore an abstraction. Its legitimacy depends on whether
the decomposition captures useful conditional independence, competing constraints, or best-response
structure.
The result is an intra-agential game: one cognitive subject, several functionally differentiated loci of
response, and one jointly regulated belief state.

    Important distinction
    The plurality required for game structure can be functional rather than personal. The interaction
    can occur inside one cognitive architecture.

5.1 What does “mixing” mean cognitively?
A probability vector in a mathematical model should not be mistaken automatically for a literal mental
roulette wheel. Several cognitive interpretations are possible:

• Stochastic uptake: a process probabilistically selects among admissible responses under uncertainty.

• Graded policy: mixture weights encode degrees of endorsement or allocation of attention rather
  than repeated random choice.

• Unresolved competition: several candidate responses remain active with different strengths until
  downstream resolution.

• Population or temporal interpretation: the mixture represents frequencies across repeated
  episodes, sub-processes, or time slices.

The model must say which interpretation is intended. “Convex” is not itself enough; the convex
combination has to correspond to something cognitively or epistemically meaningful.


6 Exactly where Nash enters
6.1 The mixed extension of a finite game is not yet the theorem
The existence of Σ gives the geometry needed for mixed strategies, but an ordinary Nash game also
requires real-valued payoff functions

                                               ui : S1 × · · · × Sn → R,

or their multilinear extensions to mixed strategies. Such utilities can be posited directly, or they
can represent preferences over lotteries when the relevant completeness, transitivity, continuity, and
independence conditions are satisfied. A merely partial or non-von-Neumann–Morgenstern ordering does
not automatically supply this representation. A Nash equilibrium σ ∗ ∈ Σ satisfies

                       ui (σi∗ , σ−i
                                  ∗                ∗
                                     ) ≥ ui (σi , σ−i )   for every i and every σi ∈ ∆(Si ).

No locus gains, according to its encoded payoff, by unilateral deviation.
For a finite normal-form game, a mixed Nash equilibrium exists. The convexity relevant to the standard
proof is obtained by passing from finite pure strategy sets to mixed-strategy simplices; expected payoff is
continuous and affine in each player’s own mixture.

6.2 What the theorem does and does not establish
Once the encoding has supplied finite strategy sets and Nash-representable utilities, equilibrium existence
is guaranteed. But this is an existence theorem for that model. It does not establish:

• that the original evidence uniquely determines the player decomposition;

• that the chosen utilities are historically or epistemically correct;

• that a Nash equilibrium is unique;

• that equilibrium coincides with truth;

• that one source class receives a uniquely correct scalar weight;

• that non-cooperative Nash is the best solution concept for the cognitive architecture.

Thus,
                   equilibrium existence ̸= correct scalar weighting ̸= historical truth.

What equilibrium establishes is conditional stability: under the specified decomposition, admissible
responses, and payoff representation, there exists a policy profile resistant to unilateral improvement.


7 The vector-valued problem: when scalar Nash is too strong
The original motivation was precisely to avoid premature scalarisation. If each process evaluates outcomes
by a vector
                                                Ui (s) ∈ Rk
with only a partial order, then ordinary “profitable deviation” is not automatically well-defined. One
must specify how vector outcomes are compared.

7.1 Cone-ordered improvement
Let Ki ⊆ Rk be a cone defining admissible improvement for process i. We may say that x weakly improves
upon y when
                                              x − y ∈ Ki .
A vector equilibrium can then be characterised by the absence of a unilateral deviation whose outcome
lies in the strict improvement cone:

                                                  ∗
                                                     ) − Ui (σ ∗ ) ∈
                                                                   / Ki \ {0}

for every admissible unilateral deviation σi′ .
This preserves multidimensional evidentiary profiles instead of collapsing them into a scalar merely to
invoke the classical theorem.

7.2 Pareto-Nash intuition
The corresponding epistemic intuition is simple: a stable profile is one in which no process can unilaterally
change its policy and make the relevant epistemic outcome strictly better in every dimension recognised
by its improvement order.
Such an equilibrium may leave trade-offs unresolved. That is a feature. If one response improves
provenance sensitivity while reducing semantic coverage, neither response need dominate. The equilibrium
can preserve the tension rather than hiding it.


8 Nash may exist and still be the wrong solution concept
A cognitive architecture may be representable as a game without being best understood as a non-
cooperative game. Several alternatives deserve explicit separation.

8.1 Common-interest or team formulation
If all faculties serve one shared epistemic end, such as reliable true-enough belief under resource constraints,
then the more natural object may be
                                                   max W (σ)
                                                  σ∈Σ

for a common objective W . The faculties are then components of a team rather than strategic competitors.

8.2 Constrained optimisation
If one process proposes responses while others act as hard constraints, the model may instead be

                                     max f (x)    subject to   gj (x) ≤ 0.
                                      x∈X

For example, a coherence process may not have its own “interest” but may forbid policies violating
consistency constraints.

8.3 Belief-revision or pipeline formulation
If the architecture is essentially sequential,

                                    e → F1 (e) → F2 (F1 (e)) → · · · → B,

a pipeline or belief-revision operator may be more faithful. Strategic interaction should not be imposed
merely because a game-theoretic vocabulary is available.

8.4 Correlated and bargaining formulations
If cognitive processes share signals, common causes, or coordination devices, correlated equilibrium
may describe stable coordination better than independent mixing. If the core phenomenon is negoti-
ated compromise among incompatible desiderata, bargaining or social-choice machinery may be more
appropriate.

    Model-selection principle
    The thesis is not that every faculty-mediated epistemic problem is a Nash game. It is that
    intra-agential game structure is a coherent and sometimes natural encoding once responses are
    mutually conditioning. The correct solution concept depends on the architecture.


9 Worked example: hadith and inscriptions
The example is not intended to resolve any particular historical dispute. Its purpose is to show why source
classes can carry strength in different respects and why scalar ranking can destroy useful information.
Let a source profile contain six dimensions:

                                            e = (T, P, I, S, D, C),

where, illustratively:

• T : transmission integrity;
Epistemic Tuples and Intra-Agential Equilibrium                                                          10


• P : temporal or material proximity;

• I: independence from other witnesses;

• S: semantic specificity or richness;

• D: resistance to known defeaters;

• C: coherence with independently supported background information.

A hadith report h and an inscription r could have profiles of the form

                                   h = (0.88, 0.56, 0.61, 0.94, 0.73, 0.81),

                                   r = (0.72, 0.96, 0.90, 0.42, 0.84, 0.77).
These numbers are purely schematic. Their role is to make the structure visible. The report is stronger
on semantic richness and perhaps transmission-specific dimensions; the inscription is stronger on material
proximity and independence. Neither coordinatewise dominates the other.

9.1 Faculty-mediated handling
Different cognitive operations may respond differently:

• source-monitoring may privilege the chain and provenance structure of h;

• material-evidence assessment may privilege the direct physical dating of r;

• defeater-recognition may discount either profile when dependence or anachronism appears;

• coherence-checking may compare both against a wider evidentiary network;

• action-guidance may require a decision threshold without pretending that the underlying profiles have
  become one-dimensional.

The system therefore seeks a joint response policy, not necessarily a winner.

9.2 The error of forced scalarisation
Suppose one chooses
                           W (e) = .15T + .30P + .20I + .10S + .15D + .10C.
The output may rank r above h. Another defensible coefficient vector may reverse the result. Unless
the coefficient choice is independently justified by the inquiry, the scalar ranking simply relocates the
substantive disagreement into the weights.
Preserving the tuples keeps visible the fact that the two source classes “hold” in different respects.

11 Propositions and consequences
Proposition 1: tuple preservation
If evidentiary dimensions are not known to be commensurable, preserving the tuple contains weakly more
explicit comparative information than an unexplained scalarisation.
Reason. A scalarisation maps many distinct tuples to the same or comparable numbers and therefore
discards coordinate-level structure unless the aggregation rule itself is retained and justified.

Proposition 2: endogenous cognition
If an evidentiary object is defined by its role in apprehension, discrimination, integration, defeat, retention,
comparison, and action, then the cognitive architecture is part of the epistemic model rather than an
external add-on.
Consequence. Interaction among cognitive processes can supply the functional plurality required for an
intra-agential strategic encoding.

Proposition 3: finite-game mixed architecture
If there are finitely many functional loci and each has a finite admissible response set, then allowing
probability distributions over those responses yields a product of finite-dimensional simplices.
Consequence. The mixed policy space is compact and convex.
Epistemic Tuples and Intra-Agential Equilibrium                                                          12


Proposition 4: conditional Nash existence
If, in addition to Proposition 3, each locus has a real-valued payoff over joint response profiles and the
usual mixed extension is used, then the resulting finite normal-form game has at least one mixed Nash
equilibrium.
Consequence. Existence follows from the formal construction, not directly from the historical evidence.

Proposition 5: equilibrium is not truth
A Nash equilibrium is stable relative to the encoded utilities and strategy sets. It need not maximise
truth, evidential adequacy, historical accuracy, or any unencoded epistemic good.
Consequence. Equilibrium should be read as a property of the cognitive-policy model, not as an oracle of
fact.

Proposition 6: vector order changes the solution concept
If outcomes are genuinely vector-valued and only partially ordered, ordinary scalar best-response language
is not available without an additional comparison rule.
Consequence. One may either justify a scalarisation or adopt a vector/Pareto equilibrium or another
multi-objective solution concept.


12 Common misunderstandings
12.1 “One mind means one player”
Not necessarily. Game-theoretic “players” can be functional loci in a model. The modelling question
is whether the sub-processes have sufficiently distinct response sets and conditional objectives to make
strategic decomposition informative.

12.2 “If the space is convex, Nash follows”
Not by convexity alone. In finite games, convexity arrives through mixed-strategy simplices, while
equilibrium existence also relies on the payoff/best-response structure. In more general games, compactness,
continuity, convexity or quasi-concavity assumptions must be stated carefully.

12.3 “A mixed policy is just uncertainty”
Not necessarily. It may encode graded endorsement, probabilistic response, unresolved competition, or
temporal/population frequency. The interpretation must be supplied.

12.4 “An equilibrium gives the correct evidentiary weights”
No. It gives a stable policy profile under the chosen encoding. Correctness of the encoding remains a
separate epistemological question.

12.5 “If Nash is not uniquely appropriate, the game model fails”
No. The discovery that a team, vector, correlated, bargaining, or constrained-optimality formulation
fits better is itself information about the architecture. Game-theoretic analysis can identify where

non-cooperative assumptions are too strong.


13 The deeper epistemological picture
The thesis can be read as a general argument against treating epistemic rationality as a single scalar
scoreboard.
A cognitively realistic subject often faces several non-identical goods:

• sensitivity to provenance;

• responsiveness to direct material evidence;

• semantic informativeness;

• resistance to defeat;

• coherence with background knowledge;

• calibration of confidence;

• actionability under finite time and attention.

Some of these goods can conflict. A system that jointly regulates them may reach a stable policy without
proving that one good always outweighs another. The equilibrium, optimum, or fixed point is therefore
best understood as a mode of coordinated epistemic governance.
This suggests a broader conception:

        rational belief regulation ≈ stable coordination among differentiated epistemic functions

subject to the evidence, the admissible transformations of that evidence, and the architecture’s governing
norms.
The important shift is from asking

                                 “Which source has the bigger weight?”

to asking

“What joint cognitive policy handles these dimensions without destroying their relevant distinctions?”


14 Research programme
A serious development of the thesis would separate empirical cognitive science, formal epistemology, and
mathematical game theory rather than allowing one to stand in for the others.

14.1 Step 1: define the evidentiary coordinates
Specify what each coordinate measures, its scale type, uncertainty, and dependence on other coordinates.
Determine which dimensions are genuinely independent and which are derived.
Epistemic Tuples and Intra-Agential Equilibrium                                                           14


14.2 Step 2: specify the faculty decomposition
Identify the smallest useful set of functionally distinct processes. Avoid both extremes: a single undiffer-
entiated agent, and an artificial proliferation of pseudo-players.

14.3 Step 3: define admissible responses
For each process, define coarse-grained response types and the conditions under which each is available.
This is where the word “admissible” becomes substantive rather than decorative.

14.4 Step 4: formalise mutual conditionality
Specify how one process’s output changes another’s available responses, payoffs, constraints, or information.
If there is no meaningful interaction, a game model may be unnecessary.

14.5 Step 5: interpret mixture
Decide whether mixture means stochastic choice, graded activation, temporal frequency, unresolved
competition, or another mechanism. Test whether convex combinations correspond to possible cognitive
states.

14.6 Step 6: choose the solution concept
Ask whether the architecture is best represented by:

• non-cooperative Nash equilibrium;

• vector/Pareto equilibrium;

• correlated equilibrium;

• common-interest/team optimisation;

• constrained optimisation;

• bargaining;

• belief revision or fixed-point dynamics.

14.7 Step 7: distinguish descriptive from normative success
A psychologically stable equilibrium can be epistemically bad. A normatively desirable policy can be
psychologically unstable. The model should state whether it is describing actual cognition, prescribing
rational cognition, or connecting the two.

14.8 Step 8: test robustness
A useful model should be tested under alternative coordinate systems, faculty decompositions, scalar-
isations, partial orders, and perturbations of response sets. If the conclusion disappears under trivial
re-encoding, it is not a robust epistemic result.

16 Conclusion
The substantive thesis is not the trivial conditional “if one has a finite game, Nash gives a mixed
equilibrium.” The substantive work lies in identifying a cognitively defensible route from heterogeneous
evidentiary profiles to an endogenous architecture of mutually conditioning epistemic responses.
Epistemic Tuples and Intra-Agential Equilibrium                                                             16


Evidentiary weights are often tuples, not scalars. Their coordinates can carry force in different respects
and need not be totally ordered. Because the objects are epistemic, they are situated within cognitive
operations rather than floating outside cognition. Those operations can be functionally differentiated
and mutually conditioning. In an appropriate coarse-grained model, they can therefore be represented
as strategic loci with admissible response sets. Finite responses and meaningful mixtures yield the
finite-dimensional compact convex mixed-policy space associated with a finite game.
At that point, ordinary Nash equilibrium is available only if the additional real-valued preference structure
required by Nash has been justified. If the original multidimensionality is retained, vector or Pareto
equilibrium may be more faithful. If the faculties genuinely share one end, a team or constrained optimum
may be better still.
The durable result is consequently architectural:

    Central thesis. A well-formed epistemology of heterogeneous evidence need not ask which
    source class “wins.” It can model how differentiated cognitive faculties jointly regulate belief
    while preserving the dimensions in which each source class succeeds or fails. Stability may reside
    in the policy of coordination itself. Equilibrium, where appropriate, is a formal account of that
    stability – not a substitute for truth and not a licence for unexplained scalarisation.


A Glossary
Evidentiary tuple
A multidimensional representation of an item or source’s evidentiary profile.
Scalarisation
A rule mapping a multidimensional profile to one real number.
Partial order
A comparison relation that permits some pairs to remain incomparable.
Faculty-mediated apprehension
The treatment of evidence through functionally differentiated cognitive operations.
Intra-agential game
A strategic model whose functional players are processes within one cognitive subject.
Admissible response
A response available to a cognitive process under the governing architecture or norms.
Mixed epistemic policy
A probability distribution or other convex weighting over admissible responses, with an explicit cognitive
interpretation.
Nash equilibrium
A joint strategy profile in which no player benefits, according to its payoff, from unilateral deviation.
Vector equilibrium
An equilibrium notion for multi-objective or partially ordered outcomes that does not require scalar
utility.
Team solution
A joint policy chosen to optimise a common objective shared by the component processes.

Congenial tuples
Evidentiary profiles admitted into a common cognitive comparison or coordination problem under a
specified compatibility/admissibility regime; the term does not itself imply total commensurability.
