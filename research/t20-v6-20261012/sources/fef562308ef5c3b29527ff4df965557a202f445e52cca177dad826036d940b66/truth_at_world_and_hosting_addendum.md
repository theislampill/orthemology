# Truth at a world and the intentional hosting premise

## The new pressure point

The first hosting note distinguished a necessary proposition type from one necessarily existing thought particular. A further distinction appears earlier: **being true with respect to a possibility** need not mean **locally instantiating the property of truth inside that possibility**. That matters because a valid actualist principle about local property instantiation cannot settle which relation modal truth uses.

This is not a claim that the conceptualist argument simply confuses de dicto and de re necessity. Anderson and Welty explicitly defend the truth-to-existence step by property actualism. The question is whether their interpretation of truth at a world is independently obligatory. Speaks's 2012 paper proposes an alternative specifically designed to retain Serious Actualism and contingency while treating propositions as potentially nonexistent. Its motivation is wider than theology: singular propositions, their objects, and the evaluation of absences raise the same issue. [Speaks, “On Possibly Nonexistent Propositions,” pp. 528–536](https://www3.nd.edu/~jspeaks/papers/2012nonexistentprops.pdf)

## An exact semantic test

Use a two-world model with an actual evaluation context c and circumstances w0 and w1. A content p is available at c. Write:

- E(w,p): p exists in w
- Sat(c,p,w): the content fixed at c is satisfied by the circumstance w
- LocalTrue(w,p): p locally bears truth in w

These are different relations, not different spellings of one predicate. In the illustrative semantics, set

    LocalTrue(w,p) iff E(w,p) and Sat(c,p,w).

This directly validates Serious Actualism for local truth. To say that p is necessary at c is to say that Sat(c,p,w) holds at every admitted circumstance w. That does not decide E(w,p).

Consider two contents:

- l: a logical truth, with Sat(c,l,w0) = Sat(c,l,w1) = true
- q: the claim that propositions exist, with Sat(c,q,w0) = true and Sat(c,q,w1) = false

Let l and q exist at w0 but not at w1. Then l is necessary under the external evaluation; q is not. Every locally true proposition exists. The familiar objection that “propositions exist” becomes necessary whenever truth is restricted to worlds containing propositions does not apply: this account does not restrict modal evaluation to such worlds. It evaluates q as false at w1.

The semantic construction has a narrow role. It exhibits a consistent interpretation in which the property-actualist premise is retained and the crucial export to E(w,l) fails. It does not establish that the mindless circumstance w1 is metaphysically possible. If a theory excludes w1 for independent reasons, the example is outside that theory's domain. But simply excluding it because no propositions or minds occur there would assume exactly the relevant existential constraint when using this argument to establish that constraint.

The defender can instead argue independently that the particular logical contents at issue exist necessarily, even if some other contents do not. That would bypass this semantic objection. It would still leave the type-to-particular, original-representation, hosting and root-identity questions.

## Why the distinction is not a free notational trick

Three non-theological considerations motivate taking it seriously.

First, an actual representation can describe a circumstance in which that representation does not exist. A map's evaluation against a counterfactual landscape does not require the map to be present in that landscape. This observation does not itself prove the same account for propositions, but it blocks an unargued inference from representation of a circumstance to local presence in it.

Second, modal semantics ordinarily distinguishes the context fixing an expression's content from the circumstance at which that content is evaluated. The need for such separation is not invented to evade a theistic conclusion. The question is how far the distinction reaches once propositions are identified ontologically.

Third, theories of singular content disagree about whether a proposition depends for its existence on the object it concerns. That disagreement has substantive consequences for evaluating the object's possible absence. It cannot be settled merely by inserting an existence predicate into the truth table.

The conceptualist reply remains substantial: perhaps primary truth-bearers differ essentially from contingent representations, and truth really is their locally instantiated property. Or perhaps all relevant propositions necessarily exist by their own natures. These are positions that can be defended. They must be defended as ontological or semantic claims, not treated as automatic consequences of classical modal notation.

## The uniform representation premise

The next issue is what it means to call propositions “representational.” A subject represents the world, a mental occurrence represents it, and the proposition that p is said to represent it. Must these be instances of the same property type?

Speaks's 2025 argument says no. He accepts ordinary representational talk about propositions while explaining it through their role in propositional attitudes, without identifying them with subject-involving cognitive entities. His analysis uses polysemy and the contrast between an activity and a thing suitable for use in that activity. [Speaks, “What Propositions Need Not Be,” §§3–5](https://jspeaks.site/papers/what-propositions-need-not-be.pdf)

This matters to original-content arguments because the claim that a subject's own representing is mental does not immediately classify every object described with the predicate “represents.” The strongest conceptualist response must establish a **uniformity claim**: the relevant proposition possesses original, noninstrumental representationality of the same intellectual kind as the subject-level phenomenon, rather than possessing a relational or dispositional role in possible acts of representation.

The uniformity claim is not automatically false. A purely instrumental theory may leave unexplained why exactly these abstract objects support the relevant attitudes, why they have their particular truth conditions, or how an agent grasps them. A conceptualist can press those demands. But the existence of such demands does not establish that one necessary mind is their only adequate explanation.

There is also an important limit to parsimony. If the proposed reduction assigns two different semantic jobs to “representation,” more distinctions do not automatically make it less economical. Conversely, using one word for the subject, its act, and a proposition does not demonstrate ontological unification. The comparison must ask which relations and explanatory laws each account actually requires.

## Effect on the Orthemology bridge

Deep AY already distinguishes semantic identification, constitutive semanticity and mental realization. The present analysis adds an interpretation audit inside that separation:

1. Which item is evaluated: an objective condition, a proposition, a representation type, or a particular act?
2. Is truth predicated locally at the modal world, or evaluated from a fixed context with respect to that world?
3. Does “represents” express a subject-level intellectual property, an instrumental role, or a relational property of content?
4. Is actual hosting required at every world, or only possible tokening under the correct conditions?
5. If actual hosting is required, does one rigid particular retain one essential bearer, or does a type admit different tokens and hosts?
6. Is that bearer the proposed ultimate ground, or a downstream subject supported by it?

These are not six arbitrary further flags. Each is exposed by a distinct established alternative in semantic or metaphysical theory. Their importance is that a sophisticated opponent can accept genuine content, objectivity, local minds, and ordinary actualist principles while rejecting the particular export needed by the ascent.

The corresponding positive research target can be stated without using a “God” or “Personal” predicate:

> For at least one objectively necessary content p, show that p's truth at every admitted possibility is grounded in one and the same necessarily existing, originally representational particular, and that the particular essentially inheres at the proposed fundamental ground's own locus.

If this is established under an independently defended hosting theory, a necessary intellectual bearer follows. The premise is much stronger than a complete neutral truth table. It is also stronger than saying that every possible circumstance is intelligible to some possible thinker. Whether it can be defended without presupposing a necessary thinker is the next real philosophical contest.

## Classification

This addendum is a semantic and ontological challenge to one proposed premise. It does not demonstrate atheism, the impossibility of necessary minds, global anti-conceptualism, or metaphysical parity of every R5 expansion. It leaves the positive hosting conditional intact while making its required input more exact. It also leaves source-relative divine Knowledge fully available within an independently warranted revelational framework; that framework supplies different premises from the neutral truth-at-world argument.
