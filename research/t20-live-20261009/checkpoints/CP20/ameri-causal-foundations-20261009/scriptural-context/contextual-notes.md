# Ameri comparative scriptural appendix and conclusion

## Scope and status

This is an additive contextual reading of Sami Ameri, *وقفات مع شبهة ... فمن خلق الله؟*, printed/PDF pp.191–260. It records the appendix's own argument and its relation to the preceding cosmological project. It does not independently validate biblical history, Arabic/Hebrew/Greek philology, hadith criticism, patristic doctrine, cosmology, geology, evolutionary biology, or classical Islamic doctrine. ## Main finding

The appendix reallocates apologetic authority rather than offering an independent proof of a first cause. Ameri accepts the earlier philosophical and scientific case for creation and asks whether Christian/Jewish and Islamic scripture can consistently support its use. The announced tests are compatibility with creation from nothing and with the scientific account of cosmic origins (pp.191–193). He then argues that Genesis fails both tests, whereas the Qur'an and authenticated Sunna both avoid the alleged biblical errors and positively anticipate modern discoveries (pp.238–254). The final biblical critique and Islamic concordance therefore belong to the book's religious identification and comparative-scriptural stage. They cannot be counted as another scientific confirmation of the earlier cosmology.

## Argument in its own sequence

### 1. Why insert the comparison here

Ameri begins with an imbalance in contemporary apologetics: Christian authors prominently deploy cosmological reasoning while Muslim voices are comparatively absent. He praises Craig's ability as a defender of the temporal-origination argument even as he challenges the scriptural compatibility of Craig's deployment (pp.191–193). The proposed standard initially distinguishes non-conflict from the stronger achievement of positive corroboration. Ameri promises original-language/contextual interpretation and reliance on authenticated prophetic reports (p.192). This distinction matters: his eventual Qur'anic scientific-concordance claims go considerably beyond the opening minimum of compatibility.

### 2. Genesis and creation from nothing

The lexical discussion denies that Hebrew bara by itself entails creation without prior material. Ameri compares its uses with Arabic and other Hebrew creation verbs, distinguishing divine origination, unprecedented making, and strict ex nihilo creation. He notes Craig and Copan's concession that the verb alone is insufficient, while criticizing Hugh Ross's more categorical presentation (pp.194–198). The cumulative/contextual character of the opposing argument is thus acknowledged rather than wholly omitted.

He next reconstructs Genesis against ancient Near Eastern creation traditions, especially Enuma Elish. A narrative of textual compilation and Babylonian contact supports his reading of primordial waters, unformed earth, separation, firmament, and cosmic architecture (pp.198–205). Comparative tables and two diagrams reinforce the contextual interpretation (pp.201–202,219–220). He invokes Walton's account of functional ordering, then argues that Genesis describes organizing pre-existing material rather than originating matter itself (pp.202–203).

The central philological contest is whether Genesis 1:1 is an independent opening assertion, a temporal clause, or an introductory heading. Ameri supplies modern translations and medieval Jewish precedents for the temporal reading, critiques appeals to Masoretic pointing and the Septuagint, and uses the subsequent narrative sequence to prefer his reading (pp.205–217). His argument then moves from a reading that begins with material already present to the stronger claim that the material is beginningless (pp.206,217–218). That transition is part of his inference; the two propositions are not identical.

New Testament passages are treated as insufficient to establish ex nihilo creation: Ameri distinguishes invisible material from no material in Hebrews 11:3, connects 2 Peter 3:5 with water, and argues that identifying the Logos as creator does not itself specify creation without antecedent material (pp.211–214). He invokes Hubler's historical reconstruction and Oord's alternative theology. Even if a New Testament text did teach ex nihilo creation, Ameri says that it would not determine Genesis's meaning, given different authorship; on his premises it could instead expose intrabiblical inconsistency (p.214). This is an explicit fallback argument, not merely an all-or-nothing denial of every conceivable Christian reading.

### 3. Scientific chronology and competing Christian responses

The next stage challenges the ordering and duration of biblical creation, not only the meaning of its opening verb. Ameri uses young-earth Christian critics of concordism, presenting creation-order comparisons and genealogical chronologies (pp.221–228). He distinguishes observable fossil ordering from evolutionary derivation and explicitly rejects the latter; he also corrects one borrowed table's treatment of water (pp.222–223). Accordingly, his use of these tables must not be reported as wholesale acceptance of all their scientific columns or of biological common descent.

He surveys rejection of Christianity, restricted biblical inerrancy, figurative/framework readings, literal young-earth creationism, day-age interpretation, and the gap theory (pp.228–238). His preference is polemical: he regards literalist young-earth readers as more faithful to the biblical wording, while finding their cosmology scientifically untenable. Craig's lack of a settled preferred Genesis interpretation is used against Christian cosmological apologetics (p.238). The resulting conclusion is that advocates cannot consistently claim biblical support for the philosophical/scientific creation argument. It is not a separate demonstration that an otherwise valid cosmological argument becomes invalid when a Christian presents it.

### 4. The Qur'an and Sunna as a positive alternative

Ameri first agrees that imposing the Big Bang on biblical wording would be eisegesis. He asks whether Qur'anic wording and context support a different result (p.239). His ex nihilo case is cumulative: several creation terms, no passage positing an eternal thing alongside God, no asserted influence from ancient eternal-matter cosmologies, and an asserted agreement of the Companions (pp.239–240). He does not say each creation verb individually and invariably means ex nihilo; he concedes lexical dispute and relies on the collection and context. His broad historical/consensus claims remain claims in Ameri, not independently established findings here.

The divergence argument assumes the Qur'an is deeply acquainted with biblical tradition, then asks why it omits or corrects biblical cosmological features without an available seventh-century scientific reason. This is intended as an argument for revelation, not merely as a catalogue of differences (pp.240–246). He rejects water as the material source of the whole cosmos, distinguishes water beneath the Throne, treats creation days as potentially extended periods, denies a solid firmament or attached heavenly lamps, and interprets the angels' question in Qur'an 2:30 as consistent with prehuman animals shedding blood (pp.243–245). The last is Ameri's interpretive proposal; he explicitly contrasts it with earlier explanations involving jinn.

Authenticity filtering is integral, not incidental. The extensive note on p.242 rejects the Saturday-creation report transmitted in Muslim and attributes its criticism to earlier hadith scholars, including Ibn Taymiyya. Page 243's note separately contests reports identifying water as the origin of all creation. Pages 256–257 contest the ten-generations reports and distinguish a Companion statement from a prophetic report. These are reported critical arguments; this reading has not independently settled those reports' authenticity.

Ameri's positive concordance includes primordial heat/smoke, cosmic expansion, shared initial material followed by separation, and an Earth/cosmos age ratio (pp.247–254). His six-period construction distinguishes initial material production, arrangement of the heavens, and later terrestrial formation/preparation. He identifies the final two periods with Earth's geological age and extends them to the present (pp.249–250). He acknowledges one disagreement with the Ibn Abbas sequence: contemporaneous origination of heaven and earth rather than heaven's later origination, defending his difference through his reading of the verses (pp.251–252). This concession prevents representing the complete reconstruction as simply the undisputed inherited interpretation.

The last section uses Ibn Hazm and prophetic comparisons of the Muslim community with preceding peoples to reject a tightly bounded young-world chronology. It acknowledges that the Qur'an supplies no number for humanity's age (pp.254–256). Ameri infers vast prior duration but does not derive a determinate scientific age of humanity. His discussion of the ten-generations reports combines transmitter criticism, textual criticism, and possible Israelite-source transmission, with some local uncertainty explicitly acknowledged (pp.256–257). Page 259 closes devotionally with Qur'an 6:102, linking universal divine creation to worship and guardianship; it does not supply a missing metaphysical deduction.

## Qualifications that must survive synthesis

- Ameri acknowledges major theological differences between Babylonian and biblical creation narratives. Similarity does not require total biblical fabrication; he allows an inherited true narrative supplemented with borrowed details (pp.203–204).
- He records Albright's rejection of direct borrowing even while citing Albright for the temporal translation (p.208). He acknowledges capable defenders of the traditional translation through Sasson and conditionally grants linguistic admissibility to both readings through Bandstra (pp.209,215).
- His warning against exaggerated pagan-borrowing claims, including indiscriminate Arabic repetition of Western works, applies to his own comparative undertaking (p.217 n.2). The Murdock citation is accompanied by disagreement with her general thesis (p.198 n.1).
- Hebrew yom can denote differing durations; he makes the biblical twenty-four-hour reading rest particularly on contextual morning/evening language (p.236). His Islamic extended-period reading is not based on an exclusive dictionary meaning (pp.243–244).
- The Sproul example concerns the duration of the six creation days, not directly the current age of Earth, as Ameri's own footnote specifies (p.232).
- He praises Schroeder's anti-atheist scientific argumentation while rejecting his biblical harmonization (p.235 n.1). His comparative criticism does not amount to denying every contribution by those he criticizes.
- In the late hadith discussion, Ameri marks a proposed interruption in transmission as needing examination (p.257). A summary must not turn every proposed defect into a settled historical fact.

## Use in T20 necessity and agency

This unit adds evidence about Ameri's project and intended religious conclusion. It is not an independent primary witness for Ibn Taymiyya's ontology or a completed argument connecting necessity to voluntary agency.

1. Origination, material emergence, and creation must remain distinguished. Ameri himself distinguishes initial material production from later ordering in pp.249–250, but his interpretation supplies the theological connections; the numerical cosmology does not independently establish them.
2. Creation without antecedent material does not, by that phrase alone, specify whether the whole succession of divine acts has a first member. Denying a particular eternal material coexistent with God and denying beginningless successive activity are distinct theses.
3. Creature effects are not thereby divine act-tokens. Neither the creation verbs nor the six-period reconstruction in this unit supplies the missing identification.
4. The provided project safeguard that Ibn Taymiyya permits beginningless successive activity remains in force. Nothing here licenses quietly harmonizing that position with a stronger Ameri thesis that all succession must begin. The appendix does not explicitly analyze that distinction.
5. The single encountered Ibn Taymiyya attribution in this unit concerns rejection of the Saturday-creation report (p.242 n.1). It is not a statement that divine action itself began after inactivity. If used consequentially, retrieve Ibn Taymiyya's own passage and establish the exact object of criticism.
6. The final verse's universal-creator language does not independently adjudicate the ontological status of divine acts, divine attributes, or their temporal succession.

## Local verification cautions

These cautions arose from the source read itself. They are not an expanded external refutation of the appendix.

- **Printed wording on p.194:** The second counter-thesis actually contains a negation before attributing evidence for cosmic eternity: “كلها غير دالة على أزلية الكون”. The rendered page confirms that this is not extraction noise. It conflicts with the sustained argument immediately developed and with p.238's summary. It is plausibly a print-level slip, but should not be silently corrected or quoted as an intentional reversal.
- **Argument-strength transition:** A narrative's failure to describe absolute material origination is weaker than a positive denial of it. Likewise, a functional-ordering interpretation does not by itself prove eternal matter. Ameri makes the stronger move using his cultural reconstruction; that reconstruction requires its own verification before adoption.
- **Arithmetic on pp.253–254:** The stated numerical pair is 4.5 and 13.7 billion years, and p.254 describes its agreement with one third as exact. Pure arithmetic gives 4.5/13.7 = 0.3284671533, whereas 1/3 = 0.3333333333, and 13.7/3 = 4.5666666667. The numerical match is approximate even before assessing the exegesis or the external age estimates. No current cosmological estimates have been substituted here.
- **Quotation boundary on p.256:** The text remains within the quotation attributed to Ibn Hazm begun on p.254, but states that more than fourteen hundred years have elapsed since the prophetic mission. The rendered page confirms the wording and closing quotation mark. Before treating this as verbatim Ibn Hazm, inspect *al-Fisal*, the cited 1999 edition, 1:325–326, and distinguish quotation, modernization, and editorial error. The local source alone cannot decide which occurred.
- **Hadith and evil on p.242:** In rejecting the Saturday report, Ameri makes a strong statement separating evil from God's created things and calls evil an effect of creatures. Do not convert this isolated explanatory language into a settled ontology of evil, independent creature agency, or divine act-tokens. It needs contextual theological checking if used in T20.
- **Bibliographic defect on p.271:** Entry 102 begins as *Refuting Compromise* but then repeats the *Genesis Account* particulars of entry 101. The image confirms the conflation. Prefer the specific chapter footnote when tracing a quotation and verify the original work.
