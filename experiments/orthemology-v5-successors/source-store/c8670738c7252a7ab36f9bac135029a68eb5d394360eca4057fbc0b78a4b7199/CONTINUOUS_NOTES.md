## V2 window 13, S002L139–145
The author separates the proposition that a person is a messenger, an act which commissions that messenger, and a subsequent report/attestation that the commission exists. Divine sending can be constitutive speech; public announcement can instead inform of an earlier private sending. Calling a wonder an extraordinary event does not fix the relevant comparison class. One genuine counterexample outside prophetic attestation defeats an exclusivity criterion; mere local rarity does not establish it. A sign can precede birth or follow death and can occur through an adherent. The author treats eschatological signs as confirmations of prior prediction. His Dajjal case does not endorse the apparent performer merely because the victim is revived: confession before and after, prediction, failed second killing and rival's bodily defects together determine the asserted direction of the sign. The text at L142 calls revival with these conditions 'not extraordinary' in a compressed and potentially confusing way; retain context and avoid isolating this sentence. The Qur'anic challenge is read as including assistance by all dissenters, not simply inability of local opponents. L143 explicitly asks what makes the evidence evidential rather than merely restating its description. L144 distinguishes human/jinn powers from angels acting by permission, and kinds of sign differ: revelation, prediction, transformation, multiplication. Claims that particular wonders were exclusive remain authorial historical claims, not newly established here.

## V2 window 14, S002L146–153
Relative habit varies across groups, while the author says divine wise practice itself is not broken. Extraordinary events have appropriate reasons and ends; he argues arbitrary changes without reasons would undercut judgements of relevant likeness. His criticism targets the coexistence of unrestricted voluntarism with confident ordinary expectations. This is more than an assertion that an unusual event is divine communication; identifying the relevant reason remains necessary. Etymological and semantic discussion derives prophet from informing/being informed, connecting truthful transmission with the scope of prophetic protection. New technical terms can blur matters and be treated as authorities in their own right. The author rejects the untraced report 'I am nabi, not nabiʾ', saying he has seen no chain or recognised collection supporting it. This is a useful source-critical control within devotional exposition. L153 presents immediate and inferential cognition as compatible, person-relative routes: knowledge of character, circumstances and prior teaching can supply what another reader lacks. Moses/Aaron, Khadija/Waraqa, Najashi and early believers serve as examples, not independent historical verification by this study. ## V2 window 15, S003L1–8
Continuation explains the role of prior trust and corroborating circumstances. The author categorises rejection as ignorance or resistance, but explicitly exempts the unable person who cannot reach evidence. The broad psychology of all rejecting persons exceeds what the abstract valid-proof principle alone establishes. Wisdom requires a sender demanding obedience to furnish recognisable evidence; a sign is not simply a self-asserted commission. The a-fortiori perfection argument explicitly runs from creaturely perfection without defect to the Creator, from necessary/eternal/rich to possible/originated/poor, and from ability to send human envoys to divine ability. It includes slavery and male/female hierarchy as claimed natural examples. These are the author's historical evaluative premises, not endorsements here, and their fit as universal rational intuitions is contestable. The key argument at L4 distinguishes actual recognisability from a 'sign' that fails to indicate its purported message: an inaccessible demand would defeat fair accountability. L5–8 supplies Qur'anic claims of false prophets' exposure, divine promise and prediction, and extends revelatory inimitability beyond the Qur'an to earlier scriptures. Such textual testimony is downstream of an accepted authority unless an independently intelligible rational argument is extracted. L6 repeats a clause about divine words; preserve repetition rather than silently edit.

## V2 window 16, S003L9–14
Wisdom is explicitly known first, then supports expectations about truthful and false envoys; narration of observed victory is distinguished from demonstrating why wisdom requires the outcome. Discussion of oaths, expiation and Job separates human regret/ignorance from divine foreknowledge. Voluntary agency is compatible with effects becoming necessary through complete willing power. The author rejects necessity merely by bare essence and rejects contemporaneous eternal effect as its necessary interpretation. Mental uncertainty ('may or may not') differs from external possibility and from actuality fixed by knowledge, will, promise, writing, justice and mercy. The tenfold enumeration is not ten independent observed datasets. Both rational-to-revelatory and revelatory-to-rational routes are proposed; one must avoid treating the latter as independent confirmation of its own authority. Khadija's confidence uses moral character plus providential expectations. The critique of cruelty/suffering says trials can have wise ends, but this is not an independent case-by-case solution of evil. L14's physiological and seawater accounts illustrate purposive arrangement; they are inherited physical assertions, not modern science verified here. Their argumentative role is means/ends suitability, whose force cannot depend on endorsing every illustration.

## V2 window 17, S003L15–20
L15 argues that order signifies not only knowledgeable selection but arranging this for that; removing purpose empties the very account of purposive order used to infer knowledge. Perfect knowledge and preference are then used to exclude choosing the inferior without reason. This needs scrutiny: recognising a better option and acting for it are not synonymous in imperfect agents, and the divine perfection/will bridge bears substantive weight. L16 tests a deliberately total reversal, rewarding corrupt liars eternally and humiliating truthful good people eternally. It is stronger than asserting every local bad outcome refutes wisdom. L17–19 critiques the ability-based attestation argument: if identical external wonders are possible for liars, absence of their performance is not incapacity. Actual signhood requires more than undifferentiated power. Author retains knowledge that opponents possess while rejecting its compatibility with their proposed foundations. No general dismissal of every opponent's truth follows. L20 credits a correct goal in both opposed schools while rejecting their purported necessary means; Ghazali's appeal to rich implicit cumulative indications is partly approved, though the author judges his model of prophecy too narrow and closer to dreams/skills.

## V2 window 18, S003L21–28
Theological polemic continues; the author sees rival schools as useful critics of one another and does not label them all total rejecters. Moral/providential distinction between truthful and false envoys is claimed across evidence and lasting support. L25 makes the crucial distinction: an effect can indicate an originator independently of anyone intending this evidential use; speech, gestures, roadmarks and entrusted rings signify through intentional designation. Prior explicit convention is not always required, but intention is. L26 distinguishes impossible 'true evidence of a falsehood' from a possible similar phenomenon that is not true evidence. The study must not mistake the first definitional incompatibility for the historical identification of a token as genuine evidence. False apparent signs and interpretation error remain possible. L27 plainly rejects the inference that failing actually to attest destroys ability to attest. L28 says a proposed invariant association would need establishment, especially where it is intentional rather than intrinsic. Purpose/ability/performance are not interchangeable. The text's further broad necessity claims rest on its wisdom and willing framework, not on a token's mere causal provenance.

## V2 window 19, S003L29–35
Wisdom discussion concludes, then divine customary action is presented as an inductive/analogical route shared even by opponents denying intrinsic purposiveness. Solar cycles, food, medicine and linguistic competence illustrate learning stable practices; histories of prophets are claimed to exhibit an analogous pattern. Whether the historical selection is representative is a substantive question, not answered by the ordinary examples alone. The Qur'anic narrative is extensively quoted to connect resistance, delay, rescue and judgement. L32 openly describes reasoning from past relevant cases to future ones; not every temporal defeat is final failure, and endurance is part of the expected pattern. L33–35 turns to daʾb, habit, effort and semantic variation, comparing lexicographers and exegetes. The author disagrees explicitly with Ibn Atiyya over celestial voluntary obedience, and with al-Zajjaj's preferred sense; these are distinct quoted and authorial voices. The habit may denote the subjects' behaviour and the divine response to it. L35's compressed denial and affirmation of 'comparison in recompense' needs its subsequent explanation, not an extracted contradiction claim.

## V2 window 20, S003L36–43
The routine's asserted constancy completes. Logical entailment is then rehearsed across evidence of prophecy, ownership, knowledge and justice. Important scope: a sign entails the relevant prophetic truth, not that evidence must occupy the prophet's body, time or place. A testimony about the past can be current evidence. The author explicitly says the present purpose concerns the genus of prophetic signs, not an individual prophet or particular proof (L40). This controls what the book itself establishes historically. Prophecy combines an intrinsic qualification in the prophet and a relation to divine commission, rather than only one of those descriptions. The electronic text repeats its relational account; preserve it. First-person knowledge need not always be fully articulable, but that does not automatically transfer its warrant to an external assessor. Signs associated with faithful successors may support the earlier commission. L42–43 rejects a restrictive theory of agency confining everything a power acts on to its own subject; ordinary language calls a person a builder or maker through mediated actions. Authorial reports of jinn, harmful spells and uncommon travel are classified as powers of created agents, not automatically evidence of prophecy.

## V2 window 21, S003L44–49
The distinction turns on the event's entire purpose and character, not travel speed alone. The night journey's claimed disclosures and meeting distinguish it, in the author's account, from rapid transport that evades religious duties. Spectacular performance can lower rather than increase spiritual standing. L45 offers a relational causal criterion: means used and end served distinguish superficially similar walking-on-water reports. The threefold use of jinn is compared with human cooperation: commanded righteous service, ordinary compensated service, and wrongful service. This is a historical theological account of invisible agents, not verified incident reports or practical medical guidance. Discussion includes whether jinn have prophets, entry into paradise, and ranks of prophet-kings versus servant-messengers. Solomon's exceptional unrestricted command differs from ordinary negotiated cooperation, domination or exchange. The author asserts many possessions, injuries and counterfeits; the fact that this treatise asserts them does not independently authenticate each one. His broader lesson is that possession of a striking capacity does not settle moral authority or commission.

## V2 window 22, S003L50–55
'Unseen' is distinguished as relative to a knower versus knowledge exclusively divine. The author offers theological criteria for spirits' access to concealed information, and contrasts Abraham/Abu Muslim's deliverance with rival experiences of fire or blows. His own claims of witnessing or administering beatings are authorial historical assertions; this study neither verifies their diagnoses nor recommends their practice. He qualifies claims about devils' weakness during Ramadan rather than absolute inactivity. Denial of all strange occurrences and automatic elevation of their performers are both attacked. L53 especially explains why denying an event someone has strongly witnessed can wrongly destroy credibility, leading that person to dismiss other true criticism. The right dispute may concern origin, purpose and interpretation. L54 distinguishes not knowing a difference from knowing that no difference exists. L55 rejects a bare numerical threshold for reports: informants' properties, contextual factors and the recipient's knowledge affect warrant. Necessary and inferential knowledge can overlap after relevant grounds are grasped. This is not permission to call every felt certainty knowledge; the text still demands an objective difference between truth-conducive and non-conducive conditions.

## V2 window 23, S003L56–63
The author contrasts prophetic cognition with philosophical psychic-power models and treats some theologians' suspension about serviced soothsayers as insufficiently attentive to their own scriptures. Kahanah has different social meaning across languages/communities; the priestly name among scriptural communities does not carry precisely Arabic soothsayer stigma. L58 makes a useful textual-scope point: 'Torah' may denote the broad pre-Gospel scriptural corpus or legal tradition, so attribution need not be to the Pentateuch narrowly. This is not a modern textual identification of the quoted passage. Counterfeit appearances and imitated voices create misidentification risks, including visions of a dead teacher or claimed prophet. Remote communication is represented as mediated, not literally infinite human vocal reach. The same abstract event class can have different agents and normative meaning. L63 rejects a crucial ambiguity: 'only God can do it' interpreted as universal dependence describes all events and therefore cannot by itself discriminate prophetic signs.

## V2 window 24, S003L64–69
The author expressly permits angelic mediation and causes in signs, rejecting a stipulation that God must produce them without any cause. Gabriel's blowing and Mary's conception, angelic military support and ordinary gestation are examples. L65 has an abrupt transition from usual angelic mediation to Qur'anic order of monotheism/prophecy/resurrection; preserve pending print control. L66–67 says creatures have a genuine contribution without independence: Gabriel can blow, Jesus shape clay, fighters fight; life or victory is not thereby solely theirs. Differentiating token authorship from subordinate causation is essential, but these doctrinal examples are not themselves a fresh demonstration of universal numerical source uniqueness. The challenge is to bring comparable revelation, whether originating or transmitting; the author means to include all available routes. He offers more than ten discriminators between prophets and rival performers: truth, justice, relevant comparators, non-acquired commission and so on. L68 makes a conditional concession to acquired-prophecy theories: even on them a truthful/righteous character is required, so the concession does not endorse the theory. L69 distinguishes intentional deceit from a factually false assertion made in culpable ignorance, with legal examples. The sectarian generalisation about lying is the author's polemic, not a conclusion authenticated here.

## V2 window 25, S003L70–78
The discriminator list concludes with resistance to opposition, broad relevant comparison class, unique divine initiative, continuity with earlier prophetic teaching, and fit with rightly functioning perception/reason. Signs of different faithful agents can strengthen each other rather than function as competitive domination. Crucially, L71 says God can answer a distressed unbeliever's prayer; therefore an answered request alone is not a certificate of the requester's religion or status. Ordinary saintly gifts do not bear all the weight of establishing prophecy and may serve honour as well as indication. Prior prophets supply a comparison class and constraint, but availability and authenticity of that class remain epistemic issues for an external reader. The final pages reject sensory distortion and conceptual reification in rival accounts, returning to the long middle discussion of atoms, substances and real generation.
