## T8 «مسألة في مقتل الحسين وحكم يزيد», PDF 253–270

253 title; 254 blank.255 opens the question: al-Ḥusayn's murder, killer, Yazīd, alleged captive family/head in Damascus, Muʿāwiya and earlier caliphs. The answer calls ʿUthmān, ʿAlī and al-Ḥusayn unjustly killed martyrs and cites promised-Paradise reports.255–257 expand Companion merits, especially Abū Bakr (friendship, mosque door, succession-related reports, leading prayer, ʿUmar's praise), the six-person shūrā, Badr and the tree-pledge. These establish the author's evaluative framework rather than directly answering every historical question yet.258 uses Ḥāṭib's wrongdoing together with promised salvation to resist treating a grave mistake as erasing earlier merit; it turns to al-Ḥasan/al-Ḥusayn, cloak report and al-Ḥasan's greater reconciliation virtue.

259 continues al-Ḥasan's reconciliation and Usāma's reluctance to civil war. Al-Ḥusayn is explicitly a wronged martyr and killers transgressors. The application of a report about splitting a unified community to justify his death is rejected: he sought to desist, return or go elsewhere, so refusal to accept that is decisive.260 presents the author's account of Iraqi invitations, warnings from Ibn ʿAbbās/Ibn ʿUmar/Abū Bakr ibn ʿAbd al-Raḥmān, Muslim ibn ʿAqīl's death, failed return and the killing. It places head-striking before Ibn Ziyād in Iraq, and rejects transfer/parading-in-Syria versions. The editor specifically says he did not find the cited Abū Barza report in the *Musnad*: apparatus qualifies authorial citation certainty.

261 rejects weak Yazīd head-striking reports, describes his family's mourning and return of the survivors, and differentiates ordering resistance to al-Ḥusayn from ordering his killing. Nevertheless it condemns Yazīd's failure to punish killers and the sack of Medina. Aḥmad's refusal to transmit from/praise Yazīd and reluctance to curse particular persons are given together. Yazīd is neither elevated to a saint nor declared outside Islam, but a Muslim king with good and bad. The first Constantinople army report is cited; modern footnote corrects narrator attribution from Ibn ʿUmar to Umm Ḥarām.

262 distinguishes Yazīd ibn Muʿāwiya from his Companion uncle Yazīd ibn Abī Sufyān. Denies Muslim enslavement of Hāshimites in Yazīd's reign; contains broader contested historical/sectarian assertions about Baghdad and Ibn al-ʿAlqamī which are recorded as this author's polemic, not independently established here. Turns to ʿUthmān's nonresistance.263 declares ʿAlī innocent of ʿUthmān's killing/approval, describes accusations and mistrust, and says ʿAlī, Ṭalḥa and al-Zubayr did not originally intend the ensuing battle. Muʿāwiya's camp sought justice for ʿUthmān rather than originally claiming equal/superior succession entitlement.264 explains ʿAlī's inability to punish amid dispersal and powerful factions, and affirms his camp as nearer right. It distinguishes that comparative judgment from whether to initiate fighting rebels: the latter is disputed; the majority-position account favours waiting until they attack, and says refraining would have been better, while affirming ʿAlī as rightly guided fourth caliph.

265 reads Q49: 9–10 as reconciliation first, then restraint of aggression. Wrongful fighting under sincere interpretive error is distinguished from knowingly defiant unbelief/immorality; Usāma's killing a professed convert illustrates rebuke without the stated retaliatory penalties.266 adds Khālid/Banū Jadhīma and Mālik ibn Nuwayra to the author's error/excuse discussion, contrasting blame for an act with wholesale disqualification of its agent. Praise of al-Ḥasan is praise for reconciliation.267 sharply separates commanded fighting of Khārijites at Nahrawān from the civil-war fighting whose avoidance was better, listing notable abstainers and reports recommending withdrawal.268 quotes withdrawal/fitna reports and a prayer granted against total external destruction/famine but not internal fighting; a modern note says a report attributed to both Ṣaḥīḥs was not found with that wording in Muslim.269 finishes with the enduring victorious group: the whole community is not wholly overcome, though groups may be.270 blank.

### T8 appraisal
The text's moral distinctions are not simple partisan approval/disapproval: wrongful killing, degrees of comparative right, lack of capacity, interpretive excuse, unpunished culpability, and preferable nonparticipation are separated. Claims about historical acts and chains remain source claims. Neither an agent's good antecedents nor a sincere interpretation establishes that every resulting act is good. Conversely blame for an act need not erase all other virtues. The lack-of-capacity explanation at 264 concerns practical politics, not a metaphysical independence theorem. Modern critical notes visibly correct/qualify authorial citations. The first-section reader's report of a 728 copy is not visible in the body ending 269 and has not been independently verified here.

## T9 «مسألة في الاستغفار», PDF 271–280
271 title; 272 blank.273 starts mid-source with al-Walīd asking al-Awzāʿī what forgiveness-seeking means. The editor explicitly says «من هنا تبدأ القطعة الموجودة من الأصل», so the beginning is lost. Repeated istighfār after prayer is compared to repeated glorification within it; reports of seventy/hundred daily repetitions and concluding deeds with forgiveness follow.274 pairs tawḥīd (all positive religious good) with istighfār (removal of evil/punishment), and traces forgiveness requests through prayer's stages. The Satan story is qualified by the editor as weak, indeed fabricated, rather than treated as sound evidence.275 adds prostration formulas and emphasises the Prophet's seeking forgiveness for believers; forgiveness is faith-conditioned.276 distinguishes this from worldly protection, provision and general guidance, which can reach nonbelievers; Qurʾānic and prophetic cases of forbidden forgiveness requests for deceased polytheists/hypocrites are discussed.

277 distinguishes praying for another's worldly welfare/guidance from guaranteeing final salvation; sincere personal repentance can benefit a person formerly in unbelief, but another person's tawḥīd cannot substitute for one's own. Anchor: «لا ينجيه إلا توحيد نفسه». Istighfār connected to repentance from shirk and faith cannot be performed by proxy in that foundational sense, even though other deeds/prayers may be gifted or performed for another (278).278 makes repentance/forgiveness the culmination of human religious life because human sin is pervasive; even sins outweighed by merits can affect rank though not entail punishment. Muḥammad's prior forgiveness explains the distinctive intercession position, unlike those first occupied with themselves.279 uses Abraham's hope for forgiveness on Judgment Day and the intercession narrative, then concludes repeated istighfār's special importance.280 blank.

### T9 appraisal
The significant distinction is personal foundational qualification versus another's beneficial prayer/action. It does not deny every mediated benefit. “All good/all evil” is the author's devotional-theological compression, not a generalised causal algebra. The lost opening limits reconstruction of the original question. Modern hadith grading is kept distinct from the authorial use of a report.

## T10 «مسائل في الصلاة», PDF 281–298
281 title; 282 blank.283 begins mid-sentence; editor again explicitly marks the start of the surviving fragment. It links prayer's acts and utterances (brief rest sitting as exception), then asks whether vocal/silent recitation is obligatory or customary. Familiar Mālik/Aḥmad positions make them Sunnah, with disagreement reported. Silent opening invocation, bowing/prostration supplications and tashahhud are normal; solo reciter, refuge formula, basmala and amen have differing reported practices.284 adduces quiet-prayer reports and incidental loudness: ʿUmar's teaching opening invocation, some Companions' audible basmala, occasional audible verses in noon/afternoon, Ibn ʿAbbās's audible funeral Fātiḥa.

285 states why occasional audibility can be good: teaching, waking attention, assisting recitation, removing difficulty, eliciting reverence/tears or benefitting followers. It must not become an invariant practice like obligatory Fātiḥa/bowing. A follower's spontaneous praise approved by the Prophet shows room for wording within a prescribed class even without prior instruction of that exact wording.286–287 develop the evidential inference from companions reporting what they heard of the Prophet's openings/bowing/rising/prostrating to occasional audibility, while noting that in another report the Prophet explicitly explained afterward what he had said. Ḥudhayfa's night prayer, responses to mercy/punishment verses, Ibn Abī Awfā's praise after bowing, and ʿĀʾisha's tasbīḥ/forgiveness formulas supply cases.288 adds ʿĀʾisha hearing prostration supplication and permits relaying takbīr if an imam's voice cannot reach; the imam's normal takbīr is audible, evidenced by ordered coordination.289 finishes this and opens recitation length.

289 argues sunset prayer is normally promptly performed and shorter in recitation, though longer reported recitations (al-Aʿrāf, al-Mursalāt, al-Ṭūr) occur; dawn is normally longest.290 lists variable dawn recitations, travel shortening and actual shortening for an infant's cry: “lightening” is relative, not necessarily minimal verses.291 discusses eleven/thirteen night units, possibly explained by initial light units; a “the shaykh said” transition corrects those preferring all regular daytime supererogatory prayers over night prayer. Night prayer is best generally, while witr and two dawn units are best regular attachments. These were retained in travel, unlike the usual daytime rawātib.292 concludes the reported full daily count and begins Qurʾānic hearing/prostrating/weeping.

292–294 resist restricting broad praise of prostrating on hearing revelation to the few formal sajdat-al-tilāwa verses. Such texts address obligatory prayer's combined reading/hearing and bodily prostration, even while supporting recitation prostration.293 contrasts reading as greatest verbal pillar and prostration as greatest bodily pillar; 294 prefers balanced relation of long reading and many prostrations to an absolute superiority of one. Scripture's context of successors neglecting prayer helps identify its scope.

295–296 contain explicit lexical/contextual analysis: rukūʿ and sujūd can include one another when a term occurs alone but distinguish acts when conjoined, analogous to faqīr/miskīn. Bowed entry into a gate cannot mean full forehead-on-ground posture; a leaning palm can be described as “prostrating”; David's described rukūʿ is actual prostration, with bending as its beginning and grounding as its increase. A disputed practical consequence is whether the prayer's bowing/prostration can satisfy a recitation-prostration at a chapter ending. These are semantic and legal claims with examples, not mere word identity.297 moves from paired prayer components to prayer coupled with zakāt, patience and jihad, then says permanent dawn qunūt is not the Prophet's normal established practice.298 distinguishes fixed qunūt, abrogated practice, and temporary calamity-qunūt; the last is defended. Examples include captives, killed reciters, ʿUmar's armies and Aḥmad's tribulation. Questions remain about civil-war parties praying for mutual victory versus unity. Reported qunūt often occurs where direct armed relief was unavailable, and is described as a substitute for personally fighting, not as an abstract equation of causes.

### T10 appraisal
The most distinctive findings are the norm/exception relation of quiet and audible invocation, functional reasons for exceptions without making them invariant, and semantic inclusion under isolation versus differentiation under conjunction (295–296). The fragment lacks its beginning; no reconstruction is attempted. Liturgical coordination and relay of voice 288 are practical cooperation, but carry no independent thesis of identical awareness or causally independent action. The repeated distinction between usual practice and exceptional occurrence should prevent cherry-picking one hadith example as an unrestricted rule.

## T11 «فصل في الصلاة الوسطى», PDF 299–318
299 title; 300 blank.301 identifies the middle prayer as ʿaṣr through explicit reports, then its special warnings and virtues.302 adds solemn oaths after ʿaṣr and reasons for guarding it: unlike other prayer starts its beginning lacks a sharply salient sensory marker, and its time cannot simply be pushed into the next legal period.303 adds shortness of interval and competing daily work, sleep/forgetfulness cases, and the Banū Qurayẓa instruction with differing Companion responses; severe-fear postponement versus adjusted on-time prayer is disputed.

304 sets out two positions: choice between proper-form delay and on-time adapted prayer, or obligation of adapted on-time performance, with earlier postponement superseded. Doing both duties (prayer and combat) as circumstances permit is preferred to forfeiting one. Joining prayers is still praying within legally extended time. The analogous pilgrim near the ʿArafa deadline should preserve both prayer and standing through adapted performance, rather than sacrifice one.305 critically reconsiders an explanation the author himself had earlier used: the Banū Qurayẓa story should not too quickly be made an unrestricted precedent approving mutually opposed ijtihāds, because correctness and excused error are different. It returns to ʿaṣr, then discusses dawn as the next most plausible “middle” candidate.

306–307 compare dawn/afternoon: protected endpoints, non-postponability across sunrise/sunset, and one-unit-before-boundary reports. “Day” can begin at dawn for fasting or sunrise for other reckoning, so “half-day” does not automatically mean the midpoint of dawn-to-sunset. Dawn has a night/day overlap of characteristics, yet counts among daytime prayers.308 uses finger-order analogy to put dawn first and afternoon in the middle, preferring this prayer ordering over starting with noon; noon-first follows Gabriel's inaugural teaching event, not necessarily the abiding daily order.309 distinguishes the Companions' conventional name “the first” for noon from a divinely instituted name, and proposes two explanations for Gabriel beginning with noon (announcement after the night journey or an earlier stage before later completion of prayer forms).310 concludes dawn/afternoon share angel shifts and post-prayer prohibitions, while dawn has long reading and is never joined to another prayer.

311 begins a related section on simultaneous prayer and jihad. Time may be determined by a temporal interval or an act such as the iqāma: once the congregation begins, only that prayer should occupy its specified performance. Remembering a missed prayer then creates sequencing conflicts; three reported views prioritize the missed, prioritize the current to save its time, or repeat the current after the missed.312–313 assemble Qurʾānic and prophetic commands to preserve times, including praying on time even if a ruler delays and later joining him as supererogatory prayer.314 distinguishes immediate/deferred and individual/collective jihad duties, with historical imam commanding prayer and war, and book/justice/iron as complementary social means.315 says entering an undertaking can make completion binding; prayer and steadfastness are both commanded, and remembering God remains prayer's purpose.316 explicitly classifies duties by time (expanded/determined), act (specified/optional), and agent (individual/collective). It gives two scenarios of simultaneous duty: guarding without actual close combat permits fear-prayer variants, even departures from ordinary coordinated congregation.

317 contrasts immediate fighting, called sword-contact prayer, and reports three rulings: combine adapted prayer with combat (familiar Aḥmad and Shāfiʿī view), choice to advance/postpone (another Aḥmad report), or delay on the outward command (some raʾy/Ẓāhirī jurists). A final section announces three kinds of threats during prayer, humans/jinn/animals, and starts the human-threat case.318 ends abruptly mid-report, with editor's «انتهى ما في الأصل». The conclusion and the promised treatment of all three threats are not extant here and must not be reconstructed.

### T11 appraisal
The argument is not that urgency erases ritual duties: it redefines feasible form within the time while coordinating obligations. Vocabulary about specified/expanded time and personal/collective duty is legally discriminating. The explicit self-questioning 305 is important evidence of reflective revision, not a general declaration that every interpretation is equally correct. The end is demonstrably lost 318; no reading credit extends beyond the surviving fragment.

## T12 «فصل في المواقيت والجمع بين الصلاتين», PDF 319–368

### 319–330: number, form, cause and occasion
319 title; 320 blank.321 begins with prayer at appointed times and five acts/fifty in reward. Travel prayer's two units are treated as its original full obligation, so a separate intention to shorten is not required on the preferred view; divergent school views are acknowledged.322 sharply separates shortening from joining: the Prophet's travel shortening is constant, while joining is conditional; alleged habitual four-unit travel practice is rejected and transmitted wording about ʿĀʾisha criticized.323–324 distinguish Meccan pilgrims at ʿArafa/Muzdalifa from Meccan residents praying behind a visitor in Mecca: relocation itself matters, not mere ritual status. Three school positions on local pilgrims' shortening/joining are compared. The preferred account makes shortening attach to travel, joining to need or a preponderant legally recognised benefit.

325–326 address apparent Sunnah/Qurʾān tension. Q4: 101 says “shorten” without specifying only number; fear can alter form, while two units for a traveler remain full. An Ibn ʿUmar wording attributed to Muslim is corrected by the editor 325 as found in ʿAbd ibn Ḥumayd, not Muslim. Misreadings of tayammum, pilgrimage tamattuʿ and completion are used as analogous misunderstandings.326 contains an explicit ellipsis whose footnote says words in the original are unclear; it is not filled here.327 continues pilgrimage release/re-entry and then divorced-pregnant maintenance: the author locates maintenance in the child rather than continuing marital entitlement. This is a historical legal analogy, not advice about a present case.

328 distinguishes reduction of acts/pillars because of fear from travel's numerical rule; common circumstances explain why Scripture names travel/fear together without excluding rare resident cases.329 distinguishes legal classification/typical rationale from a necessity that every individual instance manifest hardship. Travel remains the classification even if its wisdom varies across cases. Ritual status is not the operative property for numerical shortening, because it precedes and outlasts pilgrimage and mere entering iḥrām in Mecca does not authorise it.330 explicitly warns against attaching a ruling to a nonoperative description. Examples concern historical marital consent, divorce, khulʿ and legal formulae: the rule follows the named transaction's meaning, not whether its sentence has conditional syntax. Joining is then introduced as not attached to bare travel or bare pilgrimage; ʿArafa's prolonged standing and the urgent route to Muzdalifa supply actual need/benefit.

### 331–336: cases and the act/reward distinction
331 lists joining during urgent travel, Ibn ʿAbbās's Medinan seven/eight units to avoid hardship, Tabūk, and rain. Companion non-objection to Medinan rain-joining is compared with their actual objections to other gubernatorial ritual innovations.332 gives irregular bleeding as a choice between separate prayers with ablution and paired prayers with fuller washing. The fuller purification can make joining preferable; uncertain purification status is compared with retaining known prior purity while allowing a prudent renewal.333 distinguishes admissibility of sitting/lying and proportional reward from reward preserved by habitual intention under illness/travel. Not every ill traveler automatically gains every healthy person's deeds: the habitual deed and genuine intention matter. A modern footnote notes that the complete quoted wording on lying prayer is not in the cited Muslim narration.

334 extends this to arriving at congregation too late and to people prevented from accompanying a campaign; actual participation and reward granted for sincere frustrated intention are different. The four-person wealth/knowledge report gives parallel rewards or burdens for genuine intentions under unequal means.335 is explicit: «فالثواب الذي يكتب بالنية غير الثواب المستحق بنفس العمل». A general comparison of rewards does not by itself settle legal permission or validity: obligatory prayer standing requires ability; separate proof allows optional sitting and travel prayer on a mount. Likewise congregational superiority alone does not prove an unexcused solitary prayer sufficient.336 compares fuller purification, permissible place and congregation as perfections, while distinguishing obligatory elements from recommended ones. ʿArafa's joined prayer is then justified by preserving uninterrupted standing; praying ʿaṣr separately in its own time would still be valid but would forgo the preferable arrangement.

### 337–348: normal times, excused times, and textual consistency
337 surveys Mālik/Shāfiʿī/Aḥmad on advance/deferred joining, long/short travel, rain, illness and irregular bleeding; 338 quotes the longer bleeding reports then names the organizing distinction: time of ease/choice and time of need/excuse.339 compares the normal five times and disputed shadow/twilight limits; 340 favours afternoon from equal shadow to yellowing over later starts, and identifies three excused intervals: noon-to-sunset, sunset-to-dawn, dawn-to-sunrise.341 interprets “caught one unit before sunset” as valid eventual capture, not permission for an able person deliberately to delay; the hypocrite's last-minute hurried prayer is still censured.342 uses recovered eligibility (purity after menstruation, waking, recovering reason) to support paired obligations in common time. **Apparatus warning:** where the body says purity “before sunrise” for sunset/night prayers, the editor proposes “before dawn”; retain the distinction rather than silently normalise.

343 again distinguishes day from dawn versus day from sunrise and reads the two ends of day plus night as three broad intervals, with dawn alone neither joined nor shortened.344 adduces privacy times and other verse formulations; 345–346 challenge an inconsistent appeal to Qurʾānic generality: opponents accept comparatively general wording for opening dhikr, bowing and recitation against specific Sunna reports, but deny general/shared prayer-time wording despite positive joining reports. The author compares evidential strengths rather than making Qurʾān and Sunnah incompatible.

347 distinguishes sinful delay into a still-valid common time from abandoning day prayer into night. Rulers criticized for delay still performed recognizable valid prayers, though culpably late; their practice was not substituting arbitrary recitation, omitting takbīr, or moving all daytime prayer after sunset.348 returns to the three excused times and insists the second half of night, like the period after afternoon yellowing, is not a chosen normal delay for an able person. Recovered eligibility before the final endpoint requires the associated pair according to the cited Companions and majority.

### 349–360: intention, contiguity, and qualitative priority
349 says no separate advance intention-to-join is needed when the second prayer is in a time legally available to that person. Nor were followers at ʿArafa instructed beforehand to intend joining.350 supplies the principle: if it is a permitted prayer-time the act is valid whether intended as joining or not; if not, mere intention cannot make it permitted. Requiring entrants to know an imam's prior joining intention would obstruct the concession and congregation. The Dhu-l-Yadayn case is similarly used against a necessary separate shortening intention.

351 contests obligatory immediate contiguity: joining denotes placement in one of the pair's specific times, not necessarily no pause. Earlier permission cannot rationally exclude a somewhat later position closer to the second prayer's usual time; Muzdalifa's stopping/unloading supplies an interval example. The editor records a marginal correction to one verb.352 distinguishes two excuses: real hardship, and preserving a superior worship benefit (continuous ʿArafa standing). It then argues joining before demanding military duty is preferable to missing the time or necessarily fragmentary prayer.353 limits the trench-delay precedent: perhaps no prior opportunity to join, or later supersession; it is no warrant to miss an available time.354 compares complete joined prayer with diminished ordinary-time performance, since common time remains legally time, and completing the prayer itself can outweigh insistence on its usual particular interval.

355 refines priority: obligatory prayer completeness cannot be sacrificed just to preserve recommended uninterrupted invocation. **Textual warning:** the body here prints an apparently contrary “لا يجوز التكميل”; its marginal note says a lacuna is probable and proposes «ترك التكميل». The intended contrast is visible in context, but the correction remains an explicit conjecture, not a silently restored reading. Joined full washing, standing or congregation can be preferable to lesser form at the specific time.356–357 test harder cases: bathhouse/prohibited place, inability to wash, unclothed, sick or fearful. Pray in available form before the final time ends rather than wait for ideal form later; if legally joined prayer outside the prohibited place is possible, that is preferable. The bathhouse example is practical ritual jurisprudence, not a current medical prescription.

358 reports disagreement in edge cases requiring preparation beyond the remaining time (rope for water, sequential shared garment, space to stand on a boat, learning direction, sewing cover). It returns to the preferred ranking of legally joined tayammum prayer over prayer in an intrinsically prohibited setting.359 responds to arithmetic of defects: two apparent deficiencies do not automatically make an act worse than one; shared time is itself permissible for a reason, tayammum is a prescribed substitute, whereas a prohibited location is allowable only from necessity.360 says tayammum where appropriate serves the functions of water purification, including major impurity, optional/obligatory worship and leading a water-purified congregation. Distinct source of legal permission matters more than counting surface departures.

### 361–368: conclusion and boundary
361 says time is more emphatic than congregation, so one prays alone in time rather than with a congregation after the final time; joining itself is still within time and requires need or a prevailing benefit.362 argues from ʿArafa as a major precedent even for a recommended completion, not solely strict necessity.363 adds that if joining followed one precisely bounded cause just as travel shortening does, revelation would have attached it accordingly; actual cases instead support need/recognised benefit.364 preserves the default: every prayer belongs in its own time, with joining permitted or preferable for a reason, fuller purification, sure direction, covering or congregation. Even a resident in the mosque may join the rain congregation under the preferred view because the communal benefit applies.

365 restates five ordinary versus three excused times, and connects excuses for congregation with excuses for precise separate times. Its footnote explicitly marks an obscure expression «كذا في النسختين، وفي العبارة غموض»; this is evidence that the editor reports two copies at that locus, not proof they are independent.366 rejects missed prayer as a substitute for permissible joining, contrasting majority permission-to-join/no-missing with restrictive joining approaches.367 concludes that allowing time forfeiture and forbidding joining combines two weak principles. Even within the broader final endpoint, deliberate afternoon delay to yellowing or night prayer to the second half needs necessity; earlier tayammum can be better than waiting for water into that emergency interval. Ends «والله أعلم».368 blank.

### T12 appraisal
This is a sustained legal argument, with several distinctions relevant methodologically but not new metaphysical claims: act versus intention-based reward 335; operative description versus incidental accompaniment 329–330; legal availability independent of separately intending that label 349–350; quality/type of departure rather than arithmetic counting of “defects”356–360; normal and excused standards with the final boundary still preserved. “Perfection” concerns completing legally required prayer features and justified tradeoffs, not an unrestricted ontological source-perfection predicate. The explicit uncertainties 326,342,355,365 must remain visible. The supposed two-copy genealogy is not settled by this reading.

## T13 «مسألة في رجل فقير وعليه دين، هل لأخيه الغني دفع الزكاة إليه؟», PDF 369–374
369 title; 370 blank.371 asks about a full brother who is poor and indebted and advance zakāt for a year or two. The answer permits giving the amount to which someone in his condition is entitled and permits advance zakāt; equally needy kin have the additional good of kinship. It then addresses the objection that obligatory family support already renders him supplied. Five responses span 371–374: the giver may have no surplus for support; support is conditional on unmet need and inability to obtain resources; another obligor's refusal does not invalidate zakāt reception; the poor recipient and wealthy relative have choices among available lawful provision, and a mutually accepted zakāt transfer need not be barred; distribution through imam/administrator would be valid, so personal administration does not inherently change entitlement.

373 is a crucial qualification: zakāt cannot simply shield the giver's own customary dependent expenditure, even where that regular support is voluntarily undertaken rather than legally imposed. Nor should kin preference displace someone with greater need. Preference for an equally needy relative is different from favoritism.374 treats zakāt as owed to God and delivered to entitled recipients through the owner as distributor; recipients owe no counterpayment. Abū Ṭalḥa's charity to near kin supplies a precedent. Ends «والله سبحانه أعلم».

Appraisal: poverty/kinship, actual access to support, habitual dependence, gift choice and administrator identity are distinct. This is not a blanket license to replace obligatory support with zakāt, nor a claim that all linked financial obligations duplicate one another.

## T14 «مسألة في التسمية على ذكاة الذبيحة وذكاة الصيد», PDF 375–390
375 title; 376 blank.377 presents five reported Aḥmad positions via Abū al-Khaṭṭāb: naming always required, waived for forgetfulness, different for slaughter/hunt, arrow versus dog/hunting feline (الفهد), and Shāfiʿī permissibility without naming. Transmitter phrases «قال تقي الدين» introduce the author's preference: no eating when naming was omitted, with direct scriptural convergence claimed.378 quotes relevant Qurʾānic passages on naming, prohibited offerings, and food; 379–380 collect ʿAdī ibn Ḥātim variants concerning trained dogs, blade versus blunt projectile, another dog mixed in, prey found later, and drowning. These are read as historical hunting law, not instructions to harm animals here.

381–382 give a sustained grammatical argument: the pronoun in «واذكروا اسم الله عليه» returns to the captured thing, not merely an implicit verbal noun “eating.” Returning a pronoun to a noun inferred from a verb is possible where no appropriate explicit noun precedes, but an explicit nearer antecedent is preferred. Naming while dispatching the dog is naming over the prey at capture; literal proximity to the prey then is not required because the hunter can be far away.382–383 enumerate commands and conditions in the hadith.383 says the unmentioned other dog blocks reliable permission where one cannot tell which killed; naming is treated as a condition and its absence a prohibiting factor. This should not be recast as a theorem that two dogs could not jointly kill: the issue is lawful authorisation and uncertain provenance. Hunting excitement could cause forgetfulness, so the author argues slaughter, performed more deliberately, is not more readily exempt.

384 adduces Abū Thaʿlaba on bow/trained versus untrained dog and vessels of scriptural communities, arguing no exception for inadvertent omission is stated.385 answers the attempted differentiation that ordinary slaughter is fully voluntary while hunting is an incomplete necessity-concession, or that arrow is human action but hound is animal action: legitimate arrow/hound slaughter is legally complete relative to capacity, just as sick/fearful prayer discharges the duty. Mother/fetus slaughter is another capacity-based example. This is a narrow legal completeness argument, not an unrestricted claim of causal self-sufficiency.386 adds Rāfiʿ ibn Khadīj's combined blood-flow/naming conditions and the food-of-jinn report; 387 extends the latter a fortiori, then treats the crucial report about meat brought by recent converts.

387–388 distinguish **actual condition** from **individual knowledge of its satisfaction**. A buyer need not independently verify each Muslim/scriptural slaughterer's naming, just as a market buyer may presume a seller's proper title/agency; if that knowledge were required ordinary meat consumption would become impracticable. The permission to name and eat despite not knowing is therefore no permission to eat when one knows naming was omitted.388 states that ignorance of a slaughterer's actual conduct is excused under a presumption of regularity; the actual rule is not erased. Compares throat-location requirements and humane slaughter, claiming stronger naming evidence.389 compares the evidence for naming in slaughter to basmala/Fātiḥa in prayer, then a transmitter says objections and responses were abridged. It labels a further argument “ninth” and cites a marital invocation against satanic participation. ## T15 animal meat and hides, PDF 391–394
391 title «مسألة في أكل لحم الضبع والثعلب وسنور البر وابن آوى وجلودهم»; 392 blank.393 distinguishes species, meat permissibility, death/slaughter and tanning. Hyena meat is allowed according to the cited Mālik/Shāfiʿī/Aḥmad positions; tanning after death is separately disputed, while lawful slaughter plus tanning is treated differently. Wildcat and fox have two reported food positions, with consequences for whether slaughter alone purifies skin; Abū Ḥanīfa versus Aḥmad and variants are carefully separated. Jackal meat is forbidden under the listed Abū Ḥanīfa/Shāfiʿī/Aḥmad positions, with tanning disputed.

394 gives the author's evidentiary conclusion: what is established as a prohibited beast of prey (examples: leopard, jackal, weasel) is neither eaten nor worn as fur; hyena is treated outside the prohibited predatory class; fox and wildcat remain disputed. Do not replace these juristic classifications with modern zoological taxonomy, or infer hide purity merely from physical tanning without the text's legal conditions. Modern source footnote references Abū Dāwūd's predator-skin reports. Ends «والله أعلم».

## T16 sale of slaughtered sheep apart from hide, PDF 395–398
395 title; 396 blank.397 asks whether meat without hide, or hide alone, may be sold. Answer permits both and whole carcass, appealing to continuing practice and earlier/later authorities. It rejects the claim that visible carcass sale is an unseen/undescribed-object sale: ordinary inspection conveys the needed knowledge, as with hide/meat separately or a live animal. The distinction “living animal's skin is its preserving cover, dead one's not” and the analogy to beans within outer husks is judged weak. Transmitter «ثم قال» bridges show excerpted presentation here too, though no final explicit abridger colophon accompanies this short answer.

397–398 cite Companion purchases of beans and animals with head/hide/offal retained by seller, a migration-sheep report, al-Shaʿbī's report, Zayd ibn Thābit's opinion, and Mālik/Aḥmad's permission. **Modern critical qualification 398:** the migration report is cited from Ibn Ḥazm's *al-Muḥallā*8/403 via ʿUrwa ibn al-Zubayr and said to have several defects. Thus this reading records the author's larger practice/analogy argument and does not falsely call every named precedent sound. Ends «والله أعلم».
