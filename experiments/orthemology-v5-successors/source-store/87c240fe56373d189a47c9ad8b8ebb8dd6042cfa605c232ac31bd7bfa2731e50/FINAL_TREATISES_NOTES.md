## Treatise 17: «مسألة في إجارة الإقطاع»

### PDF 399–400: boundary
399 is the framed title leaf, with precisely that title. 400 is blank. The issue begins on 401, not on the title leaf.

### 401
The question asks whether leasing an iqṭāʿ grant is valid, reporting two alleged Shāfiʿī positions and judgments based on one. The answer affirms validity and says the author knows no Muslim scholar, of the four schools or others, who held it invalid. The contemporary contrary fatwa is represented as relying on some teachers, not transmitted authority. Its reasoning is that the grantee did not own the usufruct, hence could not convey it, like a borrower leasing a borrowed object.

The answer announces two stations of discussion. First, a new claim cannot simply be introduced against an established public Muslim practice. Then it gives two responses to the analogy: leasing a borrowed thing is itself disputed, and if its owner authorises the lease it is allowed; the granting ruler has authorised grantees to obtain revenue by cultivation, lease and sharecropping. This is an argument about the legal source and content of authorisation, not proof that the grantee possesses the land's corpus.

### 402
The second response denies that these usufructs are merely a private loan: the ruler does not own them; they are the Muslims' right and property, and he apportions them. The grantee therefore receives entitlement/ownership rather than bare permission. An analogy with waqf follows, then a careful distinction among succession mechanisms. A waqf's later beneficiary generation receives its right from the settlor, not by inheritance from the previous generation. Consequently the earlier beneficiary's lease may end at death, while an ordinary owner's lease generally survives the owner's death and the property passing to heirs. The positions of Mālik, al-Shāfiʿī, Aḥmad and Abū Ḥanīfa are contrasted. The Ḥanafī explanation concerns whether usufruct is owned before enjoyment and what newly arises in the heir's ownership; it is not simply “all death terminates all leases.”

The beneficiary personally is distinguished from a supervising administrator with authority over later generations. If an iqṭāʿ holder dies or loses the grant, the lease is analogous to that of the personal waqf beneficiary, so it terminates according to the majority; the tenant's standing crop remains protected and can stay to maturity at an equivalent rent. That is prospective termination, not retroactive invalidity.

### 403
Opening completes the crop rule and states that the first grantee's lease does not bind the next grantee, just as the first waqf generation does not bind the next. The principal argument is explicit: there is no legal evidence requiring every initially valid lease to concern a usufruct immune to subsequent dissolution. Hiring a wet nurse is valid although death of the nurse necessarily ends it; death of the particular child also ends it according to the favoured view, with the alternative of substituting another child reported. Destruction of the specifically hired slave or camel likewise terminates a valid lease.

Anchor: «فالإجارة جائزة بالنص والإجماع في مواضع متعددة، مع إمكان انفساخ الإجارة في أثناء المدة». Hence making impossibility of dissolution a condition contradicts these cases. The contrary borrower's analogy also misidentifies the operative legal feature: on the assumed restrictive view the borrower has no alienable benefit before taking/using it, rather than the contract's merely being susceptible to ending. Gifts are not owned before receipt on that theory. A tenant can sublease despite possible future termination of the headlease.

### 404
The grantee is more like a tenant or waqf beneficiary than a borrower because he receives an entitlement and recompense for his work. An objection to the appeal to consensus asks whether lease itself is contested. The reply limits recorded early disagreement to leasing land; wet-nursing, riding animals and analogous leases were not disputed by the early Muslim authorities. Any later denial is preceded by consensus grounded in text. The treatise closes «والله أعلم».

### Appraisal of T17
The strong portable distinction is **valid institution now versus defeasibility later**, together with the need to identify the actual basis of a legal analogy. It does not claim that every transient entitlement is leasable, that every future contingency is acceptable, or that all successors take from a common source in the same way. The distinction between inheritance and successive waqf generations is integral. The author's claims of absence of precedent/consensus are authorial assertions within a polemical fatwa, not independently verified historical findings here.

## Treatise 18: «مسألة في ضمان البساتين والأرض»

### PDF 405–406: boundary
405 is the framed title leaf. 406 is blank. 407 opens the question about taking an orchard/land with trees or palms for one or two years before fruit ripening.

### 407
Three legal positions are announced. The first prohibits the arrangement outright by assimilating it to the prohibited sale of fruit before ripeness. In that model a purchaser buys fruit alone while the seller must bear watering/service until maturation. This is attributed to the familiar Shāfiʿī and Aḥmad positions, with Abū Ḥanīfa stricter. A disputed evasion leases the land and attaches a musāqāt contract for an extremely small share. Aḥmad's reported text forbids it; Abū Yaʿlā in *Ibṭāl al-ḥiyal* and familiar Shāfiʿī doctrine allow it. At the bottom a parenthetical transmitter says the shaykh argued the device's corruption from several directions, then resumes a quoted conclusion. This already marks abridgment, not an uninterrupted authorial argument.

Modern footnote supplies Bukhārī2194 and Muslim 1534 for the prohibition, from Ibn ʿUmar.

### 408
The transmitted excerpt resumes that authorities should restrain the invalid device, followed by additional “then he said” bridges. The second position permits leasing when the land's benefit is principal and trees subordinate, attributed to Mālik, with one-third used as the limit of subsidiary inclusion. Fruit before ripeness can accompany the sale of an already pollinated palm by stipulation; the authorial argument considers whether the same subsidiary status applies in lease.

The third position permits taking land and trees together even where trees predominate: Ibn ʿAqīl's view and an act attributed to ʿUmar. ʿUmar took a three-year rent/guarantee for Usayd ibn Ḥuḍayr's orchard to discharge his debt. Reports are ascribed to Ḥarb al-Kirmānī and Abū Zurʿa al-Dimashqī. Medina orchards are noted to have predominantly trees. The modern footnote references Bukhārī2204,2716 and Muslim 1543 for the pollinated-palm case.

### 409
The report chain for ʿUmar's act is given: Ḥarb, Saʿīd ibn Manṣūr, ʿAbbād ibn ʿAbbād, Hishām ibn ʿUrwa, his father. Usayd died owing six thousand dirhams; ʿUmar summoned creditors and assigned them the yield/rent of his land for years, including trees and palms. A western Ẓāhirī compiler's claim that this opposes consensus is rejected. The answer argues the reverse is nearer because the act was publicly performed in Medina among emigrants and helpers without reported objection. Comparisons include objections made to ʿUmar in pilgrimage matters and ʿUthmān's inheritance judgment for ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn ʿAwf's divorced wife during mortal illness.

The author's first substantive reason: forbidding sale of grain before hardening cannot mean forbidding rental of land where the lessee cultivates grain. The legal relation and allocation of work matter. Modern footnote gives the grain-prohibition reports.

### 410
The land lessee works the land to obtain grain. By contrast the bare-fruit purchaser expects the seller to complete cultivation/service. Similarly the prohibition on grape sales until blackening does not prohibit receiving vines to water and tend them oneself. The comparison is functional, not merely a switch of contractual name.

The second reason compares muzāraʿa on land with musāqāt on trees. Their permissibility is attributed to Aḥmad and other ḥadīth jurists including Ibn Khuzayma and Ibn al-Mundhir, also Ibn Abī Laylā, Abū Yūsuf, Muḥammad and al-Layth. Opponents classified sharecropping as a lease for unknown remuneration. Abū Ḥanīfa applied that analogy consistently; al-Shāfiʿī and Mālik made limited subsidiary exceptions. The page ends with an analogous mistaken categorization of muḍāraba as exceptional to analogy.

### 411
Central classification: these are partnerships, not wage leases. «فالمضاربة والمساقاة والمزارعة مشاركة، هذا يشارك بنفع بدنه، وهذا بنفع ماله، وما قسم الله من ربح كان بينهما كشريكي العنان». One contributes bodily labour, another the benefit of assets; the yielded profit is shared. Labour itself is not specified and contracted for in the same manner as ijāra, where it must be known. Juʿāla is a closer comparison, with a share of the result stipulated to the worker. A military example gives a finder of enemy assets a portion after the fifth; this is the author's historical example, not a new recommendation.

A transmitter bridge skips other examples. The author again distinguishes prohibited bare-fruit sale, where the purchaser does no work to bring it about, from receiving land and trees to produce fruit/crop. Third reason: fruits can function like usufructs in waqf and loan; trees can be endowed so beneficiaries take their fruits, just as land is endowed for yield. Another abridger bridge ends the page.

### 412
An objection says Ibn ʿAqīl allowed a need-based exception rather than the author's broader ordinary-analogy argument. Trees cannot conveniently be split from rented land; allowing fruit subsidiary to a larger transaction parallels pollinated palms. Response distinguishes two approaches: concede that ordinary principle forbids, then allow for need; or deny that the prohibition covers this class at all. The latter is defended: the prophetic prohibition concerns sale, not lease, and neither its wording nor its meaningful rationale extends to this form. If it were a fruit sale, fulfilment costs would lie on the seller. A leased orchard is like leased land; the argument continues on 413.

### 413
The tenant takes productive assets and performs cultivation until crop or fruit develops, like a musāqāt/muzāraʿa worker. Calling him a fruit purchaser would equally misdescribe the crop lessee and growth-sharing partners. Since the transaction is not within the term “sale,” the prohibition's verbal generality does not include it. Nor does analogy/semantic generality, because its relevant difference is evident and its affinities to land leases, waqf, loan, musāqāt and muzāraʿa are stronger.

Terminological observation: these are ijārāt and qabālāt, popularly called ḍamānāt; the earlier term was qabāla. The conclusion is tested by liability: if a seller fails to water and ripen sold grain/fruit, the shortfall is his; if a lessee fails to irrigate or serve rented land/trees, the shortfall is the lessee's, not the lessor's. This prevents a merely nominal redescription from doing the legal work.

### 414
Prohibited nonexistent-object sales include unborn animal offspring, offspring of offspring and multi-year produce, where the seller keeps and cultivates the productive source and delivers eventual products. Two interpretations of ḥabal al-ḥabala are distinguished: sale of offspring's offspring, or sale payable only at that event, invalid for an unknown term. Such transactions are assimilated to gambling and unjust taking of wealth. By contrast orchard taking by the cultivator is an asset lease, like cultivating land or ground leasing for building/planting. The page moves to ʿUmar's kharāj precedent. Modern footnotes furnish source identifications for these historical prohibitions.

### 415
The kharāj argument accommodates differing theories of conquered lands: compulsory collective fayʾ, distribution to conquerors, or the ruler's choice, with school attributions. Across the differences, imposing kharāj without wronging conquerors is accepted; many jurists construe it as land rent, including assessments on palm and vine land. Therefore renting land with trees has a precedent whether ordinary analogy or need-based exception explains it.

Practical need: orchards may contain dwellings of substantial rentable value, including waqf or orphan property. Giving them only to crop-share workers can suspend the housing benefit. Separating resident and cultivator creates interference and harm. The argument does not assume that asset interests can always be divided without changing practical availability.

### 416
Continues the reciprocal harms of separately housed residents and orchard workers: resident access to fruit/crop, intrusions into dwellings, and the worker's concern about protecting produce. Historical examples of household visitors, children and women reflect the passage's setting and are not adopted as present-day judgments about groups.

Further analogies: the prohibition of muzābana coexists with the need-based ʿarāyā exception; a tree owner causing entry-related harm can be required to sell/give or have the tree removed; compulsory co-owner buyout/pre-emption removes harms of divided ownership. The explicitly stated norm is securing/completing benefits and averting/reducing harms, sometimes foregoing a lesser good or accepting a lesser harm. Here serious established housing/cultivation harm outweighs small speculative contractual harm. Sharecropping also depends on worker trustworthiness, which may be difficult to find; a fixed debt rent can satisfy a genuine need.

### 417
After a transmitter bridge, the text distinguishes loss before one is able to enjoy a leased benefit (no rent owed) and loss before one can receive purchased goods (seller's risk). Then it considers loss after the opportunity to receive but before actual receipt, reporting Mālik/Aḥmad versus Abū Ḥanīfa/al-Shāfiʿī, with qualifications about real property and takhliya as receipt. Fruit sold after onset of ripeness but lost before full completion produces a similar disagreement; Mālik and Aḥmad put the loss on the seller, based on the blight/damage report. The footnote is modern Muslim 1554 sourcing.

### 418
Al-Shāfiʿī's familiar view assigns fruit loss to the buyer after receipt, while Abū Ḥanīfa does not treat continued attachment as implicit or a valid stipulation. The other position says early receipt is like receiving usufruct: not full receipt transferring risk because the intended contractual benefit cannot yet be exhausted. Anchor: «المعتبر هو القدرة على الاستيفاء المقصود بالعقد». Delaying harvest after full ripeness is different: then loss can be buyer's risk. Neglect in using leased benefits also shifts risk to the tenant, whereas innocent loss lies with the lessor. Inability to sow because of a supervening impediment removes rent; crop loss by heavenly calamity after growth but before harvest is explicitly disputed, not settled here.

An abridger bridge introduces a recapitulation: sale is not lease, and musāqāt/muzāraʿa are participation contracts justified by Sunnah, companion agreement and analogy, applicable to varied trees and soils.

### 419
Seed can come from landowner, worker or both; worker-supplied seed is argued a fortiori and linked to Khaybar. The analogy requiring all seed from the owner because muḍāraba has capital from one person and work from another is rejected for two reasons. First, muḍāraba's capital is returned to its owner separately from divided profit, whereas seed is consumed rather than returned; it belongs with inputs functioning as benefits, like irrigation water and cattle fodder. Second, capital and work need not be distributed in one rigid pattern among partners.

**Textual caution:** the scan's lower paragraph visibly reads «فيجوز ببدنين ومال، ومال وبدنين» after wording that is also awkward/repetitive. Two close crops and a higher-resolution rerender confirmed the printing, but no manuscript collation was performed. Preserve this as a scan reading; do not silently replace it with the smoother expected “two capitals/one body” contrast. The secure conclusion is the explicit allowance of varied contribution allocations, not a reconstructed enumerated formula.

The prohibited old mukhābara stipulates the landowner's crop from one specifically designated patch, as opposed to an undivided share of the result. Modern footnote gives Bukhārī2720.

### 420
Continues across land configurations: bare land, building, trees, or combinations. If the lessee waters, tends and tills to obtain the result, it remains lease rather than premature fruit sale. The worker in crop-share receives a common/undivided fraction; the tenant obtains all crop/fruit and owes a named rent as debt. This distinguishes contractual shares from ownership of isolated patches.

The text then considers hiring an enslaved worker/wet nurse for stated wages or maintenance; these are historical slave-economy examples, not endorsed present-day arrangements. Hiring cattle, camels or sheep during milking for a stated rent, possibly with fodder, may be treated similarly. Whether the transaction resembles sale or lease depends partly on who feeds/tends the animal and supplies milk.

### 421
Where an owner feeds and the customer takes a specified quantity, the milk transaction is an ordinary sale; unrestricted taking can likewise be sale, while the nurse directly feeds the child. Gharar is characterised here as oscillation between existence and nonexistence, akin to gambling and unjust appropriation, not every imperfection in foresight.

Usual benefits known by custom—land/animal use, ordinary nurse/animal milk, customary fruit/crop—are allowable. Failure to obtain the intended benefit proportionately reduces rent, analogous to casualty reduction in sale. Final crucial qualification: all the orchard-lease analysis assumes that the taker must do the productive work until fruit/crop arises. If service/work remains the seller's and the customer only resides and eats, it is sale, allowed only after ripeness. Once some trees of a kind ripen, selling the whole of that kind in the same orchard is allowed; extending this across all orchards is not allowed under the more familiar of the two opinions.

### 422
For mixed-fruit orchards (grapes, dates, apples, apricots, mulberries), one opinion allows selling the whole after one kind begins ripening, since the ordinary bulk object requires ripeness of part, not every component. Separating trees/kinds and introducing multiple buyers can harm both parties. An objection about later unripe parts failing from blight is answered by applying the same casualty adjustment as for ripe produce.

The benefits/harms rationale is repeated with nuance: pre-ripeness sale is unnecessary risk; after onset, preventing needed trade is more harmful than the residual risk. That risk is repaired by casualty reduction. The final sentence crosses to 423.

### 423
Loss before ability to receive lies on seller, analogously lessor. Once harvesting is possible, negligent delay or deliberate delay for a price rise puts the risk on the taker. Ends «والله سبحانه أعلم».

**Transmission fact, not speculation:** modern note 1 quotes the exemplar's final notice: «قال الناقل لنفسه - عفا الله عنه -: اختصرت جواب الشيخ تقي الدين، وحذفت منه المكرر وغيره، والله أعلم». Thus T18 is a transmitted abridgment, with omitted repetitive **and other** material. The parenthetical transitions throughout must not be mistaken for either continuous Ibn Taymiyya wording or a fully preserved argument. The editor's source citations below the rules are modern apparatus. Their presence does not mean this reader independently checked the cited hadith books.

### Appraisal of T18
1. The answer's governing distinction is among sale of a future product, lease of productive assets, and partnership in realised yield. It tests classification with work/service allocation, cost of fulfilment, control/receipt and casualty liability.
2. 411 is a genuine juridical discussion of materially different contributions to a common result. It is relevant to a cooperation study without being a theorem about independent causal sufficiency, necessary coexistence or perfection.
3. 419 broadens contribution patterns, but a printed textual difficulty blocks exact reliance on its short combinatorial formula. The seed/capital contrast is secure and orthogonal: consumed productive input is not automatically equivalent to capital returned before profit division.
4. The text does not eliminate risk. It differentiates intolerable avoidable speculation from ordinary customary benefits, need, proportionate adjustment and preventable loss. 403,418 and 423 must be kept together if extracting a general distinction between initial validity and later availability.
5. No passage here proves that partners have identical powers, awareness, intentions or equal causal shares. Shared profit is a contractual structure, not evidence of psychologically identical aims. Nor does it prove that either contributor can independently produce the joint result.

## Bounded rereads performed

After the complete sequential first pass, full-page visual rereads of 403,411,419,423 were performed. 419 additionally received a close crop at 1800-source render resolution and a 3200-pixel rerender/crop. These rereads are explicitly bounded, not represented as a second full read of 399–444.

- **403**: tested whether “may later dissolve” was being confused with “invalid now.” The explicit wet-nurse/animal/corpus cases confirm the distinction; the borrower case has a different stated legal basis. No unconditional persistence follows from validity.
- **411**: tested whether “partnership” merely renames unknown-wage lease. It does not: profit is shared, contributions differ, and work is not specified like a wage service. The page also acknowledges juʿāla as a closer comparison.
- **419**: tested whether contribution roles were rigid and whether seed equals recoverable capital. The argument rejects both simplifications. The printed short formula remains awkward and must not be emended from expectation.
- **423**: tested whether the frequent parenthetical omissions are just modern paraphrase. The quoted final copyist notice confirms an abridged exemplar. Final liability rule distinguishes inability to harvest from negligent/speculative delay after ability.

## Result for the collection-level study

This section adds an orthogonal, substantive legal treatment rather than a new metaphysical proof. Its strongest transferable cautions are (a) do not infer permanent availability from valid initial relation, (b) classify relations by productive duties and entitlement rather than surface vocabulary, (c) shared end/yield allows differentiated contributions, (d) do not equate consumed productive inputs with returned capital, and (e) inability to obtain the intended product is a separate condition from breach/negligence in one's own work. None by itself establishes the wider philosophical study's claims about independent agents, cognition, necessity or source-mode perfection.
