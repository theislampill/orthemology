# Information receipt and practical obligation

9 October 2026 UTC. Bounded source and formal follow-through within active T20.

## Result

The existing *Majmūʿ* 14 findings support **qualified beneficial non-disclosure**, not a general ordering in which less knowledge is better overall. They say more than that disclosure can feel burdensome: Ibn Taymiyya himself calls some obscurity mercy and explains how withholding one detail may be better for its recipients. But they supply no complete comparison of all goods, harms, duties and alternatives in an actual case.

The formal bridge is exact: **receiving knowledge may change the practical requirements applicable to its recipient; declining to use that knowledge need not restore the previous normative situation.** Consequently, a fixed-decision-problem argument about optional use of a signal does not establish universal practical benefit from actual disclosure. That argument remains valid within its assumptions. No new theorem, general recommendation to avoid knowledge, or T20 closure follows.

## What the source actually evaluates

At 14:159, Ibn Taymiyya glosses the reported withholding of the Night of Decree's date: it may be better because people will strive throughout the ten nights. This is a positive purposive assessment of obscurity. It is not a claim that doing less is better; here obscurity encourages more effort.

He next says disagreement over rulings may be mercy **if obscurity of the ruling does not lead to great harm**. The qualification is explicit and must remain attached to this use of the passage. He immediately maintains that the truth in itself is one, while its being hidden may be mercy for some people because disclosure would bring severity. His repeated “may” and “some” matter.

The market-goods example supplies the closest bridge to knowledge-dependent practical status. Goods may in fact have been wrongfully taken; the recipient who does not know this has the stated permission and lack of sin, in contrast with someone who knows. The paragraph does not specify every consequence, remedy or duty once knowledge is present. It does not say that acquiring knowledge is itself a sin. Its support is narrower: actual knowledge matters to the applicability of the stated protection, not merely to the recipient's feelings about an unchanged practical situation.

The same paragraph contrasts ignorance of what entails severity with ignorance of a dispensation, which may instead be punishment. Removing doubt can have either practical effect. It ends by allowing that something unwelcome may be more beneficial. Thus experienced ease, epistemic possession and benefit are not interchangeable measures.

This is Ibn Taymiyya's authorial evaluation and explanation. The hadith he quotes, the anecdote he reports about Aḥmad, and his scriptural applications are source-owned material, not newly authenticated historical findings or an independent present-day legal ruling.

## Continuous chapter context

The retained chapter account, 14:142–167, prevents turning this qualification into indifference to truth. Commands have wisdom and can combine the act's good with the good of responding to a genuine direction (144–147). Lack of knowledge can deprive later people of an already valid legal relief without abrogating that relief (151–155). At 160 the author expressly returns to harmful loss of beneficial knowledge; the immediately following text praises knowledge and faith.

Two distinct mechanisms should remain distinct. At 153–154, mistaken understanding of unchanged source law can coexist with a separate duty to state the result of qualified inquiry. At 159, knowledge of an actual fact can affect which protection or requirement applies. Neither mechanism requires that the source's standing norm be rewritten each time someone's epistemic position changes.

The author gives some deprivation a moral-causal explanation. The research cannot reverse that explanation into a universal inference from ignorance, difficulty or suffering to a particular person's guilt. The mercy cases expressly defeat such a universal converse. Nothing here licenses negligent inquiry, deliberate concealment, invented source-attributions, or evasion of an established duty.

## Why optional use is insufficient

In the elementary fixed decision problem, a state ω has a fixed prior, available actions A and evaluation U(ω,a). A signal s enlarges policies from constant actions to functions π(s). If a constant policy remains admissible and has the same consequences as before, the informed agent can reproduce the baseline policy. Therefore the optimal **ex ante expected** value cannot fall. This is not a claim about every realized signal, every chosen response, or every dimension of goodness.

The source-supported case challenges transporting that conclusion into a different semantics. Let k record whether the relevant fact is actually known. A standing norm may have knowledge-dependent applicability, represented by admissible actions A(k), or by an evaluation of the resulting conduct U(ω,a,k). Holding the physical act a constant while changing k can then change its normative status or consequences. Ignoring s in choosing a leaves k unchanged. It does not make a received and known fact unknown again.

One can preserve a single fixed higher-order norm and a single evaluation over the expanded state (ω,k). The comparison then includes an information-receipt event that changes a normatively relevant part of that state. It is no longer the simple comparison in which only the policy's dependence on an observational signal changes. Thus the issue is not necessarily “the utility function changed,” and it is not a contradiction of the fixed-problem result.

A genuine option to decline receipt **before knowing**, when that option is permissible and preserves the baseline consequences, can restore the baseline-replication argument for the value of the *offer*. That condition must be established. It cannot be manufactured by renaming non-use after receipt as non-receipt, or by assuming an agent has no duty of inquiry. This distinction gives no advice to decline knowledge.

The inherited information-gain result for a fixed truth-score and target has a still narrower evaluative surface. Epistemic improvement under that score is not automatically reduced practical burden or an all-things-considered normative improvement.

## A transparent comparison without a selected utility

Track three questions separately:

1. **Epistemic position:** Has the relevant fact become adequately known?
2. **Practical requirement and burden:** What now applies, and what must the person undertake or forgo?
3. **Overall evaluation:** How do truth, fitting conduct, benefits, harms and other relevant goods compare?

The market example can answer the first affirmatively while making the second more demanding. That alone does not settle the third. Conversely, the Night of Decree example supplies the author's positive rationale for some non-disclosure while encouraging effort. No numerical weighting or complete aggregate ordering is given in the passage. Calling one outcome “mercy” is genuine source-level evaluation; it should neither be erased into mere psychology nor inflated into a derived universal net-value theorem.

The supported conclusion is therefore: wise guidance need not maximize explicit detail for every recipient in every situation; some non-disclosure may serve a real good, while truth remains determinate and beneficial knowledge remains valuable. Any specific invocation must establish the purpose, legitimate epistemic and practical conditions, and relevant harms. In particular, the passage's no-great-harm condition on merciful disagreement cannot be omitted.
