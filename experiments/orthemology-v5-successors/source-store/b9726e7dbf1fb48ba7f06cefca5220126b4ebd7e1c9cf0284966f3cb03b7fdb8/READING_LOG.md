# Continuous Minhaj volume 2 reading

## Scope and conventions

The body is the Islamicbook electronic transcription assigned stable local identifiers V02L0001–V02L0437. The identifiers are extraction lines, not printed pages. Every reported first-pass interval is continuous and was shown in an untruncated tool output before credit was added. Medieval authorial claims, his quotations of an opponent, reported hadith/historical claims, and present interpretive judgments remain distinct. This reading does not authenticate every quotation or independently verify the historical assertions. Site duplication is not independent manuscript evidence.

## V02L0001–0012

The second volume begins within a structured refutation of an Imami opponent's claim that his school alone ought to be followed. Four proposed grounds are announced: truth, difference from other schools in doctrinal fundamentals, assured salvation, and receipt of religion from infallible imams. The present opening develops political succession and the epistemic/moral standing of the Companions rather than a cosmological argument.

At L0003–0004 the opponent is quoted dividing the post-Prophetic community into self-interested claimants, imitators driven by worldly benefit or defective examination, and a rightful claimant with a small disinterested following. Ibn Taymiyya's reply disputes both the exhaustiveness and the historical fit of this taxonomy: in his account neither Abu Bakr nor Ali sought the office in the way described. The reply's epistemic framework distinguishes knowing truth from following it; absence of knowledge and willful refusal are different failures, and practical salvation requires knowledge plus action. Qur'anic and polemical characterizations of Jews and Christians occur inside this authorial scheme, not as present study conclusions about populations.

L0007–0012 argues against wholesale disqualification of the earliest Muslim community by invoking its scripturally attested excellence. The argument mixes a reductio from the opponent's purportedly exhaustive partition, textual praise of emigrants and helpers, and numerous reports commanding forgiveness for or prohibiting abuse of Companions. The later community's membership among praised successors is tied to seeking forgiveness and lacking hostility; the author uses this against the rival school. L0012 begins detailed historical placement of the tree pledge, Hudaybiyya and Khaybar, identifying those praised with people who subsequently pledged to the early caliphs. The force of the reasoning depends on its scriptural/historical premises and the proposed persistence of the relevant standing; it is not a generic proof that a numerical majority is true.

Orthogonal material retained: allocation of fayʾ, reported comments by Malik and Aisha, chains and report variants, historical dates and participants, and the duties of knowledge and righteous action. No chains were independently authenticated in this pass. Apparent electronic anomalies include L0006's wording `فالضال الذي يعرف الحق` (the surrounding contrast suggests a missing negation, but the retained text is not silently repaired), L0007 `بالإضرار`, and typographic run-ons. These require a better witness before exact wording is used decisively.

## V02L0013–0025

The author narrows the preferred meaning of the earliest predecessors to those who spent and fought before Hudaybiyya, rather than simply those who prayed toward both directions. He distinguishes a genuine priority-based excellence from the claim that an arbitrarily selected legal transition itself supplies the decisive ranking. Here interpretation is constrained by comparing the relevant scriptural texts rather than isolating one phrase.

L0015–0017 distinguishes allegiance as love and its contrary enmity from political appointment. A reported ring-giving interpretation of Qur'an 5:55 is rejected through source-authenticity claims, syntax, context, rules about praise, and practical objections about almsgiving during prayer. Its plurality, circumstantial wāw, and legal details all matter; the author does not rest only on an unsupported translation of wali as ruler or friend. These claims remain his polemical and legal argument, not an independent authentication exercise in this study. L0018 contains sweeping hostile characterizations of rival groups; preserve their authorial attribution rather than laundering them into neutral history.

L0019–0020 uses the promised succession, security and religious establishment as an interpretive/historical argument for the early caliphs and their community. He also includes Ali and other later combatants because they participated in the earlier period of establishment. His appeal to the historical fit of a divine promise must be distinguished from a general thesis that political success always establishes righteousness.

L0021–0024 is a careful anti-overreading of the preposition min. The author says that its generic function permits inclusion of all members without itself proving that all meet the specified condition. L0024 expressly concedes this to an objector: the grammatical point removes a proposed exclusion, while the broader affirmative case comes from other praise and descriptors. This is important methodological material: non-exclusion is not positive exhaustive proof. L0022 also distinguishes a conceivably shareable universal from actual numerical sharing. Legal examples involving conditional emancipation and divorce illustrate how a condition can apply to every qualifying member of a group.

L0025 explains that attaching a ruling to an appropriately derived designation indicates the ground of the ruling, then begins to distinguish outward hypocritical affiliation from the substantive characterizations in the texts. The conditional promise is tied to faith and righteous works, not mere bodily proximity. Orthogonal notes: phased legislation, particular early-Muslim geography and chronology, Arabic negative-scope semantics, legal conditionals and household examples. Apparent missing negation at L0022 (`و يجوز ... لم يغفر إلا لبعضهم`) is retained as an electronic uncertainty; the surrounding argument does not license silent emendation.

## V02L0026–0040

L0026–0028 continues to distinguish genuine believers from outward affiliation. A historical inference is drawn from an announced sanction not occurring to the inferred cessation of the conduct that would trigger it. The author then attacks appeals to dissimulation when they would neutralize reports of Ali's praise for earlier caliphs. He distinguishes an actual coercion exception from a proposed general license for saying the opposite of one's belief; his evidentiary claim is that the reported praise was unforced, sometimes private, and multiply transmitted. These historical and sociological claims are source claims, not newly verified facts. Especially harsh group accusations remain explicitly attributed to the polemical author.

L0029 supplies a significant quantifier distinction: a description of the assembled community can concern its corporate condition, whereas individual forgiveness requires the relevant predicate in each individual; a corporate grouping may contain a hypocrite. This qualifies any move from group-wide public success to universal individual status. The chapter's next numbered response contests the opponent's description of Abu Bakr as a self-seeking claimant by reports that he proposed others, the community chose him, and he later sought release. L0030–0031 also argues from the absence of the alleged worldly incentive: equal stipends, no privileged treasury distribution to the alleged supporters, and personal expenditure rather than enrichment. This is explanatory challenge to a motive attribution, not merely hagiographic assertion.

L0031–0036 develops a comparative evidential argument. An interlocutor who affirms Jesus or Ali yet attacks Muhammad or the early caliphs with analogous objections is pressed to explain why weaker evidence suffices for the favored case while stronger evidence is discounted in the other. The author explicitly separates two patterns at L0032–0033: (a) comparing genuinely blameworthy conduct of different degrees, and (b) removing false objections in two cases where neither subject deserves the accusation, but evidential strength and apparent suspicion differ. The latter does not amount to endorsing the hypothetical accusation against Ali; L0033–0035 gives the hostile alternative as an immanent challenge to selective standards. The method requires the comparative evidential ranking, including its asymmetry, actually to hold. It cannot create that ranking by mere analogy.

L0035–0036 emphasizes transmission dependencies: rejecting almost all the Companion transmitters would undercut the rival's own use of their reports about Ali, and there were allegedly too few distinct rival transmitters to create the claimed alternative tawatur. A report-based warrant cannot selectively use a disqualified channel without an explanation. The source's own tawatur assertions have not been audited here.

L0036–0040 rejects comparison of the early caliphs with Umar b. Sa'd as a morally irrelevant analogy; kinship does not make father and son alike. It draws an explicit reverse analogy involving Muhammad b. Abi Bakr, calls both Uthman and Husayn wrongfully killed martyrs, compares kinds and degrees of wrongdoing in al-Mukhtar and al-Hajjaj, then states the author's ideal of informed justice toward both Companions and the Prophet's family. Historical atrocities are not defended simply because a rival committed something judged worse. L0039–0040 records disagreement over Ali/Uthman's relative ranking, while claiming broad agreement on Abu Bakr/Umar. Special excellence in one respect is distinguished from unconditional overall precedence. The passage then returns to the epistemic/moral partition: bad intent, negligent inquiry, or inability to inquire.

Orthogonal details retained include scriptural promises and threats, coercion, private/public speech, distribution policy, al-Baqillani's diplomatic anecdote, legal and military history, rankings within early scholarly schools, and the difference between specific and global excellence. Several names/words contain likely electronic slips or run-ons; none has been silently repaired or independently used to establish a historical event.

## V02L0041–0054

The author demands evidence for imputations of ignorance or corrupt motive even before offering contrary praise: lack of knowledge of someone's condition does not license adverse testimony about it (L0041). The report attributed to Ibn Mas'ud is interpreted at L0042 as combining good intention, deep understanding and little artificial burden. His subsequent countercase uses recognized later scholarly figures, including figures attached to the Prophet's family, and argues that their alleged agreement cannot all be explained by immediate worldly benefits from Abu Bakr's accession. This is a historically mediated challenge to the proposed causal explanation of assent. Sweeping claims about whole sects, armies and rulers at L0043 are polemical source assertions; they have not become independently checked generalizations in these notes.

L0046 makes an explicit objection to prejudging an inquiry: if one has already established that one faction follows truth and the other falsehood, further inquiry is unnecessary; if not, the verdict should not have been inserted into the proposed inquiry's description. The same passage distinguishes seeking allegiance, receiving it, privately preferring someone, and alleging an unexpressed right. The opponent's description is contested on those separately specified historical acts, not only on evaluation of Ali's rank.

L0048–0050 opposes the opponent's idealized depiction of a small fearless ascetic following with reported failure to support Ali and Husayn. Comparisons with Khawarij distinguish observable zeal or ascetic practice from doctrinal correctness. The account of advice to Husayn and abandonment at Kufa is retained as historical argument in the source, without independent report verification. L0051–0052 distinguishes early political partisanship around Ali or Uthman from later named doctrinal identities, and recounts a proposed origin of the label Rafida in the followers' rejection of Zayd. This authorial genealogy is not assumed to settle modern historical scholarship. The embedded report about Sa'd b. Hisham also preserves opposition to abandoning marriage for uninterrupted warfare, consultation of Aisha about the Prophet's night prayer, and her role in the two political factions; these ostensibly orthogonal details are part of the continuous reading.

At L0053–0054 a new opponent quotation begins the first of his four grounds. He claims Imami doctrine is the truest, purest and most protective of divine, prophetic and imamly perfection. The excerpt begins claims about divine eternity and uniqueness, creation of all else, incorporeality and noncomposition, then breaks in the middle of the argument from the parts of a composite. L0054 is the opponent's voice; it is not yet Ibn Taymiyya's endorsed final account.

## V02L0055–0070

The opponent's quoted doctrinal programme (L0054–0055) includes divine ability over all possibles; justice, wisdom and purposive action; prophecy for guidance; nonvisibility and denial of direction; the createdness of command/prohibition/report because nonexistent addressees cannot be addressed; and comprehensive prophetic/imamic infallibility to safeguard trust and the benefit of sending prophets. It also opposes independent opinion, analogy and preference in subsidiary law. These positions form the object of a layered response, not an authorial credo to be indiscriminately quoted under Ibn Taymiyya's name.

The first responses (L0057–0061) contest the proposed evidential link to imamate: the metaphysical doctrines and the political/imamly conclusion can occur independently, and the opponent himself gives different routes to them (reason versus transmitted designation). Neither truth nor falsity of the shared metaphysical view establishes the distinctive claim about twelve infallible imams. A further historical genealogy attributes the later doctrine to Mu'tazilite borrowing and contrasts it with earlier Shi'i doctrines. The author says true elements are shared with Sunnis while false elements should be rejected; group membership does not itself decide a proposition's truth.

L0062–0066 distinguishes disputed technical vocabulary from underlying propositions. A report that someone is a 'corporealist' may record an opponent's claimed entailment rather than the person's own term. Three responses to the premises 'attributes require a body' and 'body is impossible' are distinguished: contest the first, contest the second, or reject the ambiguous unlicensed vocabulary while separately analyzing its senses. The author criticizes both particular positive and negative formulations, retaining a preference for differentiated meaning over bare slogans. His claims about historical schools' transmission remain uncollated.

L0065 contrasts reinterpretation, suspension and the philosophers' alleged pedagogical-falsehood theory; these are not identical strategies. L0066 positively claims that sound rational and transmitted evidence cannot really conflict and that revelation can communicate by report, by drawing attention, or by furnishing rational proof. Its rational content is presented as richer than unaided systems, not as merely opaque commands. This is a claim requiring its own instances, not a license to assume every preferred inference is proved.

L0067–0070 develops shared predication without identity of concrete qualities. Divine and created bearers can both be called living, knowing, powerful, merciful, king, and so forth without their particular perfections being alike. Lists of scriptural usages and transmitted supplications make this an affirmative semantic argument. L0070 begins a consistency challenge to the selective attribute-denier: the same nonassimilation rationale allowed for affirmed will, speech, hearing or sight must be considered for rejected love, satisfaction or anger. This does not yet say that every sense of every predicate is a pure perfection; the text's subsequent qualifications remain to be read.

Orthogonal details preserved include prayer formulas, requests for life/death according to their good, truthfulness under anger or contentment, moderation in wealth/poverty, and differences among scholastic classifications. Apparent duplications and omissions in the electronic wording (for example `بل أئمة بل أئمة` and the tail of L0064) remain transcription limits.

## V02L0071–0088

L0071–0072 advances an affirmative argument rather than merely rebutting anthropomorphism. It divides existence into necessary/nonnecessary, beginningless/temporally originated, creator/created, and independent/dependent, and asserts that the nonnecessary, originated, created or dependent cannot obtain without the corresponding ultimate. Sensory recognition of originated existence then yields both an independent creator and a dependent created existent. Common truth of 'existent' does not make their concrete realities alike; full likeness would transfer incompatible necessities and possibilities. The pivotal dependence premises deserve their full force, but the mere dichotomies are not by themselves proofs of those premises or of each proposed identification across the classifications. The passage remains affirmative evidence for an original, self-subsisting source.

L0073–0075 rejects both concrete sharing of one existence and pure verbal equivocation. A meaningful genus can be divided into necessary/possible or old/new; a merely equivocal word cannot be divided that way. Unequal realization (tashkik) still involves a common intelligible meaning. The universal exists mentally, while each concrete existence is particular. Reifying the universal or comparing generic existence with a particular essence creates a false composition problem. Even the hypothetical allowance of an external universal would not immediately yield the opponent's proposed existence/essence composition. This is closely relevant to later role typing: sharing a predicate is neither numerically sharing a concrete constituent nor erasing the predicate's meaningful commonality.

L0076–0077 distinguishes the central revealed proclamation of unique divinity/worship from the later technical formula that only one is 'ancient'. Its validity depends on sense. A living, knowing, powerful reality does not first exist as a bare essence and then receive detachable attributes. 'Attributes additional to essence' is explained as additional to what a denialist has conceded, not separate ancient entities added to an intrinsically bare thing. Four scholastic positions concerning attributes and states (ahwal) are laid out; criticizing one must not automatically refute all others.

L0078–0080 argues that true attribution tracks an intrinsic reality: speech occurring in a different bearer does not thereby make this bearer the speaker, and a derivative designation requires the corresponding source-meaning. The point also covers acting and creating, not just static attributes. The objection that an intrinsic creating must be either beginningless (making its product beginningless) or originated (requiring another creation and regress) receives a survey of distinct answers: eternal creating with temporal product, terminating the demand for a separate creating of the creating, simultaneous infinite meanings, successive intrinsic acts, and the distinction between regress in effects and regress in producers. The author favors the legitimacy of successive freely willed acts without conflating these proposals. Opponent arguments and surveyed alternative schools are not all his adopted positions.

L0081–0083 rejects 'an eternal attribute would be another god': a prophet's quality is not another prophet. An attribute can lack a temporal beginning or efficient cause while not subsisting independently. Consequently 'necessary' can mean self-subsisting, uncaused, or unrelated; the first is not said of attributes, the second can be, and the last describes nothing at all. God relates to his creatures, essence and attributes are mutually inseparable, and attributes are mutually concomitant. Intrinsic inseparability is thus not an external donation of being or perfection. The formula 'everything other than God is originated' does not remove intrinsic attributes from the named God and count them as creatures. A genealogy of Aristotelian and Avicennian classifications accompanies the argument but has not been historically collated in this pass.

L0084–0088 separates senses of body: previously assembled components, possible disintegration, matter/form and indivisible atoms are denied; a visible, indicated bearer of attributes is not denied merely by using that term. The author lists more than the two theories usually supplied by his rivals' manuals and denies that a school textbook's restricted alternatives exhaust Muslim or rational positions. He disputes atomism partly because it supposedly reduces creation of new bodies to rearrangement of preexisting atoms plus accidents, and appeals to observable transformation and legal disputes about purification through transformation. These are the text's premodern natural-philosophical arguments, not verified modern physics. L0088 ends within a discussion of continuous rotation and atomist consequences.

## V02L0089–0104

L0089–0092 continues the critique of reified abstractions and alleged corporeal-composition entailments. It separately attacks the inference to composition and the claim that the resulting consequence is impossible. 'Place' is likewise disambiguated into enclosure, a needed support, something beneath an independent superior, or an unoccupied beyond; dependence on a containing/supporting thing is not inferred merely from spatial language. The particular divine-aboveness thesis is argued here, rather than neutralized as an incidental metaphor.

L0093–0097 develops an extended consistency challenge concerning natural cognition and the technical faculty wahm. The adversary is accused of accepting the same spontaneous impossibility judgment in one case while rejecting it in a parallel case as 'imagination'. Ibn Taymiyya distinguishes the adversary's faculty of particular nonsensible meanings in sensible objects from universal rational judgments and from ordinary-language 'error'. He further alleges a circle: the existence of an imperceptible self-subsistent thing is used to discredit the spontaneous rule that would rule it out, while rejection of that rule is used to establish the entity's possibility. L0096 argues that theoretical proofs built from basic cognition cannot globally destroy their own basic cognitive grounds. This is not a claim that any individual's first impression must be infallible: its terms are sound fitra and genuinely basic universal judgments, and identifying them remains part of its actual burden.

L0097 compares hermeneutic standards in divine attributes and bodily resurrection. L0097–0098 then distinguishes the beginning or ending of each event token from a beginning or ending of the kind or succession: impermanence of each instance does not establish impermanence of the series. The perpetual but successively consumed provisions of paradise are offered as a revealed illustration. The refusal to permit events without causes is a second reason against an absolute first event emerging without the relevant ground. The copied wording around the first token/type transition appears compressed or defective; the explanatory continuation is retained rather than silently amending it.

L0098–0100 returns to salvation as believing the messenger's reports and obeying his commands, with detailed grammatical analysis of the attribution of dhikr. Revelation is said to give detailed positive perfection and comprehensive denial of defect/likeness. Analogies with the radically different heavenly and earthly instances of identically named pleasures preserve intelligibility while denying equal realization. Crucially, L0100 cites a prayer recognizing divine names reserved to divine knowledge; the source does not claim a human exhaustive enumeration of all divine predicates.

L0100–0104 is a substantial affirmative bridge from necessary self-existence to complete perfection. The source argues as follows. Receiving intrinsic completion from another would make the subject incomplete through itself; if the donor is another dependent, the demand repeats, whereas a fully self-complete donor is the genuine independent source and the recipient is derivative. A removable defect is contingently present; a possible perfection without defect is claimed to belong more properly to the necessary creator than to a possible creature. Completion is then classified: if genuinely inside the thing, its external donation makes the thing itself externally dependent; if wholly external, it is a companion or conjoined thing rather than that thing's own intrinsic perfection. A proposed mutually completed pair is rejected when each can give the needed perfection only after itself being completed by the other. That is a causal prerequisite circle, not an undifferentiated statement that all lawful concomitance is impossible. The text also argues that a giver of perfection is more entitled to it, and that the necessary cannot be surpassed by a possible existent. These perfection-ranking and giving/possessing premises are affirmative argumentative work, not mere decorative epithets, and their exact applicability to each modern productive-role proposal must still be tested.

L0103 explicitly examines both a partless thing and an attributed plurality of components: if the necessary whole is composed, an intrinsic part's dependence on a source outside the whole defeats self-necessity of that whole. L0104 turns this against Avicennian anti-attribute reasoning by disambiguating 'composition', 'part', 'dependence' and 'other': inseparable intrinsic qualities are not something assembled externally or separable in the relevant sense. The source is therefore not simply rejecting every conception of parts while later relying on them; it analyzes the opponent's alternatives conditionally. Exact print apparatus remains unverified.

## V02L0105–0120

L0105–0107 itemizes meanings of part, other and dependence. A part can be an ingredient, separable organ, inseparable essential predicate or theoretically indivisible physical constituent. 'Other' can mean separable/distinct in existence or merely nonidentical in designation. Dependence can mean concomitance, need for an efficient producer, or need for a bearer/recipient. The text expressly notes the Aristotelian four-cause vocabulary and that other schools do not call a recipient an 'illā in that way. An argument may therefore not transfer a consequence from one sense to another. Intrinsic attributes are not independently produced by an antecedent bare essence. L0107 repeats that lacking an efficient cause, lacking a bearer, and lacking every relation are different proposed senses of self-necessity.

L0108–0110 strengthens the perfection argument on both opponents' ontologies: on a strictly simple theory there is no second intrinsic item to receive completion; on an attributed multiplicity theory the whole includes the inseparable perfections, so outside production of one prevents self-necessity of the whole. The causal-circle argument is qualified with unusual precision at L0109: reciprocal accompaniment is allowed, including uncaused divine attributes and two jointly created relational properties such as fatherhood/sonship. What is ruled out is priority-circular completion of each complete productive source by the other's prior productive completion. L0110 extends this to mutual donation of knowledge, power, life, or the enabling factors of cooperation. The stronger assertion that this excludes two world-makers is made here, not assumed absent from the text.

L0111–0114 divides need into obtaining a perfection and preventing its removal, then into inseparable constituents and adventitious features. The direct initial proof concerns inseparable constituents: if a necessary constituent requires an external efficient source, the bearer also depends on that source. Serial efficient dependence and reciprocal efficient sourcehood are rejected. L0114's whole-alone argument says two complete independently sufficient producers of the numerically same effect are incompatible; the proof concerns that strong complete-product attribution and must not be conflated with ordinary co-production of differentiated contributions.

L0114–0119 supplies the central common-source argument. Independent makers with independent activities/products would have separately self-contained products, but the world's objects are pervasively dependent on other objects. The text illustrates this with medieval celestial mechanics and, more accessibly, a human being's dependence on parental material, air, water and sustenance. It moves from product interdependence to nonindependence of the supposed makers of those products. In L0117 realised efficacy itself is a perfection: a source whose efficacy is conditioned by others' motions, conjunctions or materials has that perfection dependently. This must not be weakened into mere possession of some standing capacity. L0118–0119 rejects a cooperative pair by contending that each then lacks intrinsically adequate power and cannot receive it from the other without the rejected priority circle. The source does distinguish co-presence from efficient derivation; the open appraisal concerns whether product coordination must carry the specific productive-source dependence asserted here. Intrinsic adequate capacity rather than merely non-bestowed capacity is doing substantial work.

L0115–0117's physical illustration depends on a premodern nested-sphere system. It argues both from being involuntarily ordered and from inability to generate the requisite diverse/opposed motions alone. This local cosmology is not silently treated as modern empirical fact. The more abstract perfection/product arguments must be separated from its applicability to a particular sphere.

L0119–0120 gives a modal trilemma about a perfection: necessary, impossible, or contingently obtainable. Pure possible perfection cannot be barred from the necessary while possessed by its derivative; contingent acquisition would require a completing outside factor; hence the relevant perfection is intrinsic and necessary. The producer/giver is said to have a prior title to perfection. Whether all candidate traits and productive roles meet the pure-perfection conditions remains an inferential question, not an excuse to overlook the argument. Negative descriptions are then interpreted as entailing positive excellences: no sleep means complete life and sustaining governance, no unknown atom means knowledge, and no fatigue means power. Bare nothingness is not perfection.

## V02L0121–0136

The source's claim of no divine likeness in any concrete perfection (L0121) continues the earlier distinction between a shared intelligible predicate and an identical realized property. It does not undo L0073's meaningful generic sharing. L0122–0123 pairs detailed affirmation with comprehensive denial of defect and equal counterpart, reads al-Samad through complete excellences, and criticizes rival systems of mostly negative predicates. Several logical readings of 'absolute existence' are carefully distinguished: abstracted under a condition of unrestrictedness, under negation of all positive determinations, or without that condition. The universal divided into necessary and possible is not thereby the particular necessary existent.

L0124–0125 allows a qualified sense in which a natural universal exists externally: the concrete exemplars exist, but their external being is not itself universal. It denies either separately floating forms or two self-standing substances called 'animal' and 'rational' inside an individual human. The cognitive composition of a description is distinguished from ontological composition. The critique of essential versus invariably accompanying attributes concerns their alleged uniquely constitutive status and the purportedly failed distinguishing tests; it does not by itself prove that any arbitrary mental conjunction describes an actual human.

L0126–0128 shifts to an intellectual genealogy through Harran and a sustained reading of Abraham's star/moon/sun argument. Ibn Taymiyya contends that the addressed worshippers sought benefit or avoidance of harm from the heavenly bodies rather than believing a visible star literally originated the entire world. The argument targets worship, and aful is disappearance from view, not mere motion or timeless contingency. Waiting for the disappearance would be unnecessary if the operative feature had already been present throughout. Avicenna's gloss in terms of possibility is specifically challenged for erasing the narrative's temporal transition. This gives a methodological control on reconstructed proof premises: narrative timing and the actual interlocutors' beliefs constrain a modernized paraphrase. The historical genealogies remain source attributions, not independently established research findings.

L0128–0131 provides unusually extended linguistic work on body, substance, magnitude, composition and matter/form. Everyday and technical ranges differ; some terms are broader in the schools than in Arabic usage. Grammatical compound expressions, physical assemblages, mental genus/differentia analysis, artificial shapes and natural bodies are distinct cases. Equivocation between them cannot establish an anti-attribute conclusion. 'Matter' is itself used for substrate body, generic extension, the material from which something is made, or a putative beginningless bare substrate; the source refuses to let one sense's case decide another.

L0132–0134 distinguishes dividing a universal into subclasses from dividing an external whole into parts. A predicate of the whole need not be a predicate of its divided head, thigh or shaft, while in some homogeneous cases (water/dates) the same common term does apply. Legal distributions of inheritance, property, booty and shared water, grammar, and arithmetic provide examples. The universal can encompass not-yet-existing or never-thought-of instances without being a numerically common external piece in all of them. This is relevant to any inferred 'common source' or 'common role': common predication, shared component, and common producing subject are separate notions.

L0135 offers the author's physical alternative to both indivisible atoms and endlessly executable physical division: very small parts may remain internally differentiated yet cease to preserve their kind when physically divided. The water/air example is premodern physics, not a present verification. L0136 returns to the task of querying what 'body' means before affirming or denying it and lists disagreements over how many atoms count as a body. Orthodox vocabulary and rational content are kept distinct; true meaning does not automatically license any ambiguous technical label.

## V02L0137–0152

L0137–0138 states an important modal epistemology: a syntactic or mental division into several descriptions does not show that each is externally possible. Imagining a third option between necessary/possible, old/new, self-subsisting/inherent, or existent/nonexistent supplies no possibility proof merely by writing it down. External possibility may be supported by an actual instance, an analogue, or an a fortiori route from a known instance. Merely failing to know impossibility is distinguished from knowing possibility. This bears directly on both source reconstructions and countermodels: a formally describable option is not automatically a metaphysically realizable case, while an allegedly exhaustive division still needs its semantic justification.

L0139–0142 gives a long reported sectarian taxonomy, primarily quoted from al-Ash'ari's Maqalat. Six views involving body, image, light, sense organs or anti-attribute denial are distinguished, followed by the author's historical comments. These extravagant descriptions are reported opponent positions, not Ibn Taymiyya's divine descriptions and not this study's assertions that the historical parties actually held them. No manuscripts of Maqalat or independent chains were collated. L0141 explicitly disambiguates 'Ahl al-Sunna': a broad political contrast with rejection of the three caliphs, versus a narrower doctrinal grouping. The opponent is faulted for transferring a subset's view to the whole broad class. L0142 similarly separates early and later Ash'ari approaches, affirmation, interpretive denial, and suspension.

L0143–0146 reconstructs the body/origination argument at length. Ordinary manuals infer temporal beginning from inability to lack originated events; more careful authors recognize that a particular event and the succession/kind of events differ. The crucial additional premise is impossibility of a beginningless succession, not simply the occurrence of a particular new state. Different formulations use motion/rest, accidents that allegedly do not last two moments, or the four akwan. The same proof pattern is said to appear in several orthodox legal schools and in al-Ash'ari, so the author is not merely grouping every criticized argument by sectarian label. Its downstream consequences for attributes, speech, sight and aboveness are then traced. True differentiated meanings are not removed by renaming them states, judgments, or notions.

L0147–0150 reports further divisions concerning the bearers of the throne, nine accounts of divine knowledge/life/power, and four accounts of will. Some positions tie knowing to prior determination or actual occurrence; others exempt free human actions from foreknowledge; others affirm timeless knowledge. These distinctions matter for later knowledge arguments, but here they remain reported taxonomies within a historical counterexample to the opponent's claim of a single uniformly excellent tradition. The author explicitly identifies his opponent with the later Mu'tazilite-aligned branch.

L0151–0152 turns to permissible reliance on scholars. Following a member of the Prophet's family as a juristic authority can be legitimate on the same terms as following other qualified authorities. The majority permission is presented for someone unable to perform the relevant inference; known opposition to the messenger cannot be overridden by deference. Crucially, competence in inference can be divided by question or field. The author expressly rejects claiming that even a major imam knows every legal verdict by its proof. Orthogonal legal-epistemic limits therefore coexist with the aggressive polemic; no total expert omniscience is assumed.

## V02L0153–0169

L0153–0159 examines divine speech with quoted authorities and detailed semantic control. The author affirms uncreated divine speech while rejecting the inference to one timeless, involuntary speech-token. He combines ongoing freely willed speech with every created thing's temporal origination. At L0154 he explicitly says he does not know which account two named early figures held concerning perpetual voluntary speech; the study preserves that source uncertainty. L0155–0158 denies that earlier 'uncreated' merely meant 'not fabricated': the parties to the old dispute already agreed Muhammad truly conveyed revelation. The actual disagreement was whether speech is an intrinsic divine act or a separately created object. Reports attributed to Ali, Ibn Abbas, Ali b. al-Husayn and Ja'far, with several chains and variants, are read as evidence offered by the author, not newly authenticated reports.

L0159 differentiates transitive uses of jaʿala and treats 'renewed' revelation as compatible with an intrinsic divine act rather than a separately created bearer. A newly commanded prohibition is distinguished from whether each human actually obeys it. This latter distinction also prevents moving from divine command to guaranteed creaturely performance.

L0160–0164 stages extended exchanges among rival Imami, Mu'tazilite, Kullabi and Karrami positions. The disputed premises concern persistence of accidents, whether rest is positive or privative, whether any thing capable of new states must always have them, and whether a timeless inactive creator could begin acting without a new ground. Several alleged rebuttals merely restate the contested premise. L0163 expressly warns that contradiction in a conjunction establishes at most that the conjunction is false; it does not select which conjunct is false or establish a third proposed doctrine. L0164 adds that uncommitted ignorance can be better than a confidently false position. These are explicit controls against turning a successful local refutation into a positive global proof.

L0165–0168 distinguishes three separate claims about a proof: its validity, its being the only possible route, and its being necessary for religious knowledge. The historical fact that the early community did not articulate the later body/accident proof is used against indispensability, while other arguments contest its intrinsic validity. When asked for a replacement, the interlocutor says this refutational stage need not supply one, then points to multiple routes and fitri knowledge of the creator. A local burden of refutation is not confused with the ultimate desirability of a positive account.

L0168–0169 reads Moses' answers to Pharaoh in their argumentative situation. Pharaoh's question is interpreted as public denial of a creator rather than a cooperative request for an Aristotelian essential definition; his claimed supremacy controls the interpretation. The preceding passage also regards him as inwardly knowing despite outward denial, so these psychological and public-discursive descriptions must be kept distinct. The text turns back to the difference between mental essence and external existence and ends the current interval mid-argument.

## V02L0170–0184

L0170 explicitly separates routes: one can establish the creator fitrically, from observed origins, or otherwise; then establish a messenger's truth by multiple methods (miracles are only one route); and then learn the world's origination from that messenger. It also offers a rational route. A supposedly beginningless produced world would require an always-complete producing ground, yet genuinely new occurrences cannot emerge from an unchanged, eternally complete necessitating ground. The consequences are explored through a sustained division between beginningless and first-beginning event series, intrinsic and adventitious new states, and voluntary agency versus impersonal complete necessitation.

L0171–0175 repeatedly emphasizes that one may not invoke unexplained new happenings to save a preferred beginningless substrate while denying the same option to an originated world. The named models include intellects/souls, five eternal principles, and alleged desire in soul giving rise to corporeal attachment. Some inferences establish the failure of an argument for eternity rather than immediately prove its contrary; those dialectical levels are preserved. Historical reports about ancient philosophers remain uncollated.

L0175–0178 asserts that every specifically made effect begins, and that intrinsic necessary attributes are not made effects. The hand/ring example is rejected as evidence for a co-eternal product because it involves a condition or near-condition rather than an originating efficient maker; even apparent simultaneity may conceal contiguous succession. L0177–0178 disputes al-Razi's claim that the controversy is merely verbal and his purported agreement among philosophers and theologians, pointing to differences over final causes, producing causes, will and intrinsic qualities. Temporal origination of a product and volitional character of its origin are treated as independently arguable though related claims.

The 003→004 seam at V02L0178–0179 directly continues `يقولون بهذه` with `الطرق`, with no intervening body omission. L0179–0180 surveys four combinations of the event-series and body-origination questions and recognizes routes beginning with dependence and creator before world-origination. This source-order difference is relevant: not every argument presupposes a completed proof of a first temporal event. Some extreme positions are described as perhaps hypothetical sophistic constructions rather than views actually held by identifiable historical parties.

L0181–0184 begins the response to 'able over all objects of power'. It distinguishes the substantial claim of power over every intrinsically possible object from the tautological 'able over whatever one is able to do'. Intrinsic impossibility (e.g. the same thing existent and nonexistent) differs from nonoccurrence fixed by knowledge, declaration or decree. The latter may remain possible in itself and within power even though it will not occur. The author applies this to a human who is capable of believing or performing pilgrimage despite foreknown nonperformance. This is not foreknowledge error: actual occurrence contrary to true foreknowledge is excluded, while intrinsic possibility and capacity are retained under a different description. The distinction constrains any later inference from settled foreknowledge to lack of power or from capacity to actual alternative occurrence with all facts held fixed.

## V02L0185–0200

L0185 completes the distinction between omnipotence and a vacuous bounded self-description. The author's positive thesis includes producing freely performed human actions, while intrinsic contradictions and 'creating one's own equal' are excluded as no possible object. L0186–0189 then carefully types attribution: creating an act in another bearer does not make the creator the one who eats, prays, lies, has the color or suffers the defect. The bearer and creator relations differ. Conversely, the servant is a genuine actor, not merely a verbal 'acquirer' whose real act belongs to someone else. Ibn Taymiyya rejects collapse of act with produced object; he defends distinct divine creating and human action. Surveyed minority accounts involving created enabling causes are not all the author's view. He again records limited knowledge about whether a particular position belongs to Ibn Kullab himself.

L0189–0193 distinguishes threatened punishment, possible forgiveness and permanent punishment for grave sins across several schools, then presents two senses of divine injustice. One makes injustice intrinsically impossible by definition; the other treats certain actions as possible objects of power which divine justice excludes from actual performance. The latter is positively supported by not loading another's sins on someone or withholding earned good. The author distinguishes reward as divinely promised generosity from payment owed to a laborer whose labor benefits an employer. The divine reward-giver also supplies the capacities and assistance by which obedience occurs. Human good or evil does not enlarge or reduce the divine kingdom. Infant eschatology, fitra, hadith variants and a rejected attribution to Ahmad are substantive orthogonal matters; the supplied report chains and authenticity verdicts are not independently audited.

L0194–0195 holds that decreed wrongdoing does not erase responsible human action or just punishment, and denies a flat analogy between what a human bystander may permit and divine permission. Most interlocutors appeal to wisdom and purpose, while some deny explanatory final causes; the source distinguishes those positions rather than treating 'Sunni' as uniform. It also notes that gharad can misleadingly suggest a discreditable motive or need even where hikma is affirmed. An effect's being created 'at' an occasion is distinguished from acting 'through' a cause and 'for' an end.

L0196–0199 examines vision versus encompassing perception. The author reports broad agreement on seeing God in the hereafter while distinguishing disagreement about the Prophet's earthly experience. Seeing part of a sky, army or city is not encompassing it; being caught can occur without sight; Moses' companions saw pursuers while he denied they would be overtaken. L0197 carefully identifies the limited burden of a counterinterpretation. L0199 says that an available alternative meaning suffices to defeat an alleged decisive inference to nonvision, while a positive inference to vision needs additional linguistic, exegetical and other evidence. The argument that bare invisibility is no praise and nonencompassability expresses greatness is a further positive argument, not simply the weaker ambiguity point.

The electronic wording at L0198 appears to omit a negative in `يرى و يحاط به` where its surrounding reasoning contrasts vision with lack of encompassing; L0199's `فبطل ... على الرؤية` likewise appears defective given the stated target of denying vision. These are preserved unresolved, not silently normalized or quoted as an exact critical edition. The clear argumentative distinctions can be described with this transcription limitation.

L0199–0200 differentiates an existing containing 'direction' from a privative designation for beyond the created world. Created enclosure or support is denied, but aboveness is affirmed. An imagined descent trapped between created layers is specifically rejected. The author explains some disagreements as terminological while recognizing real substantive disagreements beneath other uses of the same word.

## V02L0201–0216

L0201–0203 compares particular theories of vision and aboveness, reported positions of early and later Ash'aris, and whether the relevant attributes are accessible rationally or only by report. The author explicitly announces two different aims (L0202): establish that a criticized affirmative school is closer to the truth than the denialists, then separately state the truth without defending a party. L0203 declines to assume the stronger proposition that absolutely everything existent can be seen; he records it as al-Ash'ari's position and distinguishes the narrower self-subsistent-object claim. Reported extensions to five senses are likewise attributed and differentiated.

L0203–0205 offers the positive perfection argument that visible manifestation requires positive reality rather than sheer nonbeing, so fuller existence is more eligible for vision. Failure to see can stem from the perceiver's incapacity rather than a defect in the object. Sunlight, Moses' encounter and perception of angels illustrate that distinction; comparison of vision does not imply likeness of the seen objects. Whether the positive-reality ordering is sufficient for every proposed perception conclusion remains an actual premise to evaluate.

There is substantial source disorder at V02L0206: it begins in a later objection (`ولا مماسة...`), repeats a phrase about two cognitive judges, interrupts at `الوهم إنما`, then resumes verses and a prior objection, leaving the end to join L0207. This is not normal paragraph flow. The entire retained text was read, but no clean authorial sequence or complete original wording is certified there. It has not been repaired from a denied witness or memory.

L0207–0209 again uses an a fortiori consistency challenge about rejecting innate judgments as wahm. If an even more difficult nonspatial existent is admitted, a less revisionary rival cannot be rejected merely by an appeal to the same natural judgment. Conversely, accepting that judgment rejects the denialist base premise. This is expressly a comparative argument. L0209 warns that a theory inheriting an actually false premise still cannot be fully defended as truth even if better than its rival.

L0210–0213 is a major ethical and epistemic control on the polemic. Truth spoken by a Jew, Christian or sectarian opponent must be accepted; only the false part may be rejected. Answering falsehood with another falsehood is prohibited, even for a preferred doctrinal side. The author distinguishes a teaching sequence that first exposes internal inconsistency or relative weakness, prompting reconsideration, from the subsequent task of explaining the false premises and establishing the true alternative. Every party is said to combine truth and falsehood. The extended examples of prophethood, succession and spurious divinization are comparative tests of evidential standards, not a license to adopt the hypothetical false parallel.

L0213–0214 uses analogy as a mirror against attachment bias and connects syllogistic and analogical reasoning under the idea of a just measure. L0215 returns to the direct issue: a modus-tollens-shaped argument may fail because either premise is false or because its middle term changes sense. For 'direction', an existing container makes the universal visibility premise contestable, while a privative beyond changes the denial of divine direction. The external surface of the world is a counterexample to requiring another containing world to be visible. L0216 starts a comparable analysis of hayyiz and responds to claims that Muslim theologians never argued against separate immaterial substances. These are actual reasoned contrasts, not a generic rejection of formal argument.

## V02L0217–0232

L0217–0223 scrutinizes an al-Razi argument and al-Tusi reply about location and the meaning of 'in'. Spatial interpenetration, occupying a place and an accident inhering in a bearer have different structures; a single preposition does not supply their identity. Ibn Taymiyya agrees that equivocation matters but faults both sides for attributing one definition to theologians or assuming hayyiz is identical with a philosophical account of place. Quotes from al-Baqillani, al-Isfarayini and al-Juwayni distinguish spatial occupancy from a further separately existing item. A privative region is not nonexistence of the object in it. Two philosophical accounts of place, a bare receptacle and the containing surface, are separately discussed. The issue is not resolved by simply counting a third object unless that object is actually part of the view being criticized.

L0220–0222 again distinguishes incessant successive creation from any individually co-eternal created object. Ordinary terms can refer to a supporting or containing site, or merely something below without dependence. Poetry attributed to Hassan and Ibn Rawaha and a reported domestic anecdote are invoked; these are read as the author's evidentiary materials without authenticating them. The author says the particular philosophical definitions are not what the early community meant. He also brackets the separate controversy over interpenetration because the relevant use of 'in a region' does not require it. This is a local relevance judgment within a thoroughly read semantic excursus, not a reason for the present study to omit the excursus.

L0224–0228 introduces and enumerates nine speech theories rather than the two to five usually acknowledged in limited textbooks: emanated prophetic meanings; separately created divine speech; one timeless intrinsic meaning; simultaneous eternal letters/sounds; a first intrinsic speaking after silence; beginningless type with successively willed speech-tokens; speech reduced to renewed knowledge/will; combinations of intrinsic meaning and external production; and polysemy between those two. The sixth is identified as the hadith/sunna account. Schools, variants and uncertainty about authorial development are preserved rather than conflated. The extensive list of earlier source collections is a bibliography asserted by the author, not a record that this reader independently inspected them.

L0229–0231 reiterates that the dispute concerns creation, not whether the Quran was forged, then draws a useful legal limit: truth is not confined to the four famous jurists. A view outside their four positions may be preferable when held by other sound early authorities; if a rival sect agrees with that preferable view, its position in that question is preferable. This allowance is distinct from the author's sweeping claim against uniquely sectarian doctrines. No blanket conversion of sect labels into truth-values is justified by this paragraph.

L0231–0232 disambiguates 'new speech' into externally produced speech, the first occurrence of speaking after not speaking, and newly occurring tokens of a beginningless capacity/activity. The attribution argument resumes: producing speech in a tree, stone, bodily organ or another human does not make the producing source the speaking bearer. Otherwise all creaturely discourse would be divine speech. The quoted unitive inference from Ibn Arabi begins at the interval's end and awaits its continuation.

## V02L0233–0248

L0233–0236 rejects identifying all created discourse with God's own speech, since that would include Pharaoh's false claim of divinity. The bearer-of-quality rule is applied consistently across speech, will, love, knowledge and action. Three definitions of a speaker are distinguished: merely making detached speech; possessing an intrinsic but involuntary speech; and voluntarily speaking with speech intrinsic to the speaker. The third is endorsed. 'Attribute of action' cannot simply mean a detached object while still functioning as an intrinsic predicate. The survey distinguishes creation from created object and lists several competing accounts of where/how the creating occurs.

L0237–0242 combines what the author takes to be the strongest positive points of opponents: speech occurs voluntarily and successively, yet is intrinsic to its speaker. 'Accidents/events' is disambiguated from disease, defect or exhaustion; denying the latter does not deny every intrinsic new action. A long staged argument acknowledges that one may have inconsistently accepted a rival's premise without thereby being required to abandon one's true claim. The appropriate repair is to retract the mistaken premise, not preserve it by discarding truth (L0239–0240). This is not permission to ignore consistency; it specifies which independently supported commitments should guide correction.

L0240–0242 insists that beginningless voluntary activity differs from an individually eternal cosmos necessitated by a nonvolitional source. Never having been able to act is denied as an eternal defect. Each particular created effect still follows its particular freely willed intrinsic act. Eternal perfection is thus not equated with eternal actuality of every token act or a frozen total output.

L0243–0246 surveys further responses to 'if creating is new, it needs another creating'. Some say power and will suffice to ground the intrinsic act without another act of creating it; others allow a sequence of acts while excluding an infinite hierarchy of derivative producers. The text distinguishes three views of past/future succession, attributing perpetual succession to the early tradition and several philosophical groups for different reasons. L0247–0248 introduces the opponent's total prophetic infallibility claim and its rationale in trust, usefulness and avoiding repulsion; the response begins by noting internal disagreement among Imamis. This new section is not yet covered beyond its introduction.

## V02L0249–0266

The quoted taxonomy distinguishes a theory permitting prophetic error because revelation corrects it while demanding stronger imamly protection, from one requiring comprehensive protection of both. Ibn Taymiyya counters alleged inconsistencies between those human protections and doctrines permitting divine revision through newly learned facts. His recurring argument is against elevating dependent recipients above their source; reported sectarian and comparative-religious accusations remain his attributions.

L0253–0257 identifies the agreed protection as not being left in error in conveying revelation, and denies that trustworthy post-commission conveyance requires a sinless entire pre-commission biography. Repentance can produce a superior eventual state; former unbelieving Companions can exceed their descendants born Muslim. L0256 explicitly forbids intentionally committing wrong as a strategy to gain the goods of repentance, comparing it to deliberately provoking a lion or drinking poison in hope of a cure. The remedial benefit does not turn its bad occasion into an authorized means.

L0257–0261 argues that truthful acknowledgment, humility and correction can increase trust, whereas insisting on unqualified personal impeccability can undermine it. Divine self-sufficiency and human excellence through admitted dependence are contrasted at L0259; a predicate's perfection status is role-sensitive. The historical examples about Umar, Khawarij and prophetic narratives are not independently verified. The author discusses the gharaniq report as widely transmitted and addresses the allegation of misleading conveyance by the corrective guarantee; this study neither authenticates the controversial report nor suppresses its role in his argument. Abrogation, allegedly more alienating to some hearers than correction, is used against treating every possible audience aversion as dispositive.

L0262–0264 distinguishes Joseph's passing impulse and restraint from committed wrongdoing, attributes `وما أبرئ نفسي` to the woman rather than Joseph, and uses narrative chronology to support that attribution. Other prophets' narrated faults are accompanied by repentance. The exemplar is followed in what is retained and approved, not what is corrected or abrogated. Thus practical emulation does not entail approval of every uncorrected-looking token in an isolated report.

L0265–0266 begins a taxonomy of prophecy based on underlying theories of divine acts: sheer appointment under unconstrained will; reward for prior meritorious work; and philosophical natural emanation according to human preparedness. The author's own position is not yet exhausted by these reported alternatives. Source uncertainty: L0265's wording concerning prophecy as a positive quality appears to lack a negation given the immediately following claim that it is merely relational; no silent repair is adopted.

## V02L0267–0282

L0267–0272 gives the author's favored account of prophecy: divine selection of a person endowed with fitting intellectual and religious excellences, not mere morally indifferent appointment, earned payment or a natural visionary technique. Prophets belong among the highest righteous, not the wicked. A significant attribution warning at L0270 says an implication of a school is not automatically a position its members affirm; this qualifies hostile reconstructions as well as sympathetic ones.

The wisdom-based approach is said to permit rational discrimination between a truthful, morally excellent prophet and a lying diviner. Khadija's recognition of Muhammad's prior truthfulness, kinship care, hospitality and assistance to the vulnerable is explicitly treated as rational reassurance before she had a revealed premise establishing the point. The source's premise about wise divine treatment of such a person is doing substantive work. Character, observed conduct, claim-content and comparative evidence complement rather than simply reduce to a miracle sign. No claim is made here that our reading independently proves every historical report or that good character alone is an infallible universal detector.

L0272–0276 differentiates lesser corrected fault from a stable identity as wicked, and addresses why forgiven prophets can still decline the supreme intercession: humility, fear of acting before permission, and recognition of the designated rank need not imply an unforgiven defect. L0277 expressly rules out deliberate prophetic falsehood. It distinguishes indirect expressions from deliberate lies, and notes legal concerns about admitting a previously false witness or hadith transmitter even after professed repentance because such profession can itself be instrumental. That practical evidence problem is not erased by the spiritual claim that genuine repentance restores a person.

L0278–0282 returns to achieved excellence rather than merely the initial condition. The constructive benefits attributed to repentance include humility, care for others, increased caution, and stronger reliance on help. Historical examples include Usama's later refusal to kill someone professing Islam and Ka'b's commitment to truth. This is consistent with L0256's explicit ban on deliberately seeking sin as a means to repentance. These moral-psychological and legal details are retained as part of the continuous body, not omitted as orthogonal to metaphysics.

## V02L0283–0300

L0283 expressly qualifies the preceding praise of repentance: not every penitent becomes better than every never-offender. Some surpass an earlier state, some regain it, some fall short; comparisons with other people likewise vary. The text's qualification prevents turning examples into an indiscriminate ranking rule.

L0284–0296 is a lengthy practical-theological excursus on graves, shrines, travel, prayer and worship. It distinguishes praying for the deceased and greeting them from seeking their intervention, treating a site as specially efficacious, vows, or pilgrimage-like travel. It offers chains, prophetic reports, early practice, disputed legal details and the claimed rationale of preventing routes to worship of others. Differences in facing the grave while greeting versus facing the prayer-direction when praying are recorded, as are distinctions among schools. These are Ibn Taymiyya's legal/doctrinal positions and consensus claims, not newly researched present-day legal guidance or a finding that no historical disagreement existed.

L0291–0294 differentiates divine and messengerly rights through close syntax: obedience, faith and love can have coordinated objects, while sufficiency, worship and ultimate seeking are assigned uniquely to God. Provision by divine decree is distinguished from sanctioned lawful giving. The two principles of worship are exclusive divine object and the prescribed rather than innovated manner. The passage thus extends beyond cosmological uniqueness into normative devotion; the transition cannot be replaced by a claim that a bare causal theorem already supplies every practical obligation.

L0295 qualifies a report about marketplaces by the local class of places compared. L0296 asserts that many shrine identifications lack trustworthy historical evidence and that striking appearances or fulfilled requests can mislead. The supernatural explanations offered are the author's, not independently observed or authenticated occurrences in this study. Their argumentative role is clear: unusual efficacy or an impressive experience does not automatically certify the worshipper's interpretation or the proposed recipient's status.

L0297–0300 returns to imams and transmission. Despite the surrounding harsh polemic, L0298–0299 explicitly recognizes many Twelvers as sincerely Muslim and distinguishes leaders claimed to know an esoteric programme from uninformed adherents; the source must not be flattened into an undifferentiated verdict on all persons. It contests direct exclusive inheritance of knowledge by showing the named family scholars learning through ordinary teachers and chains. L0300 distinguishes general moral fallibility from intentional falsification in a specific transmission practice, appealing to examination of reports and claimed observed reliability of Companions. These are proposed warrant mechanisms, not proof that every isolated attribution is already verified by our study.

## V02L0301–0316

L0301 separates imperfect memory from deliberate fabrication. The source then rejects a shortcut from family descent to exhaustive historical knowledge: if the later imams learned through teachers, those channels can be investigated directly, and exceptional knowledge follows exceptional attention and acquisition rather than kinship alone. L0302 distinguishes a scholar's transmitted report from his own judgment, recognizes revision and intra-family disagreement, and asks what a perpetually required inaccessible imam adds if earlier reliable transmission already suffices.

L0303–0304 challenges spurious attribution using chronology, contents and known authorship of works assigned to Ja'far. The dating of the Ikhwan al-Safa and the listed names are source claims requiring independent checking before use as settled history. Even while making broad polemical accusations, L0304 concedes that Kufa contained many major trustworthy transmitters; the practical recommendation is discrimination in a contaminated evidential environment, not that every member of a region is false. The analogy is testing coinage and identifying trustworthy persons amid many unreliable ones.

L0305–0313 distinguishes rejection of analogy from sectarian identity and outlines three operations: applying an agreed general criterion to particular cases (tahqiq al-manat), refining the operative ground, and deriving it. The first is said to be unavoidable/common; the latter two are disputed. Examples include whether a drink is intoxicant, a game gambling, a vow an oath or binding contract, and a transformed/absorbed impurity still in a prohibited category. These do not collapse into merely restating a text. The author argues that disciplined inquiry by an informed scholar can be superior to unreliable transmission from a fallible authority; the relative reliability and competence are actual premises.

L0314–0316 compares exaggerated claims about infallible imams, spiritual teachers and political rulers. It distinguishes context-bound obedience in public adjudication from treating a ruler's words as universal law or certifying all his acts. David's accountability is used against the notion that political appointment guarantees impunity. The author again qualifies the book's local objective: establishing relative superiority is not a claim that every view held within the preferred broad class is correct. Comparative expressions about good and evil can address an interlocutor's scale without granting that both alternatives are genuinely good.

## V02L0317–0334

A new opponent quotation at L0318 charges Ash'aris with multiple eternals and a deficient essence completed by outside qualities, comparing this to Christian plurality. L0320 says the quoted al-Razi 'objection' was one he reported and rejected, not his own endorsed objection. This is an explicit speaker-control lesson: presence of an argument in an author's book does not establish adoption.

L0321–0328 uses an attributed response of Ahmad and ordinary examples to distinguish one qualified bearer from several independent beings. A named subject includes its intrinsic qualities; 'additional to essence' can mean additional to an artificially stripped conception, not added outside the actual attributed bearer. The text distinguishes the majority attribute theory from a further ahwal theory that separately posits being-knowing and knowledge. Its criticisms and charitable reconstructions track that difference. L0326–0328 expressly denies that beginningless attributes imply extra gods or independently existing ancient objects.

L0329–0334 makes intrinsic conditionality particularly precise. Knowledge can condition being knowing; life can condition knowledge; both remain intrinsic and inseparable. The essence's 'necessitating' a quality here means that it cannot exist without that quality, not that an antecedent essence produces it by an efficient act. Inferential movement from the subject's being knowing to knowledge is likewise not a causal production story. The source explicitly warns that 'dependence' can misleadingly suggest an external giver. This strengthens the central study control: necessary attribute, independently subsisting necessary bearer, semantic entailment, epistemic evidence and efficient origination are distinct relations. The formal predicate 'exists at every supplied world' alone selects none of these causal/metaphysical roles.

## V02L0335–0352

L0335–0337 continues to separate an attributed actual bearer from a mentally stripped bare essence. The latter is not an independently realized entity to which a donor later adds the qualities. Three-way intrinsic implication among essence, qualities and derived descriptions is allowed.

L0338–0343 rejects assimilation of divine attribute theory to Christian plurality. The author first distinguishes Quranic accusations of multiple divinities from a philosopher's formula 'three eternals'; then argues that a quality cannot detach from its bearer and become an independently creating and sustaining agent. His exegesis involving Christ and Mary and his description of Christian aqanim are polemical source arguments, not a freshly verified neutral account of every Christian trinitarian position. Most importantly, L0341 explicitly denies a human exhaustive numbered list of divine attributes. The general all-perfection commitment does not certify a finite inventory of humanly articulated predicates.

L0342–0343 also exposes sectarian evidential contamination: anti-attribute polemicists allegedly fabricated a story about Ibn Kullab's Christian sister, which some hadith-oriented critics then repeated against him despite supporting the very attribute affirmation for which it had originally been fabricated. A true criticism of one feature does not validate every hostile anecdote or justify rejecting the target's true statements. The same warning is applied to replies against Salimiyya.

L0344 begins an opponent quotation assigning elaborate bodily defects and contact to an unspecified group. L0346–0351 answers with a long nested catalogue, much of it reported through al-Ash'ari, of extreme claims about divine bodies, incarnation, human divinization, perpetual new prophets, abrogated obligations, symbolic reinterpretations and delegated creation. The minute sect labels, reported executions, poems and genealogy are preserved in the read body; they are not authenticated by our reading. Their placement is a comparative polemic against selective accusation. The author also notes analogues among some Sufis, so the presentation must not be reduced to a claim that no such doctrine ever appeared elsewhere. L0352 then demands an identifiable target among recognized Sunni authorities rather than allowing an undefined derogatory collective label to do evidential work.

## V02L0353–0370

L0353–0355 requires naming both the attributed proponent and the transmitter; otherwise a vague hostile label such as Hashwiyya cannot identify a stable thesis or group. An ignorant affiliate's claim is not automatically the doctrine of recognized authorities. This norm is important when assessing the surrounding author's own reported taxonomies as well as his opponent's.

L0356–0358 distinguishes denial, suspension and relativization of objective truth. Refusing to assert either contradictory proposition does not make both propositions false: epistemic noncommitment and ontic absence differ. Shared meanings do not require identical concrete properties, and denying likeness cannot coherently erase every intelligible predicate. Extravagant bodily accounts are expressly rejected, while the all-perfection claim is reiterated without a finite catalogue.

L0359–0362 returns to natural philosophy, physical transformation, spatial indication and the status of immaterial substances. The text's account of angels includes actual distinct agents with narrated encounters, ascent/descent and communication, not merely psychological capacities or philosophical intellects. These scriptural/report-based commitments are part of the source's conception; this reading does not externally verify angelic events or translate them into a purely metaphorical theory.

L0363–0369 again parses the anti-composition proof. Essential attributes do not entail assembly from formerly separate pieces. The proof of an independent producer does not prove lack of all intrinsic structure or concomitance. At L0365 necessary can mean uncaused, needing no separate other, or independently subsisting; attributes meet the first senses while the attributed bearer meets the last. L0367 explains that necessary 'through itself' is not self-creation: the subject does not efficiently produce its own necessity. A whole's requiring its own parts is not an external productive circle. L0369 further distinguishes epistemic discriminability (knowing one feature without another) from separable external beings. This is the most direct later reinforcement of the intended necessary-attribute/bearer distinction.

L0369–0370 treats 'change' as another potentially equivocal term: illness or destructive alteration differs from hearing a prayer, perceiving a created occurrence or freely addressing Moses. Naming both change does not prove the latter defective. The closing discussion differentiates a possibly true intended meaning from an unlicensed or linguistically misleading term, even where both disputants reject the same actual composition.

## V02L0371–0388

L0371–0374 argues that familiar public vocabulary cannot have always meant a contested microscopic theory which ordinary speakers neither conceive nor know. Saying one body is thicker than another does not establish that its everyday meaning is 'composed of indivisible atoms'. The familiar predicate and the theoretical explanation of its instances are distinct. Several alternative atom-count definitions are again recorded.

L0375–0382 explains how to handle contested language: affirm or deny the substantive meaning with clarification, preserve a common agreed paraphrase where possible, and use unfamiliar technical words only when needed and with disambiguating context. The author is therefore not imposing a complete ban on every non-scriptural term. He applies the method to hayyiz and jiha, distinguishing containment in a created thing from separation beyond created things. The repeated visibility argument tests the sense of the middle term in both premises rather than simply denouncing logical form. Historical assertions about religious schools' origins and textual evidence remain source claims.

L0383–0385 explicitly concedes that agreement on perfect divinity does not settle which particular predicate's affirmation or negation is the defect, nor the method of establishing that classification. A consensus on the general formula cannot be invoked as consensus on the disputed instances. The author also argues that the texts for actual perfections are clearer than the secondary texts authorizing an appeal to consensus. This is directly relevant to a modern finite role classification: the broad perfection rule does not spare us warrant for the contested specific assignment.

L0385–0388 distinguishes formerly assembled or actually separable components from inseparable differentiated qualities. Bodies growing together, rather than first existing as detached organs, illustrate why not every material unity is assembled in the same historical way, though it may still be divisible. Epistemic discrimination is then separated from distinction in the subject itself: knowing existence before knowledge, or power before will, is uncontroversial in human inquiry; whether the real attributes are differentiated is a further ontological issue. Neither meaningful distinction nor separate knowledge alone proves possible detachment.

## V02L0389–0405

L0389–0391 offers a notable charitable attribution principle. The author doubts reports that intelligent opponents literally denied motion or sensible qualities: error in theology need not erase genuine competence in medicine, mathematics or ordinary observation. He explicitly praises the medical knowledge of Muhammad b. Zakariyya al-Razi despite condemning his theology. He reconstructs a more plausible terminological thesis and says he later found textual confirmation in Abu al-Husayn's account. This is source-sensitive repair of an attributed position, not permission for us to silently repair our received transcription. The 004→005 seam directly continues `مرادهم هو` with `هذا المعنى` at L0390–0391.

L0391–0397 discusses the different perceptual channels for taste, color and smell, and the disputed bearer of knowledge, life and will in the human whole. It considers whether attribution extends beyond the local organ, whether every atom bears the quality, and whether an alleged indivisible soul is needed. Mathematical points, lines, surfaces and bare numbers are distinguished from concrete measured/countable things. Indivisible cognition is not established by first assuming an unproved externally indivisible object. The examples of integrated bodily life and sight explore how connection can be required for a capacity without each separated piece retaining it. This is a substantive premodern mind/body discussion; the study does not certify its physiology or quietly omit it.

L0397 notes that a catalogue of many views can still omit the true one. L0399–0405 then systematically treats divine/created names: mere verbal equivocation, priority/analogy, true predication in only one class, and bare proper names without meanings. The favored account retains common intelligible content without reifying a numerically shared property. 'Existence', 'essence', 'reality' and 'self' must be compared at matching levels of generality; mixing generic existence with individual essence produces a spurious extra constituent. A concrete instance has a particular property analogous to another's, not the very same external universal parcel. The five logical universals and genus/differentia structure are mental tools rather than externally assembled pieces. L0405 extends the point to the relationships among writing, utterance and meaning: general linguistic signs express general mental content without requiring a general external object.

Textual caution: phrases around L0402's division of answers and L0404's shared/distinguishing contrast appear defective; they have not been silently corrected. The surrounding extended argument establishes the intended distinctions more securely than those isolated strings.

## V02L0406–0421

L0406–0409 continues the semantics of external instances and mental universals, using the image of sun/moon in a mirror to show how ordinary true reference need not relocate the object itself. Conceiving a triangle before establishing an external instance does not establish a separately existing essence beside that instance. L0408–0409 gives three levels explicitly: the divine particular quality, the created particular quality, and the common intelligible predicate. Some consequences concern that common predicate wherever instantiated (e.g. an attribute requires a bearer); others follow from the particular bearer. This is a disciplined typed comparison, not universal permission to transfer every creaturely property to God.

L0410–0415 examines sect labels and the historical role of Ahmad's ordeal. It says a preferred school can contain false positions which should be rejected without treating them as its constitutive norm. The alleged necessity of a consequence must not silently become the subject's affirmed doctrine. L0413 requires proof both that a morally charged category is valid and that a person actually falls under it. L0415 permits technical language or translation for understanding, provided the communicator knows both systems and preserves the true content. Merely repeating familiar religious words to someone who cannot understand them is not the ideal method.

L0416–0418 returns to particular alleged corporealist claims. The author distinguishes David al-Jawaribi from David al-Ta'i and explicitly leaves open whether the error arose in the opponent, an earlier transmitter, or the copy before him. He also cautions that hostile sources may have exaggerated Muqatil's position; lack of hadith reliability does not erase genuine exegetical learning. This local restraint is important alongside the text's broader polemical allegations.

L0419–0421 records ecstatic and antinomian claims across multiple social/religious groups. It gives a psychological explanation for some alleged direct vision: inner illumination can absorb outward attention and be mistaken for an external object. A felt dialogue or vividly perceived image therefore need not establish an external divine speaker. The author additionally proposes deceptive spirit encounters in some cases; those are his explanatory claims, not events verified by this study. Distinguishing inner representation, external occurrence and correct identity remains essential to his own attestation discussion.

## V02L0422–0437

L0422–0423 distinguishes not knowing a claimed proponent, known critical disagreement about a report, and contrary wording variants. It does not let both affirmative and negative versions be silently joined into one proposition. A disputed report's rejection by other members of a broad school also prevents assigning it to the whole school. L0424–0429 quotes and rejects sensational anonymous stories, states possible false-attribution versus obscure-person alternatives, and distinguishes alleged earthly appearances from the specifically transmitted descent reports and their further doctrinal interpretations. The author names reports judged fabricated, but this study has not independently verified those judgments or chains.

L0430–0437 closes the volume by returning to the opponent's claim that 'in a direction' entails temporal beginning and dependence. The reply distinguishes a needed existing receptacle from noncontaining aboveness, and appeals to basic cognition with an anecdote about al-Hamadhani. L0433 carefully analyzes a theoretical proof's failure: it may fail through one premise, another, or their arrangement, without falsifying all basic premises. A derivative proof cannot undermine all the basic warrant on which it relies. L0434–0435 asks for actual evidence rather than restating an implication and insists that the creator of the throne, its bearers, their power and their acts does not thereby require those effects as independent suppliers. The possibility of being voluntarily borne is distinguished from existential need for the bearer. The closing objections again target disputed premises in the body/event/direction syllogism. L0437 contains the explicit second-volume colophon and announces the third.
