# Darʾ taʿāruḍ, volume 9: continuous electronic reading

## Method and source limits
Read the body in source order, retaining philosophical, exegetical, legal, historical and epistemological material without keyword selection. Stable locators use the original downloaded filename number and physical newline number, e.g. `015:43`. Headings are transcription/editorial organization unless source voice establishes otherwise. Website wrappers are not book text. The body boundary manifest is separate.

This is electronic textual study, not print-page collation or complete Rashād Sālim apparatus certification. The manifest verifies the start against Shamela 9:3 and the subsequent-volume opening against 10:3; no unverified intermediate page numbers will be assigned. Boundary-only tail previews of 015/016 do not constitute substantive sequential reading credit. Whole-volume completion must await the entire body and a reflection pass.

## First pass 01: 014:940–956; 015:20–110
- 014:941 is Ibn Taymiyya's framing: claims that knowledge occurs without reason may have a correct sense. 014:942–956 is the quoted account of Ibn Abī Mūsā, not an undifferentiated authorial doctrine. It permits inference from produced things to a producer but says identification/names require hearing. It compares rational and sensory faculties, locates their assigned ranges, denies access to divine whatness/how, and holds prophetic instruction remains operative during intervals between prophets. 014:953 calls knowledge a gift but inferential rather than necessary, because necessary knowledge would be shared by rational people. The author has not yet endorsed that last inference.
- 015:21–27 separates rationally available general recognition, revealed identifying information, and inaccessible divine how/whatness. A syllogism alone supplies a general description, not acquaintance with an individuated referent. Crucial distinction at 015:25: a proof's failing to identify its subject does not license denying that subject's real individuation. The criticism of negative theology at 24 is ontological, not simply epistemic: the alleged merely universal object cannot exist externally as such.
- 015:28–43 explores individual identification through perception, testimony, distinctive description and effects. Recognition of the Prophet can occur without firsthand sight (29–35); legal analogies include property boundaries/testimony, specific versus generic sale, debts and sacrificial obligations (36–42). A specific item remains specific when the buyer does not know its identity. These legal details explain reference and substitutability, not divine causal composition. At 43 a uniquely identifying divine property may let reason know the referent as a unique individual; this explicitly qualifies an absolute claim that hearing alone individuates.
- 015:44–61 proposes innate orientation, above-world uniqueness, inference and revelation as routes to identification, while recording dispute over which attributes reason can establish. It distinguishes genuine unseen reality from alleged purely intelligible universals (48–53): unseen means absent from present perception, not necessarily imperceptible. 56–61 charitably narrows Ibn Abī Mūsā's claim to revealed names, special predicates, revealed guidance of rational identification, or most people's actual route; alternative interpretations are not all simply declared true.
- 015:62–80 analyzes the word “reason”: native faculty and its basic deliverances do not suffice for saving faith. Differences of belief do not show reason cannot know a creator. Rational demonstrations may yield knowledge without settling public disagreement, because people contest even necessary premises (65–68). 69–70 quotes ʿAbd al-Wahhāb al-Maqdisī; 72–80 is evaluation. The author calls reason a condition of knowledge and obligation rather than a sufficient producer of saving cognition (80).
- 015:81–102 distinguishes inherited report, mental faculty, acquired knowledge, and practical exercise. He flags the weak attribution of the Muʿādh saying to the Prophet (82), rather than treating every quoted report as sound. “Reason” as beneficial action upon knowledge includes more than bare knowledge (91–98). Three senses of taʾwīl are separated (102): interpretation of meaning; actual external reality/how; and reversing a text's apparent probability. Known meaning does not imply knowledge of divine modality.
- 015:103–110 begins providence/guidance: ʿAbd al-Wahhāb likens reason to reading divinely inscribed belief; his father reports knowing God through God. Ibn Taymiyya's reply at 108 directs this against a voluntarist account that equalizes all divine aids and makes the believer independently originate the difference. 109–110 press why unequal outcomes occur and what requests for guidance ask for. Argument remains unfinished here.

## Reflection 01 (distinct from first-pass coverage)
1. For source-mode assessment, do not collapse abstract demonstration, unique individuation, and perceptual acquaintance. 015:25 and 43 block moving directly from limited proof to a nonexistent/unindividuated source. Conversely 015:22–27 warns that demonstrating some necessary producer alone has not identified every divine attribute.
2. The strongest opening limit is epistemic: native rational capacity is a necessary enabling condition, not sufficient saving knowledge (015:80). It is not evidence that God needs an extrinsic receptive material or an independent knower to exist.
3. Descriptive distinctiveness can pick out an individual without inspection. This helps the methodological issue of “full-role” description only partially: the passage supplies routes to reference, not a proof that a finite list exhausts all source roles.
4. Real-particular versus mental-universal distinctions are firm; causal nonduplication, material receptivity, and complete causal-role coverage have not yet been established in this opening. No source-mode verdict is justified from this tranche alone.

## First pass 02: 015:111–260
- 111–122 reads Qurʾanic accounts of particular divine guidance against an account granting believer and unbeliever identical total aids. Necessary cognition and reward are not simply incompatible: necessary and discursive knowledge may exchange modes, and divinely given love/knowledge can receive reward. This is a theological disagreement with reported positions, not an independently established empirical psychology.
- 123–150 is a major causal-epistemic clarification. Divine gift does not exclude discursive reason, teachers or prophets as genuine routes. At 126 God gives both causes and outcomes; at 127 a condition has a real role in its conditioned result. At 129 every created cause depends on other causes and can meet impediments, illustrated by sunlight, receptive body, clouds and roof. 133 states the Qadarī exclusive-human view; 134 states its opposite denying the servant causal contribution to generated effects; 135–136 explicitly endorses a middle view: eating plus creaturely powers produces satiety; injury plus susceptibility of the affected body produces death. God creates both and the resulting effect. Human action remains morally attributable (137–147), with battlefield verses and continuing charitable/educational effects cited. The passage is explanatory historical theology, not an instruction for action.
- 150 leaves a reported dispute over whether correct reasoning entails knowledge by inseparable inclusion or by a divinely maintained habit; both agree the knower depends on God for forming and joining premises. 151–158 pairs cognition with love/will: knowing truth alone can coexist with refusal. Dependence is no excuse to omit commanded means.
- 159–187 criticizes an exclusive obligatory route and records Abū Yaʿlā's changed/alternative position. His quoted argument says recognized prophethood establishes a sender without a prior separate speculative proof. The reply distinguishes revelation's directing reason (169), supplying detailed names/attributes (170), and the different claim that revealed obligation entails a wholly nonrational means of cognition (174). Scriptural completeness does not entail rational evidence is false or useless (181–182). At 184 the author identifies the potential circularity in a miracle-first proof: if a miracle's authentication already requires knowledge of God and truthfulness, it cannot straightforwardly be the first proof of those conditions. Alternative routes to knowing a prophet matter (186).
- 188–202 offers two possible repairs: innate recognition prior to miracles, or one sign simultaneously proving its divine source and messenger. Moses/Pharaoh and the magicians are analyzed as evidence for the latter. The domestic copyist analogy at 198 distinguishes inferring a hidden producer from merely trusting testimony. These are the author's proposals, not unqualified endorsement of Abū Yaʿlā's initial inference.
- 203–220 extensively quotes Ibn al-Zāghūnī on obligatory reflection and competing accounts of faith by imitation/necessity. The harsh verdict on non-discursive believers at 204 is Ibn al-Zāghūnī's quoted position, not Ibn Taymiyya's. The long taxonomy includes differences over implanted cognition, causes, will, nature and accountability; retain these distinctions rather than labeling all “rationalism.”
- 221–238 shifts to rational moral judgment and the relation of reason to revelation. Abū al-Khaṭṭāb's quotation at 233 gives a veracity route: independent recognition of falsehood's moral badness excludes divine lying and authentication of liars. Ibn Taymiyya calls this a known route (234), then records capacity- and necessity-based alternatives (235–237), without here resolving the contest. At 230 he interprets Aḥmad as denying that human minds comprehend all of revelation, not that they comprehend none of its directives.
- 239–260 continues Abū al-Khaṭṭāb's arguments: gratitude without divine need; prudent avoidance of possible danger; obligation to listen rather than evade a warning; moral consensus; praise/blame; an epistemic priority chain ending at rational discernment; inability to license unbelief; value of bestowed reason. Ibn Taymiyya explicitly notes remaining contested arguments at 251 and postpones adjudication. Scriptural threats dependent on messengers are stated as objections (252–255), then answered within the quoted discussion. Do not promote every quoted proof into Ibn Taymiyya's established premises.

## First pass 03: 015:261–360
- 261–287 preserves the debate over why prophets matter, obligations before puberty, children of unbelievers, physical/legal accountability, property obligations, and rational moral judgment. Ibn Taymiyya says the majority suspends punishable obligation until puberty, rejects a falsely attributed Aḥmad report based on a weak/fabricated Khadīja report (278–279), and distinguishes educative discipline from destructive penalties (281–284). He does not erase the disagreement over children's afterlife or pretend every rational-moral theorist demands prepubertal obligation.
- 288–297 frames wider epistemic access: vital religious knowledge has many accessible routes; restrictive scholastic catalogues may omit the actually correct position. The transcription at 289 has an apparent syntactic disruption (“... وقد يكون الخطأ الحاصل الرسل ...”); no missing content reconstructed or silently credited. Ibn Rushd's fourfold classification is faulted for omission, while his recognition of rational Qurʾanic proof is credited (294–295).
- 298–317 quotes Ibn Rushd's criticism of the atomist temporal-origin proof, then intersperses authorial replies. Ibn Rushd distinguishes will, act, agent and product at 304; the author at 317 rejects identifying creation-act and created thing, locating effectuation in the effecting agent. At 301 the author carefully distinguishes not preceding one particular event from not preceding an everlasting kind whose members occur. Neither entails the same conclusion. Atomism/divisibility discussion includes mathematical versus real division, vanishing/transformed tiny particles, and disagreement over an actuality of infinite parts (310–315).
- 316–321 directly concerns receptivity and nonduplication. Ibn Rushd asks for the subject receiving occurrence and for what an act relates to between nonbeing and completed being. Ibn Taymiyya denies that occurrence is a further external accident needing a separate bearer (317), rejects external nonexistent quiddities as the default answer (318), and answers the duplication dilemma at 321: action relates to the object at its existence by making it exist, not by adding existence to something that already existed independently. This rebuts a “production must duplicate a ready-made effect” premise. It does not yet establish an exhaustive no-duplicate-causes axiom.
- 323–328 denies both rival appropriations of Abraham's astronomy: his rejection concerns setting/disappearance and worship, not a motion proof of a creator. His people already acknowledged the lord but associated other objects of worship. This is a significant exegetical distinction, not a mere rhetorical aside.
- 329–360 considers time, place and infinite succession. Ibn Rushd's critiques of transferring observed accident-origination to unobserved celestial accidents are reported before replies. 331–338 distinguishes a specific accident, a kind of accidents, simultaneous cause-series, and successive conditions/effects. Ibn Taymiyya at 338 summarizes the causal/conditional distinction while noting dispute elsewhere. At 339 he argues permission of beginningless events does not establish eternal heavens; under either finite or infinite alternatives the philosophers' proof fails. 340–360 examines equivocations in “completed,” “entered existence,” “unending” and one-sided versus two-sided infinity, and distinguishes successive motions from concurrently existing souls (344–345).

## Reflection 02–03
- 015:129–136 supplies source text for intrinsic creaturely powers, receptive conditions and mediated causal efficacy, with God's creation embracing the whole. It would be inaccurate to cite the passage as denying secondary causes. It is also inaccurate to infer from created agents' need of receptivity that the divine source requires an uncreated independent material.
- 015:321 is an explicit anti-duplication answer to a production objection: “at existence” does not mean already-existent-apart-from-the-cause. It supports careful temporal/constitutive indexing. The stronger modern CND rule would need its own bridge.
- Veracity is not treated as an isolated slogan: 015:184 and 233–237 expose dependency-order and alternative warrant routes. The moral-veracity argument at 233 belongs to Abū al-Khaṭṭāb's quotation. The author deliberately postpones a final adjudication.
- Necessary divine act-kind versus successive individual acts (301–304) prevents an inference from eternal agent to eternally completed identical effect. It does not itself prove every required intermediary role or its unique realization.

## First pass 04: 015:361–490
- 361–374 turns Ibn Rushd's infinity comparison against the philosophers' several everlasting celestial cycles, then returns to divine knowledge of particulars. 370 distinguishes al-Juwaynī's “unfolding/extension” of knowledge from the thesis of merely universal cognition; 371 says knowledge of a cause should, on their own principle, include its effects. Critique of opponents' objections is not proof of every positive theology claim.
- 375–389 quotes Ibn Rushd's public teaching proposal and critique of al-Juwaynī's contingency argument. The craft analogy argues that actual structure can be needed for existence or perfection. Ibn Taymiyya accepts purposes and the distinction between existence-conditions and perfection-conditions (384–385), rejects an opposition between wisdom and divine choice (386–387), and says alternative intrinsic possibilities remain compatible with wisdom selecting this arrangement (388–389). This is important: a possible alternative is not thereby arbitrary or purposeless.
- 390–424 critically separates true derived necessity from an alleged eternally coeval produced necessary entity. At 392 he expressly says not everything Ibn Sīnā says is false. At 393 a missing cause entails absence of its effect but does not efficiently make nonexistence; intrinsic possibility, necessary-through-another when actual, and impossible-through-another when requisite grounds are absent are distinguished (393–398). 405–424 challenges the mere division into necessary/possible as insufficient to prove both external classes or a differentiated world-source relation; observed origination supplies content that a bare partition does not. This blocks treating a logical dichotomy as existential coverage.
- 425–434 responds to Ibn Rushd on philosophical expertise, possibility, spatial alternatives, and willing. Historical attributions to Plato are queried rather than confidently adopted (426–427). The author's central distinction is enduring willing-kind and newly instantiated wills/acts; eternal identical individual product does not follow (433–434). Apparent transcription defect at the end of 433 leaves the last clause grammatically incomplete; no supplied negation silently inserted.
- 435–440 gives Ibn Rushd's critique of Sufi purification as sufficient cognition; Ibn Taymiyya answers that love-directed action is an end in itself as well as aid to knowledge, and knowledge is likewise both end and aid (438). This is not a simple reduction of all religious practice to epistemic instrumentality.
- 443–448 quotes Abū al-Ḥusayn al-Baṣrī's long foundational chain: prophetic trust → truthfulness → miracles → exclusion of evil → knowledge of its badness and freedom of need → incorporeality/eternity, with knowledge/power/life and action-based discovery. Ibn Taymiyya ranks alternative origination strategies at 449 and exposes ambiguity of “other” for attributes at 453. This quoted chain matters for source reconstruction but is not the author's accepted dependency graph.
- 457–486 is an extended treatment of distributive versus collective inference. Having a first member individually need not give the whole kind a first boundary. Mathematical, bodily, legal-marriage, oath, dietary and permission analogies supply counterexamples; these are integral argument rather than digressions to omit. A predicate transfers when its grounding nature obtains in the whole (465–467, 481); the author uses dependence of possibles on another as a positive example, but rejects treating an unbounded temporal succession as a simultaneously existing aggregate (481–486). The argument is not “no collective inference ever.”
- 487–490 returns to each member's antecedent nonexistence versus absence of the entire kind. The source has evident local garbling at 473; its broader surrounding contrast is clear but individual wording there must be collated before exact quotation.

## First pass 05: 015:491–665
- 491–509 identifies and rejects a shared premise: endless successive events would establish eternality of these heavens/world-individuals. 494–499 allows created successive antecedents without an eternal created individual. Creation in six days and prior acts do not contradict that distinction. The critique targets theories forcing eternal inactivity and then an ungrounded transition (501–504); examples of sustained heavenly provision illustrate an enduring kind with perishing tokens (507–509).
- 510–544 distinguishes precedence by another event, by one's own nonexistence, and by an agent. A kind may be dependent on an agent without a temporal interval in which the agent produced nothing (516, 521, 543–544). Demanding no earlier act as a condition of this act's origination adds an unproved premise (535). The rhetoric about censures/curses is historical polemic, not a rule for present engagement.
- 545–566 quotes Abū al-Ḥusayn's modal-specialization proof and predecessors' analogy from voluntary actions/building. Ibn Taymiyya maintains that an originated thing needing an originator is clearer than the reconstructed specializer proof (556–557). It is possible for premises to be true yet epistemically less useful than the conclusion; at 565 he grants a distinct inferential aspect while criticizing clarity and order. This is not a blanket rejection of reasoning.
- 567–605 applies the need for a determinant consistently: denying a fresh difference when a new effect occurs seems inconsistent with the proof's own differentiating principle. Human power plus complete motive, divine guidance, and competing theories of agency are distinguished (569–580). Philosophical fixed-complete-cause theories are then criticized more strongly (581–583). At 605 he distinguishes false premises from merely opaque/prolix proofs: no blanket allegation of deliberate lying.
- 606–623 rehearses occurrence/accident arguments and their presupposed finite past. 608 flags the tension in identifying occurrence with the occurred object while using a distinct act to explain it. Series individuation is repeatedly the crux (612–618). The matching/addition infinity argument is recorded as a contested proof, with objections deferred at 623; it must not be presented as a verified modern theorem.
- 624–665 quotes al-Juwaynī and tests “completion,” “past,” and temporal boundary. A latest boundary does not establish an earliest one (638–647). Past/future are relational descriptions; which differences actually affect the proof must be demonstrated (652–653). The giving-coin analogy is diagnosed as switching from beginning an infinite prerequisite task now to a past sequence where each item already has a predecessor (662–665).

## First pass 06: 015:666–802
- 666–676 sharpens the distinction between mutually preventing conditions and predecessor/successor recurrence. It diagnoses why denying all intrinsic voluntary divine acts drives later thinkers between temporal-inactivity theories and eternal-world theories. Al-Rāzī's different books are not treated as a uniform position.
- 677–704 quotes/explains al-Rāzī's argument for enduring divine activity and surveys views of sufficient determination. The author favors complete power plus complete motive necessitating the effect (686; 688–695) over an ungrounded residual chance of either outcome. At 698 absence of one necessary condition is enough to remove total sufficiency; the absence of every ingredient is not required. This is a precise total-cause/partial-condition distinction.
- 705–733 gives seven anti-infinite-past arguments and replies, including numerical matching, arriving at today, distributive transfer, boundedness, treating eternity as a time, enclosure by existence, and simultaneous absence/presence. 719 warns that “nonexistent” must not mean merely “not all concurrently present”; successive motion, speech and sound really occur. 726 distinguishes an infinite future prerequisite task from a beginningless past. 729 explicitly denies eternity is a particular initial time. The source repeats material around 706, 712–713 and 732; counted as transcription repetition, not independent proofs.
- 734–750 returns to methodological fairness, the shifting polemic in al-Rāzī, relative merits of schools, and legal classifications of religious communities. It includes jizya, diet, marriage, ancestry versus present affiliation, and distinction of manifest Islam from inward belief. These are preserved as historically situated authorial claims, not current legal guidance. At 748 the author concedes philosophers sometimes get particular questions right and some non-Muslim individuals exhibit traits lacking in some nominal Muslims.
- 751–777 actively helps the kalām side against philosophical eternal-heavens arguments. Disjunctions show neither finite nor infinite event succession establishes the eternal identity of this world. 757 mentions creation through water, vapor/smoke and prior events. 759–775 centers voluntary acts intrinsic to the necessary existent: a completely unchanged timeless sufficient cause cannot explain successive varied outcomes under its own sufficiency principle; changing celestial intermediaries do not automatically repair that deficit. 773 contrasts prohibited simultaneously ordered complete efficient causes with temporal succession. The author is not endorsing the kalām proof previously criticized.
- 778–802 is a major nonextrinsic-dependence argument. “Essence sufficient” is ambiguous: sufficient for coeternal actuality or sufficient though a product occurs later (792–793). A distinct cause produced by God is not an independent source (794–797); one divine act may condition another without dependence on an independently acting other. 798–801 distinguishes an essence abstracted from intrinsic act from an essence qualified by act. The latter may suffice without coeternal particular products; enduring act-kind and enduring individual life are different sorts of concomitants (801).

## Reflection 04–06
1. Direct source-mode evidence now exceeds the opening epistemology: 015:794–801 makes intrinsic act and self-originated conditions compatible with self-sufficiency. Dependence language must distinguish one's own act/attribute from an independent giver. It does not abolish external created effects or their roles.
2. 015:393 explicitly separates entailment from efficient causation in explanations of nonexistence. A formal graph needs typed relations; “depends on” alone obscures this distinction.
3. 015:465–486 permits some collective dependence inferences, but gives a transfer criterion. It is not enough to know that each represented item has a relevant property: the proposed whole must have the same ground and must be the right sort of existent. Exact coverage remains an additional matter.
4. 015:698 supports failure of a complete condition-set when one required condition fails. It does not by itself specify that the conditions named in a reconstruction exhaust the actual full set.
5. Arguments about universal causality, divine intrinsic activity, and world eternity remain distinguishable. A source reading may support one while leaving another bridge underived. No final source-mode verdict from these passages alone.

## First pass 07: 016:20–180
- 20–69 quotes and answers al-Suhrawardī's Talwīḥāt. Continuous selection is not continuous existence of every selected individual (28–31). “Change” is ambiguous between transformation and acting differently (58–59); relabeling the disputed proposition does not prove it. 60–69 distinguishes circular origination, an infinite simultaneous completion-chain for one effect, and a temporal succession of distinct effects. The first two are rejected; the third is the disputed and, for these interlocutors, permitted case.
- 70–97 turns to Ḥikmat al-Ishrāq's Light of Lights argument. The crucial reply at 81–88: no external independent prerequisite does not entail no intrinsic voluntary act as prerequisite, nor does it exclude one divinely caused condition preceding another. 93–97 tests the claim that everything from an eternal source endures with it against the opponent's own successive motion.
- 98–115 quotes Ibn Sīnā's list of completing conditions: tools, matter, coworker, time, motive and absence of obstacle. At 102–110 Ibn Taymiyya distinguishes the hypothetical fixed-complete-cause proposition from showing that the actual source is such a bare cause. Al-Rāzī's reconstruction at 112–114 explicitly quantifies over all factors completing efficacy; he then proposes an eternal-will reply (115).
- 116–164 criticizes that reply and elaborates effect-indexed completion. At 120–122 the author asks whether the completion-conditions concern each particular or “the whole world”: different parts may depend on different conditions, and the whole is not simply a single fixed condition-set already granted. At 130–141 the distinction grounds the argument that creative “Be” cannot itself be a created prerequisite requiring another simultaneously efficacious “Be.” Ability must be present during action but can also precede it; the moral importance is that disobedient persons still have actionable capacity (133–137). This rejects conflation of enabling capacity with a full sufficient state. 161–164 distinguishes all acts, some particular act, and act-kind.
- 165–180 records al-Āmidī's versions and “daily events” objection. At 177–178 the author refuses to infer one theory's truth solely from defeating another; an omitted third option matters. That is a methodological safeguard for the modern appraisal as well.

## First pass 08: 016:181–390
- 181–208 continues objections and diagnoses the common exclusion of intrinsic attributes/acts. Celestial mediation and the active intellect do not repair an explanatory deficit merely by being interposed (202–203). Earlier philosophers may have meant enduring material-kind rather than an eternal particular; competing historical reports are distinguished (205). Abū al-Barakāt's demonstrations are said to establish enduring activity, not an eternal particular world (206–207).
- 209–240 reviews Ibn Sīnā's and al-Rāzī's divisions of positions, material eternalism, dualism, five eternals, and unity-of-being theories. Ibn Taymiyya says the classifications overlap and omit live options; he rejects the universal attribution of eternal inactivity to all religious communities. He distinguishes an eternally posited material's being caused from its being self-necessary (223,233). Transcription caution: 213 contains an internally suspect comparison; 318 below plainly inverts the labels after describing self-existence versus existence-through-another. Do not silently normalize these as exact source quotations.
- 241–269 quotes Ibn Sīnā's choice between two camps and al-Rāzī's denial that unity is relevant. The author answers that the particular simplicity thesis removing attributes/acts is decisive (254–257). Aristotle's beloved unmoved goal is not yet an efficient producer (258–263). A contingent subject's dependence extends to its acts and attributes, but that is an argued move, not license to infer any arbitrary dependency type.
- 270–306 introduces Thābit ibn Qurra's summary of Aristotle's Metaphysics. The transmission makes nature/form/motion/appetite a path to the first beloved. Ibn Taymiyya distinguishes receptivity from appetitive voluntary motion (283–284), and a necessary existence-condition from a complete productive cause (285,300,306). At 286 he openly says he does not know Greek and lacks an isnād of known transmitters: his critique uses Arabic reports by the philosophers' own advocates, not direct independent Greek collation. 301–302 affirms that the same God can be efficient source as lord and beloved end as deity, with the two roles distinct. The polemical analogies at 309–312 illustrate inconsistent standards, not additional proofs of source roles.
- 314–340 continues conditional arguments, explicitly granting the opponent's simultaneous-caused-possible for argument's sake (320), not retracting earlier objections. A beloved alone does not create a lover or the lover's love (321–326). 340 quotes the potentiality/outside-actualizer argument and the analogy of possibility to receptive matter.
- 341–390 systematically replies. An external goal alone is not a complete determinant of an agent's effective act (343–344). “Cause other than the essence” must distinguish essence stripped of action from intrinsically acting essence (354–356). No claim to ancient agreement replaces a reason, especially where the historical report is disputed (370–371). “Change” cannot carry the whole argument through an equivocal label (373–377). The author credits perfection to genuine knowledge/power/activity rather than bare inertness (387–388).

## First pass 09: 016:391–557
- 391–398 treats delayed effects in terms of complete conditions, power, wisdom and possibility. Bare possibility does not entail actual coeternality (394). This directly qualifies the matter analogy in the preceding quotation.
- 399–454 quotes and attacks a first object of love that neither acts voluntarily nor naturally. A beloved can be asleep or unaware without ceasing to be beloved; that shows love-directed motion is not automatically efficient production (407–412). A loved object can pre-exist its lover (415–418), so final causation alone does not establish eternal co-presence. The distinction is repeated through conditional world-necessity/world-contingency branches. At 431 existence of a contingent must be established before its love; age/eternity is a second question. At 452 inability to create oneself is no analogy for inability to create one's effect.
- 455–483 develops a perfection comparison: agent unaffected by another; agent also affected; passive object; inert neither-acting-nor-affected. The author places the last near nonexistence. 466–479 argues that capacity for perfections is better than incapacity, and possessing them better than lacking them. This is substantive axiological reasoning, not an empirically verified theorem. Preserve its premises explicitly in later use.
- 484–500 resists treating absence of an intrinsically impossible or inapt eternal particular as a defect (484). Action proceeding from oneself need not gain completion from an outside source (485–487). Love can be self-love, or love of one's own produced effects (491–496), without dependence on something independently self-sufficient. These paragraphs distinguish prospective realization of acts from acquired essence-defects.
- 501–518 distinguishes efficient origination from merely occasioning an emotion: gold “kills,” the world “deceives,” or bread draws a hungry person, without those objects themselves producing the agent's act (509–511). Intrinsic activity can produce successively states not compossible all at once (518), an explicitly proposed reply within the dialectic.
- 519–557 responds to treating every love as need of an independent beloved. Counterfactual reciprocal-dependence comparisons at 528 are explicitly said to be rejected by Muslims; they are dialectical, not authorial endorsement. At 527 and547 an independently originating agent's own effect is dependent throughout, so love/will directed toward it does not make it an outside source of the agent. 551 rejects induction from ordinary animals to an unrestricted universal claim about all voluntary agents. Terminology such as “natural” must be unpacked rather than mistaken for a fixed contrary of choice (555–557).

## First pass 10: 016:558–665
- 558–608 returns to Ibn Rushd's Qurʾanic providence and invention arguments. He lists environmental and bodily suitability, generation of living/perceiving/rational beings, and relevant verses; an expert knows the maker more deeply through richer knowledge of the work. Ibn Taymiyya adds accounts of celestial dependence (569–578), but at 602–603 and610 expressly rejects confining all Qurʾanic ways to the two genres and rejects making providence wholly separate from production. These are selective agreements, not an endorsement of the quoted taxonomy in full.
- 571–578 provides specific extrinsic-dependence reasoning: a needy seeker of an independently existing end is not self-necessary; none of the celestial contributors independently supplies all lower effects; partners, receptive conditions and barriers exceed each one's individual power. “Needing another” is specified as another independently beyond one's originating control, not one's own act or effect.
- 609–620 states two purposes: proposed anti-revelation proofs need not be the foundation of knowing revelation, and the purported counterproofs fail on their own terms. Mutual critiques are compared with witnesses discrediting each other (616). The author refuses unqualified invented labels such as body/composition/indwelling events and demands differentiated meanings (618).
- 621–626 quotes Ibn Rushd on unity and three Qurʾanic verses. Ibn Rushd's two-kings account suggests duplicate actions ruin a shared subject. Ibn Taymiyya's own response begins 627.
- 627–650 is the central causal-nonduplication passage. One numerically identical complete product cannot be the independent whole act of two independent agents: independence excludes shared production (627–628). Cooperation is distinct contributions, e.g. transporting versus placing bricks, or carrying opposite ends (629). All created agency requires other contributors and absent obstacles (630–643). Sunlight needs a receptive body and no screen; heat/nutrition need recipient powers; voluntary human motion uses organs not self-created; stone flight uses stone and air; satiety uses food and digestion. Medieval physical examples (including claims about substances immune to fire at637) are the author's illustrations, not adopted modern science.
- 644 states absolute lordship as acting for/nurturing a thing in all its aspects. 645–647 applies that to restrictions on calling human masters “lord,” distinguishing animals/property and accountable persons, and says divine willing alone is unconditionally effectual. 650 carefully preserves cooperative causation while denying numerical identity of each contributor's intrinsic act: eye, hand, brain, heart do different things despite interdependence.
- 651–665 clarifies that impossibility of duplicate entire acts precedes the hypothetical destruction; Ibn Rushd's “same type” addition does not carry the proof. The author distinguishes worship/deity from a merely efficient lord; he claims historically actual polytheism was not belief in two perfectly equal world-creators. That broad historical claim is recorded as authorial claim, not independently certified.

## Reflection 07–10
1. The strongest positive support for CND is 016:627–650, but its object is the numerically same whole act/product under independence. It does not deny many contributors to an ordinary composite event. Any formalization must state identity and granularity rather than hide them.
2. The strongest full-role statement is 016:644, alongside 630–643: absolute lordship covers all aspects, creaturely action uses parts/partners/materials it did not originate. This gives an explicit source ideal and contrast. An additional formal theorem is still needed if one infers that a specified finite role-list is extensionally all relevant aspects.
3. 016:300,306 and431 sharply block promoting a goal/condition to efficient cause or complete source. The source itself demands typed dependence and separate establishment of productive agency.
4. 016:120–122 warns that a whole world's causation need not be represented by one pre-granted set of fixed simultaneous conditions. 133–137 likewise separates actionable capacity from sufficient actual efficacy.
5. Intrinsic acts and one's own dependent effects differ from an independent external actualizer (016:354–360,485–495,527,547). This supports a positive intrinsic source-mode reading and leaves a bounded burden: demonstrate the specific underlying productive role and all relevant dependence types, rather than infer them solely from a title such as necessary existent.

## First pass 11: 016:666–800
- 666–676 surveys dualism, five-eternals cosmology and limited source attribution. 676 explicitly affirms created secondary causes with collaborators and absent obstacles as consistent with divine unity. It is not occasionalist denial of ordinary causes. Polemical comparative rankings and historical attributions are recorded as the author's, not independently checked.
- 679–699 quotes Ibn Rushd's unity argument and criticism of mutual-prevention proofs. 683–687 rejects interpreting Qurʾan17:42 as a rival assault upon God, preferring intermediaries' own quest for worshipful access. 695–698 states agreement, divided work and alternating work objections. Ibn Rushd replies that power to invent a part extends to the whole, but that claim remains inside his quotation.
- 700–725 is Ibn Taymiyya's defense of mutual prevention, including agreement. 710 invokes distinct products under independent agency; 711 uses actual world-interconnection to challenge isolated creators. At 712–716 the argument treats possible conflicting wills and causal circularity. At 717–718 it explicitly permits mutual concomitance, using divine essence/attributes, life, knowledge and power as examples. At 720–723 it considers powers intrinsic to each source rather than caused by the other, then infers each could act independently; two sources cannot independently perform the numerically same whole act. Do not misreport the source as ignoring noncausal concomitance.
- 726–746 elaborates independent power, distinct creations, mutual product-dependence and dominance. At 739 dependence of one product on another is taken to entail its producer's inability to act alone; this is an important inference to audit rather than a trivial identity. 742–745 compares powers and infers dominance, with substitutability in role used for equality (744). The source does not supply a modern partial-order completeness theorem here.
- 747–756 expressly states al-Āmidī's objection: each could produce a token if the other refrains, as a single agent can do alternatives but not contradictory conjunctions. The response distinguishes self-chosen nonperformance from an external agent preventing a wanted act (749–750). 751–754 infers a genuinely independent capable source from actual events, then denies two independent complete producers. 755 concedes human kings can have separate domains, but says shared command violates unrestricted independence.
- 757–780 continues this response in terms of inability conditional on another's action. Intrinsic impossibilities are not objects falling under “all things” (761–762). At 776 the author identifies the remaining issue as cooperation/partition, not simply same-token duplication; 778–779 argues that partial/alternating powers are deficient for deity, and that harmony either allows a genuinely contrary option or reflects disabling dependence. These are the exact source argument and its modal/role premises; no assertion that all modern objections are thereby settled.
- 781–800 distinguishes worshipful unity from mere unique creator belief. 787–788 quotes Ibn Rushd's logical-form criticism; 789–798 rejects limiting cosmic corruption to one instant and distinguishes corruption from simple nonexistence. Human flourishing is construed as completion in worshipful orientation. This is a teleological/theological argument in addition to, rather than equivalent to, efficient-source uniqueness.

## First pass 12: 016:801–930
- 801–824 continues the ethical/teleological account of motion, love, intention, innate orientation and worship. 805 distinguishes unique creator from unique final object; subordinate loves are for God rather than independent rivals (806–815). 818 analogizes independent complete final orientation to independent entire production; it does not identify love and efficient causation. 820–824 says divine creative power alone does not exhaust deity. The natural-motion account and claims about universal religious history are preserved without modern scientific/historical certification.
- 826–840 resumes Ibn Rushd, first with his restrictive “true Muslim” epistemic classification, then seven perfections and divine knowledge. His purposive-house analogy establishes knowing design (828–830). His public teaching distinguishes past/present/future cognition but his account for experts unifies it; Ibn Taymiyya charges tension (837–840). The normative classification at826 is quoted Ibn Rushd, not the author's own new criterion.
- 841–868 quotes Ibn Rushd's appendix on old knowledge: changing known objects seem to change truth-tracking cognition, al-Ghazālī's relational analogy is criticized, and a producing divine knowledge is contrasted with caused human knowledge (859–864). Dreams/prophetic intimations are invoked for particular cognition (867). The quotation's confident conclusion is not the author's verdict.
- 869–896 gives a major response. Both divine and human knowing divide into causally contributory and nonproductive cognition (871–875). God's self-knowledge does not produce himself; practical human knowledge contributes to actions. Knowledge is a condition of creation, not its entire sufficient cause, because will/power also matter (873–874). Conceiving a desired act, knowing it will occur, and knowing it has occurred are distinct (876–878). The author's answer allows intrinsic knowledge whose actual relation is conditioned by one's own action without dependence on an independent giver (881–884). The allegedly exhaustive ancient/created distinction therefore does not solve the question.
- 897–918 distinguishes agreed new relations from disputed intrinsic new states, describes several schools, and cites scriptural passages of knowledge at occurring events while affirming foreknowledge (902–910). Ibn Rushd's historical defense of all Peripatetics is challenged; if a school attributes dreams to celestial intermediaries, those dreams alone do not prove its God knows each dream-object (916).
- 919–936 begins a careful plurality of philosophical positions. Knowing only oneself, knowing universals, and knowing each temporal particular cannot be silently collapsed. There are further distinctions within the latter view (935–936). The discussion is source-specific despite broad polemic elsewhere.

## First pass 13: 016:931–1092 (terminal electronic-body span)
- 931–943 concludes the taxonomy and introduces Abū al-Barakāt's quotation/review. Communicating information presupposes knowledge (932), conditional on its actually being God's communication rather than an alleged intermediate. Heading939 has “عدم الله,” apparently a corrupted “علم الله”; retained in the electronic source, not silently corrected as verified text. Abū al-Barakāt sets out an unusually charitable method of giving rival positions their strongest stated and unstated arguments (943).
- 944–970 quotes Aristotle through Abū al-Barakāt on self-intellection, alleged dependence on known others, exhaustion, movement, multiplicity and matter-form comparison. 959–964 summarizes four concerns. Abū al-Barakāt's initial reply is explicitly dialectical: whatever difficulty knowing all allegedly creates must be compared with creating all. At970–971 Ibn Taymiyya identifies this as refutation by counter-instance/opponent commitment, not yet an independent positive proof.
- 971–1009 elaborates the comparison: a maker's product depends on it more directly than an arbitrary known object does on a knower. Knowing may concern nonexistents or impossibles whereas making concerns actual products (976). Knowledge itself is not a defect merely because bad agents misuse it (1007). At1009 the author expressly marks his current aim as defeating deniers' doubts, not supplying the full proofs for all positive claims. That sentence limits how this whole section can be used in reconstruction.
- 1010–1016 notes an attribution caveat crucial to the argument: comparing knowing with creating presupposes an efficient source, which Ibn Taymiyya says Aristotle's merely final-cause account has not established. Its force against later efficient-cause philosophers does not automatically bind every Aristotelian formulation.
- 1017–1031 quotes Abū al-Barakāt: activity expresses prior perfection, with intellectual capacity versus achieved individual cognition distinguished. The author queries the premise that unity makes every lack inconceivable when attributes are distinct (1025), and does not simply adopt the entire quoted solution.
- 1032–1058 supplies alternative responses and a substantive correction. Cognition, not merely ability to cognize, can be intrinsically necessary (1034–1037); known things may be one's own created dependents (1039–1041). He gives both a timeless-knowledge/new-relations answer (1043) and a temporally articulated cognition answer (1045), and rejects the idea that realization of a perfect power adds no perfection (1046–1048). A token impossible eternally is perfected by occurring in its appropriate time, not by an impossible eternal token (1048,1052). At1054–1058 he distinguishes “another gives the knower perfection” from “without this object there would not be knowledge-of-it”; the latter need not be an objection, especially where the knower created that object.
- 1059–1091 returns to Abū al-Barakāt's reply about changed relations, physical movement, fatigue and lowly objects. The detailed heating/color/motion examples (1063–1071) rebut illicit transfer from some physical cases to all knowing; these are historical natural philosophy, not certified modern experiments. 1073 distinguishes receptive readiness awaiting completion from active ability expressing completion. 1079–1083 distinguishes human limited attention or disgust from unrestricted cognition. 1088–1091 argues knowing creation cannot make the creator dependent on an independent other. The final authorial sentence1092 rates this discussion more precise than Ibn Rushd's and Ibn Sīnā's; it does not erase the earlier corrections of Abū al-Barakāt.

## Reflection 11–13: independent appraisal after reaching the end
1. There is explicit original support, not merely a modern gloss, for intrinsic activity, action-specific completion, nonduplicable entire productive roles, the all-aspects sense of lordship, and distinction of an independent external giver from one's own act/effect. The evidence is stronger than a bare necessary-being partition.
2. The source also supplies serious internal guardrails: condition/goal does not imply productive source (016:300,306); description or existence-division does not identify every role (015:25,421–424); individual predicates do not automatically transfer collectively (015:459–486); two defeated rivals need not exhaust possibilities (016:178,208); a refutation of a knowledge-denial argument is not the entire positive omniscience proof (016:1009).
3. The necessary-harmony challenge is actually confronted. 016:717–718 permits noncausal mutual concomitance; 720–725 then tries to exclude two independently powerful agents by deriving unilateral efficacy from intrinsic power. The evaluative burden is to validate that modal transition without covertly defining every self-sufficient bearer as an unrestricted world-producer. “They never consider concomitance” would be a bad objection; “their transition needs an explicit role and modality premise” is a better one.
4. The differentiated-role challenge is actually confronted too. 016:739 transfers interdependence of products to dependence of producers, while 776–779 rejects partition and alternation as deficient deity. Reconstructing this needs exact definitions of completed effect, production, recipient condition, source-controlled enabling roles, and unrestricted divine capability. Textual definition of full lordship is evidence for the intended target, not proof that every necessary existent already meets the full target.
5. CND has a narrow secure textual core: an independently whole numerically identical act/product cannot at the same time be another's independent whole act/product (016:627–650). A formal treatment must distinguish same event description from same intrinsic action, cooperative contributions from duplicate whole sufficiency, and exclusion by conceptual contradiction from inability due to a separate agent. The source's objection-and-reply at748–753 is the test case.
6. On attributes, 016:873–884 and1034–1058 make knowledge more than productive intention. Intended conception, foreknowledge, and knowledge of actuality cannot be collapsed. Relation to a produced object is not automatically receiving perfection from an independent cause, but neither does this alone derive knowing every proposition or divine veracity.
7. Present verdict: the volume strongly warrants using an intrinsic, fully productive source conception in the reconstruction; it does not yet certify an unqualified deduction of exact complete coverage from a minimally described necessary or nonderivative bearer. That is a bounded formal burden, not a finding that the source lacks an argument. Global verdict remains for the combined audit and other volume evidence.
