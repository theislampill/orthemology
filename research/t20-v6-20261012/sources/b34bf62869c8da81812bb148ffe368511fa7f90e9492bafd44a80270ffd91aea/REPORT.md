## 3. Argument, voices, and conclusion

At 8:16–17 the author separates variation in what a person must next do from variation in the acquisition of knowledge and action. Someone already competent in a required practice differs from someone who first needs instruction. These are the source's historical legal illustrations; no current legal ruling follows.

At 8:17–18 he reports Abu al-Husayn al-Basri's proposed chain: accepting the messengers' teaching requires their veracity; this requires miracles; the miracles' force requires a source who does not do wrong; further attributes must then be established in a specific sequence, eventually through originated effects. **That chain is a reported position, not the author's own endorsed universal dependence structure.** His criticism begins on p. 18 with the observation that the whole religion has been made to rest on that arrangement. On p. 19 he disputes the asserted necessity of several particular links and notes alternative views.

Pages 20–21 compare this with stipulated Sufi stages and logicians' arrangements of definitions, syllogisms, and mental transitions. The first six printed lines of p. 21 complete the same paragraph: restricting general knowledge, knowledge of God and prophetic truth, or spiritual progress to one arrangement is unwarranted. These arrangements may contain truth and falsehood; their true content does not establish their exclusivity. The conditions, causes, and ordering of knowledge are wider than the selected pathways.

The next paragraph positively endorses the prophets' direction toward the intended ends and stronger, more useful means. This is the author's theological appraisal. It is not a supplied empirical ranking or an aggregation algorithm. The final paragraph, continuing into 8:22, identifies two opposite mistakes: projecting a particular group's route onto everybody, then rejecting a route's genuine truth wholesale from the standpoint of a different route. The passage therefore protects valid content in different actual paths while rejecting compulsory uniformity.

The intervening 8:22–23 discusses rational and revealed valuation, and will versus love and approval. At 8:24–25 the author resumes acquisition. He rejects a simple opposition between rational and transmitted evidence: rational evidence supports prophetic truth, and transmitted teaching can explain the rational evidence for God, unity, attributes, and prophetic truth. This is an explicit compatibility and inferential-link claim. It is not an assertion that the corresponding evidential grounds are probabilistically independent, premise-disjoint, or independently acquired by every subject.
