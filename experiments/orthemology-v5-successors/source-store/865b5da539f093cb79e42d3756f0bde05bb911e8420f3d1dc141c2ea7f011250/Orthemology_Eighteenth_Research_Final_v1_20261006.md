# The present judgement

Eighteenth establishes a complete ordinary mathematical answer for a new, precisely bounded restoration problem. A finite system may change once, without announcing the change, from one known transition model to another. The question is whether one lawful controller can succeed with probability one against every permitted choice of change time. For this class, a finite certificate characterises success, a concrete history-based controller can be extracted, and exact rational inputs admit a polynomial-time decision procedure. The decision problem is P-complete under logarithmic-space reductions.

The practical distinction is easy to miss. A controller that works when either model is fixed from the beginning can fail if the system changes after an earlier action has moved it somewhere unsafe. The new result checks that future-change risk throughout the uncertain stage. It permits learning from observations without requiring the change ever to become conclusively visible. It supplies no deadline for learning or recovery: the success condition tolerates any finite unsuccessful prefix.

The result also separates three questions about information. Exact rational probabilities support effective synthesis. Arbitrary real probabilities still permit an effective winner for each positive instance, but that existence claim is nonuniform. When both modes share one priority map, trusted support information alone suffices, on positive instances, for a single learning policy across all compatible probabilities. With different hidden priority maps, even the same support and row-equality information can require different statistical calibration.

# The exact hidden change problem

The new theorem concerns finitely many observable physical states and actions. Two transition models specify the probability of each next state for every state-action pair. Each state has a nonempty menu of actions already declared lawful under both modes. The initial hidden mode is 0. It may change irreversibly to mode 1, or remain 0 forever. These permissions and probabilities are mathematical inputs; the theorem does not establish their adequacy for a physical deployment.

The timing is part of the result. At each round the controller chooses an action from the observed history. The change adversary sees that action and decides whether to change. The governing mode then determines the next-state distribution and the priority attached to the departure state-action pair. The controller receives its own action history and physical observations. It receives neither a change flag nor the priority as an extra observation. Moving the change before the action, adding an observed priority, or changing how priorities are attached would alter the stated contract.

A priority is a nonnegative integer acceptance label. Success uses the minimum-parity convention: the least priority appearing infinitely often must be even. This includes familiar eventual-behaviour requirements, such as making only finitely many wrong-mode choices in the Bernoulli example. A finite run of bad priorities does not defeat parity. Almost-sure success means probability one under each permitted adversary, rather than success on every individually possible infinite path.

The controller may remember arbitrarily much of a finite history and may use an arbitrary measurable private random seed. The adversary may adapt to the public past and current action and may randomise, but cannot inspect the controller's private seed or future transition randomness. The positive construction ultimately needs no controller randomisation. The mathematical necessity argument nevertheless covers the larger private-seed policy class.

## Why fixed change times suffice

For one fixed controller, success against every legal adaptive adversary is equivalent to success under no change and under every deterministic finite change index. The proof uses a common probability space. On runs where the actual change first occurs at index n, the adaptive trajectory is identical to the fixed-index-n trajectory driven by the same controller seed and receipt randomness. The no-change trajectories agree in the same way. Actual failure is contained in a countable union of fixed-law failure events, each of probability zero.

This is an actual trajectory-coupling argument. It does not assume that a tail selected by an adaptive change has become independently stationary. An auxiliary three-status partially observable model can encode a positive mixture of the no-change and all finite-index laws, but its artificial mixture weights are a proof device. They are not a prior belief or a promised hazard distribution for the real change.

The class remains narrow: two modes, one irreversible change, finite explicit structure, common lawful menus and the stated observation and parity conventions. The result supplies no corresponding theorem for unrestricted hidden-state systems, repeated switching, arbitrary permission inference or a bounded recovery requirement.

# A finite certificate for actual success

The certificate divides public information into two stages. While every observed transition has positive probability under mode 0, the controller remains uncertain. An earlier receipt impossible under mode 1 cannot eliminate mode 1 permanently, because the adversary may still change later. A receipt with zero mode-0 probability soundly reveals mode 1. Once such a receipt occurs, the controller can stay in the known-mode-1 stage forever. Some successful runs never receive that revelation.

The certificate supplies a known-mode-1 region K and an uncertain region W containing the initial state. The sets may overlap, and K may be empty. It lists lawful action pairs and finite progress witnesses. Known-stage actions must keep every mode-1-positive successor inside K. Uncertain actions must keep every mode-0-positive successor inside W and send every mode-1-positive, mode-0-impossible successor into K. Safety checks all positive successors, not only the route chosen as a progress witness.

From each state in K, a mode-1-positive path using the known-stage safe pairs must reach a component contained in that same pair set with an even mode-1 priority minimum. From each state in W, and for each candidate, a path using the uncertain safe pairs and receipts positive under both the candidate and mode 0 must reach a qualifying component contained in those safe pairs, or, for candidate 1, a revealing exit into K. Each component is nonempty, closed under all candidate-positive transitions and strongly connected. An uncertain component must also have no candidate-positive successor that is impossible under mode 0. Finite paths witness a chance of progress; they do not command the environment's receipts.

Every uncertain component has an even priority minimum under its candidate. Its further qualification uses full numerical row equality. If the rival mode agrees with the candidate on every complete transition row of a component, the component must also have an even minimum priority under that rival. If their rows differ, eventual statistical discrimination carries an additional obligation. Identical support sets alone do not justify treating the rows as indistinguishable. Both positive and negative controls below show why this detail matters.

## Necessity and sufficiency

The accepted ordinary theorem has four equivalent descriptions for the exact rational class: a lawful measurable private-seed winner exists; a finite positive certificate is accepted; the initial state lies in the computed greatest uncertain region; and an effective deterministic history controller extracted from a positive certificate wins. The certificate contains finite structural data. It does not carry an assumed winning proof, fairness oracle or supplied convergence guarantee.

Necessity obtains safe regions and recurrent components from arbitrary winning policies. Its new cross-mode step transfers a positive-probability tail confined to a component between laws whose rows agree there. A late deterministic switch can retain an earlier mode-0 prefix that would have been impossible under mode 1. The argument uses finite-prefix seed restrictions and whole confined-tail measures; it does not restart at a random last-exit time or condition on no future revelation and declare the result Markov.

Negative evidence consists of complete descending region traces. Both begin with the full state set, recompute every update and end at a fixed region. The known-stage terminal supplies K for the uncertain updates; negative acceptance requires the initial state to be absent from the final uncertain region. Merely submitting some fixed point would be insufficient: a smaller fixed point can omit genuinely winning states. The negative result is complete for the declared class, rather than a report that one proposed positive certificate failed.

# How the extracted controller learns

The extracted controller is a finite program whose memory may grow without bound. It records state departures, uses of each state-action pair and observed successors. In the uncertain stage it alternates candidate modes through numbered phases. It fairly cycles the safe navigation actions until it reaches a submitted candidate component, then fairly cycles that component's actions. Persistent departure counts make the cycling genuinely fair; restarting a local counter at every step could lose that property.

After each receipt, a mode-0-impossible transition takes precedence and enters the known-mode-1 stage. Otherwise the controller compares empirical frequencies with the current candidate's exact rows. A row is tested only when its sample count is strictly greater than the current phase number. A rejected candidate or an exit from the retained component advances the phase once and clears the component. If both happen on one receipt, there is still only one phase increment.

For rational input, the comparison tolerance is half the least positive coordinate difference between the two row families, with a harmless fixed fallback if all rows agree. No hidden mode is queried. Every finite-history computation terminates and a lawful fallback handles histories outside the certified invariant. The counters are unbounded even though the certificate, the state space and the program text are finite.

## Finite contamination and stable phases

After any fixed finite change, pre-change observations contribute only a finite prefix to the data. Empirical frequencies at pairs used infinitely often converge to the final actual row. If phase numbers kept growing, pairs used only finitely often would eventually fail the phase-count gate. Together these facts prevent unbounded phase growth: a sufficiently late true-candidate phase can neither be rejected by stale data nor leave a genuinely closed true component.

A stable phase need not identify the global mode correctly. It first establishes row agreement on pairs that actually recur. Fair cycling and strong connectivity then force recurrence of the whole retained component, allowing the certificate's rival-priority condition to apply. A wrong candidate can therefore remain harmless when all rows that matter on its eventual component agree with reality. Exact finite-time identification is stronger than the theorem needs.

The known stage has a simpler controller: fair navigation reaches a submitted good component with probability one, and fair operation makes every selected component pair recur. The uncertain and known arguments first prove success under every fixed-index law and no change. The actual coupling then transfers that same controller to every legal adaptive randomised change adversary.

This proof gives eventual almost-sure success. It gives no uniform learning rate, maximum number of errors, bound on memory, expected restoration time or guaranteed stopping announcement. Probabilities can be arbitrarily small and different rows arbitrarily close. A physical application requiring safe behaviour at every moment or recovery before a deadline would need additional premises and a stronger result.

# Three examples that distinguish the claims

## Static success can fail after a change

Two states q and r and one lawful action are enough. In mode 0 the system alternates between q and r. In mode 1 it remains at whichever state it currently occupies. Start at q. Both modes give priority 0 to departures from q and priority 1 to departures from r.

The only policy succeeds under either fixed model: mode 0 returns to q forever, and fixed mode 1 stays at q. A change after the first receipt traps the system at r, with priority 1 forever. No memory or randomisation can repair this one-action failure. Two observed states and one action are minimal for this precise static-versus-one-change existence gap. The example already uses one common priority map; hidden disagreement about the objective is unnecessary.

## Finite certificates can describe unbounded memory

For the Bernoulli control, two physical states encode receipts 0 and 1, and both actions are always lawful. Mode 0 emits a genuine receipt 1 with probability one third; mode 1 emits it with probability two thirds, independently of the chosen action. Correct-mode actions have priority 2 and wrong-mode actions priority 1. Success requires eventually choosing only the final mode's action.

Choosing action 1 when the mean of genuine receipts exceeds one half, and action 0 otherwise, wins almost surely under every legal one-change adversary. Any finite pre-change prefix is eventually diluted. Yet no finite-memory time-homogeneous randomised controller wins: the two fixed-mode product chains have identical support graphs and reachable bottom components, while success requires opposite eventual actions in those same components. An unbounded clock or stored parameter affecting future rules cannot be counted as free finite memory.

Replacing the second bias by one third preserves every support set and destroys the winner. No change and immediate change then have identical observation-action laws but require disjoint eventual-action events. Thus support alone does not decide the general hidden-priority problem. The underlying fixed-model memory obstruction is inherited; this tranche verifies its exact one-change embedding and its diagnostic role.

## Stale data can permanently reject the true mode

A third control adds a transient state visited only before the change. Its single stored receipt matches the old row exactly. Once the system leaves, that row is never resampled. If the controller tests every row with a positive sample count, the frozen old observation rejects every true-mode-1 phase forever. Wrong-mode actions consequently recur infinitely often on a genuinely solvable input.

The actual strict count gate prevents that failure. The transient row's count remains one, so it cannot reject a mode-1 phase whose number is already at least one. More generally, every finitely sampled stale row eventually stops participating. This proves that the precise guard is necessary for this controller's argument. It does not establish that every ungated algorithm fails or that no alternative forgetting or resampling method could work.

# Decision complexity and exact representation

The first reference procedure enumerates finite pair subsets and checks the complete criterion directly. It establishes an executable fidelity baseline, but that enumeration can be exponential. A separate refinement uses maximal end components, or MECs: maximal closed strongly connected pair sets under the relevant transition model. Priority thresholds recover qualifying smaller components that an unrestricted maximal component could hide.

The thresholds use distinct priority values present in the input, rather than all integers up to the largest encoded priority. In the equal-row branch, two priority minima may need different witnessing actions, so a double-threshold test is required. The solver preserves the union of qualifying target states without claiming that its representative component list is identical to the exhaustive list. Finite reachability, bounded descending region iteration and polynomial-size witnesses then supply a polynomial-time decision and certificate-construction route.

The ordinary lower bound matches this classification. A self-contained reduction encodes polynomial-time computation through an acyclic stochastic AND/OR construction. Choice of a successful child implements OR; equal-probability branching to both children implements AND for almost-sure reachability. Hardness already holds with identical kernels and identical state-based priorities 0 and 1, two actions lawful everywhere, and row entries drawn from 0, one half and 1. The nonterminal transition graph is acyclic.

Thus the explicit rational-input existence problem is P-complete under deterministic logarithmic-space many-one reductions. This is an ordinary mathematical complexity result, independently checked by AI. It is not a kernel-checked complexity theorem, an optimal polynomial-degree result, an unconditional parallel lower bound or a bound on the controller's online learning time. The lower-bound construction does not need an inaccessible historical paper as a verified premise.

## What information determines the answer

For fixed menus and priorities, the finite winning region depends on the two exact positive-support masks and the mask marking pairs whose full rows are equal. A support mask says which receipts are possible; an equality mask records whether both complete numerical rows agree at a pair. Inputs with these same masks have the same existence answer. This does not make support alone sufficient, and it does not show that one numerical learning policy works for every realisation of the masks.

For arbitrary real stochastic kernels, each positive instance still has an effective deterministic winner. A finite program can contain exact support and equality masks together with suitable rational comparison centres and tolerance. This is a nonuniform existence theorem: the finite advice exists for each instance, but a procedure for extracting it from arbitrary real descriptions is not supplied.

Representation changes decidability. No total procedure can decide exact winner existence from computable fast-Cauchy names, even for a promised full-support family whose individual probabilities are rational. A vanishing or tiny dyadic perturbation can encode whether a machine halts. This does not contradict the rational solver: an exact fraction and an approximation procedure for a rational number provide different information about zero and equality.

# When support alone can guide learning

When the two modes have the same priority map, exact support masks, menus, priorities and the initial state suffice for more than the existence decision. One terminating procedure decides existence from these inputs. On positive instances it returns a certificate and one effective deterministic policy that wins for every compatible pair of real kernels; otherwise it returns a negative certificate excluding a winner for every compatible realisation. The policy is selected before those numerical probabilities and the adversary are chosen. It needs neither an equality mask nor numerical calibration nor a supplied lower bound on positive probabilities.

The change in objective removes the rival-priority difficulty. Every candidate component with an even minimum has the same priority labels under the eventual actual mode. The learner tests whether candidate-positive successors are appearing often enough, using a phase-dependent threshold. It deliberately leaves candidate-zero coordinates untested. At a stable phase it proves candidate-support inclusion in the actual support on recurring pairs. Full row equality is not obtained and is not needed.

The support input remains trusted. The theorem supplies no method to infer exact zeros from a finite observation sequence or extract exact support from arbitrary Cauchy names. Its program may use unbounded counters. A positive minimum probability exists after any one finite realisation is fixed, but it is a proof witness rather than a runtime input or a uniform learning-rate guarantee.

## Why general hidden priorities need calibration

The boundary is stronger than the failure of one particular compiler. Compare two Bernoulli instances with the same states, actions, lawful menus and hidden mode-owned priorities from the preceding example. In the first, mode 0 has bias one third and mode 1 two thirds. In the second, those biases are exchanged. Both have full support and an empty row-equality mask. Each instance has its own effective winning threshold policy, with opposite associations between the observed mean and the action.

No common policy, even one with arbitrary measurable private randomisation, wins both instances. No change in the first and immediate change in the second produce exactly the same receipt and action law for that policy, while requiring eventual action 0 and eventual action 1 respectively. Those two events are disjoint. Randomising a choice of policy merely enlarges the private seed and does not remove the contradiction.

This separates deciding existence from compiling one winner for every numerical realisation. The support and equality masks determine whether each instance has a winner. They need not contain enough information to associate an observed statistical pattern with the right hidden priority requirement. Exact rational coefficients can provide that association; per-instance real advice can provide it nonuniformly; common priorities make it unnecessary.

The results support a specific methodological lesson for the programme. A finite structural account of success, a finite description of a controller and a uniform method of constructing that controller are different achievements. The present mathematical proofs identify where each is available. They do not authorise action from an estimated model, establish real-world safety or convert a statistical tendency into a deadline.
