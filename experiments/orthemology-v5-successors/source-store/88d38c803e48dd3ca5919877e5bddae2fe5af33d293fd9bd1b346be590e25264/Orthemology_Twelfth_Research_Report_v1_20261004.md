## The findings in simple terms

### An explanation for a young reader

Imagine tracing a moving marker backwards through more and more independent random pushes. If those pushes keep adding uncertainty, there is a limit to how much of the marker's probability can fit inside a small fixed window. We proved that limit without assuming all the usual rules for adding infinitely many probabilities.

There is also a strange but consistent mathematical model in which every marker position is an ordinary finite number, yet every fixed bounded window has probability zero. This is a model to understand, not a machine we have built. We then examined what happens when an observer tries to use such bookkeeping. A reporting procedure can finish on every finite input while its predictions about fixed deadlines remain unusual. Under a particular rule for buying and selling, two individually tempting choices lead to a guaranteed loss. That tells us to reconsider the whole plan; it does not make the physical act of losing money impossible.

A long line of helpers can pass help along. The difficult question is whether that explains where the ability to help comes from in the first place. The philosophical review sharpens that question without claiming to have settled it.

When an organisation moves to another machine, keeping it working now and keeping its ability to move again are two different tests. It can pass one while failing the other. The continuation research asks exactly what must be kept, replaced or recovered for the next move. Published evidence now gives qualified support for both together in a limited case.

### An explanation for an older student

Three questions must be kept distinct. Is the history mathematically coherent? Is a specified observer able to acquire the required information? Is the resulting belief and action policy rational? Twelfth gives positive and negative answers at different combinations of assumptions. A contradiction in a probability specification, a limitation on observation, and a dominated policy have different consequences.

## Independent variation and bounded capture

Let Z have an ordinary centred Gaussian distribution with variance v greater than zero. Let Y be independent of Z, in the rectangle-event sense, under a nonnegative normalised ordinary real-valued finitely additive probability, with all rectangle, tail and sum events used here measurable. For a bounded interval I of length L, the probability that Y plus Z lies in I is at most the Gaussian mass of a centred interval of that length. Here Φ is the standard normal distribution function. \[F1\]

$$
P(Y+Z\in{}I)\ \le{}\ 2\Phi{}(\frac{L}{2\sqrt{v}})-1\ \le{}\ \frac{L}{\sqrt{2\pi{}v}}
$$

The proof does not assume that Y has a tight law, that the joint law is countably additive, or that a conditional density for Y exists. A finite partition controls Y on a large bounded interval. Outside that interval, the event that the sum falls inside I forces Z into an ordinary Gaussian tail. Refining the partition and enlarging the interval yields the bound. The sharper expression is the exact maximum mass of an interval of length L under the Gaussian marginal.

Apply this to a terminal state obtained by adding to an earlier state a block of n fresh standard Gaussian innovations whose joint law is the ordinary iid product law. The accumulated innovation has variance n. If the same terminal law and independence conditions apply for arbitrarily large n, the upper bound tends to zero for every fixed bounded terminal interval. One independently warranted positive bounded-capture probability already contradicts that package. Full countable additivity of the terminal law is stronger than what the argument needs.

The result concerns one probability interpretation and information state. A posterior obtained after learning a reading cannot supply the positive-capture premise while independence is silently borrowed from a different prior. Conversely, an appropriately specified observation or comparative likelihood can provide evidence for capture; the warning is not that observation supplies no evidence.

## What the coherent comparison history establishes

The companion construction retains a complete ordinary independent Gaussian noise process. It starts with increasingly broad uniform anchor laws and takes one common finitely additive limit on all Borel events. The resulting history obeys the pointwise recurrence, has the intended stationary position laws, and preserves the full past–future independence statements. Every bounded position interval has probability zero. \[F1, F2\]

This is stronger mathematical control than specifying a few matching moments or independent pairs. Fixed finite forward interventions can also be interpreted through explicit maps which preserve the earlier history and change only the appropriate later states. The interventions are pushforwards, not conditioning on null events. These achievements establish neither a physical sampler nor the actual realisability of this whole history by the support of the admitted correction.

The comparison is useful because it identifies the exact force of positive capture. Removing that premise changes the mathematical verdict. It does not establish an adequate source-free cosmos, nor cancel the explanatory advantage of original possession simply by providing a coherent probability model.

## How much coordination bounded capture requires

Positive bounded capture constrains explanations that replace freshness with coordination. Write X₀=Yₙ+Sₙ, where Sₙ has the ordinary Gaussian law N(0,n), and retain the same terminal law across horizons. Let Pₙ be the joint law of (Yₙ,Sₙ), and let Qₙ be a rectangle-independent benchmark on the same event domain with the same two marginals, including Sₙ∼N(0,n). For an interval I of length L\>0 and capture probability p under Pₙ, comparison with Qₙ gives a direct separation. With total variation defined as the supremum of event-probability differences, its maximum is one. \[O1\]

$$
{d}_{TV}({P}_{n},{Q}_{n})\ \ge{}\ p-{a}_{n}(L),\ \ \ \ \ {a}_{n}(L)=2\Phi{}(\frac{L}{2\sqrt{n}})-1
$$

The separating event is simply Yₙ+Sₙ∈I. This inequality needs no joint density and can accommodate finitely additive laws on the common event domain. With a tight terminal law, choose a high-probability bounded interval first, then increase n. The distance tends to one. The innovation sequence can remain iid throughout: the separation concerns its dependence on the earlier state.

A further information bound requires ordinary countably additive Borel joint laws. Here Qₙ is the product of the state and sum marginals, and mutual information is relative entropy D(Pₙ∥Qₙ), possibly infinite. Let qₙ=Qₙ(Yₙ+Sₙ∈I). Grouping outcomes into capture and noncapture gives binary data processing. With natural logarithms and binary entropy h(p), this yields \[O2\]

$$
I({Y}_{n};{S}_{n})\ \ge{}\ d(p\parallel{}{q}_{n})\ \ge{}\ p\ log(\frac{1}{{q}_{n}})-h(p)
$$

$$
I({Y}_{n};{S}_{n})\ \ge{}\ \frac{p}{2}log\ n-p\ log(\frac{L}{\sqrt{2\pi{}}})-h(p)
$$

The proof averages the density ratio separately inside and outside the capture event and applies convexity. A joint law not absolutely continuous with respect to its product-marginal benchmark Qₙ has infinite relative entropy, so it presents no exception. A tight fixed terminal law supplies p arbitrarily close to one, establishing an asymptotic lower coefficient of one half. This is attained by an independent Gaussian anchor A of variance τ²\>0 with Yₙ=A−Sₙ: its mutual information is $\frac{1}{2}log(1+\frac{n}{{\tau{}}^{2}})$. The resulting history has state–future dependence; it is not a fresh-innovation control.

If a discrete record Mₙ completely mediates that dependence, meaning Yₙ and Sₙ are conditionally independent given Mₙ, the information chain rule bounds I(Yₙ;Sₙ) by H(Mₙ). A uniformly bounded record entropy therefore cannot sustain fixed positive capture at arbitrarily remote horizons. Complete mediation is essential. Correlated preparation, stored transcripts and side information must be counted. A short formula or finite controller is not automatically a complete bounded-capacity record.

## The sharp bound for finitely many coordination modes

There is a stronger exact result when the complete mediator M has at most N values. Under an ordinary joint law, S∼N(0,n) and conditional independence of Y and S given M imply \[O2\]

$$
P(Y+S\in{}I)\ \le{}\ {a}_{n}(NL)=2\Phi{}(\frac{NL}{2\sqrt{n}})-1
$$

For each mediator label, take its subprobability measure on the noise axis. Conditional independence makes that label's capture probability an average of the masses of translated length-L intervals. Approximate each largest such mass by one interval. All N selected intervals together occupy length at most NL, while their submeasures sum to the Gaussian marginal. A centred interval of that total length has the largest Gaussian mass. This proves the bound without assuming that the maximising translations exist.

The bound is attained at one horizon by dividing the centred length-NL interval into N cells, assigning each cell its own label and translating that cell into I. Set Y to the corresponding constant. Outside values receive an existing label without additional capture. For 0\<p\<1, capture at least p thus requires $N\ge{}\frac{2\sqrt{n}}{L}{\Phi{}}^{-1}(\frac{1+p}{2})$, rounded upward. No finite N gives probability-one capture. This sharpness permits choosing the state marginal and encoder; it does not preserve an independently prescribed terminal law or construct a common forward-causal history at every horizon.

## When a resource bound supplies positive capture

Let C mean successful acquisition, let T≥0 measure resources, and suppose completion within each finite budget m guarantees a bounded state magnitude b(m). All probabilities refer to one prospective information state. Then \[O3\]

$$
C\cap{}\{T\le{}m\}\ \subseteq{}\ \{|{X}_{0}|\le{}b(m)\}
$$

$$
P(|{X}_{0}|\le{}b(m))\ \ge{}\ P(C)-P(T>m)
$$

This follows by splitting successful acquisitions according to whether they exceed the budget and using finite subadditivity. If P(C)≥s\>0, one warranted tail bound P(T\>m)\<s suffices. Tightness of the resource marginal is a convenient stronger condition, and independence between resources and state is unnecessary. Under the diffuse history, every bounded-position event is null, so that successful magnitude-sensitive contract cannot have a tight resource distribution.

## Why the two transactions produce a sure loss

Suppose μ supplies the trader's operative previsions, the exact-report ratios guide subsequent valuation, and the required opportunities persist until the pulse. Buy a genuine unit A-ticket for ½−ε before the branch is known and resell that same ticket for ε after the report, where 0\<ε\<¼. Each isolated transaction has expected gain ε at its own information state. Together they yield \[O3, O5\]

$$
[{1}_{A}-(\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon{})]+[\varepsilon{}-{1}_{A}]=-\frac{1}{2}+2\varepsilon{}<0
$$

This is a fixed loss in every outcome, using two bounded transactions. Outright resale or issuer buyback with cancellation locks it in at report time; actual later settlement is unnecessary on that executed path. The ticket must nevertheless have its warranted contingent value if retained. Delay, costs, transfer terms and stake values must preserve the stipulated comparison.

Abstention therefore dominates the completed plan. The second sale already has negative prior expectation, so this is not a static collection of initially favourable bets. An agent anticipating the continuation may reject the first purchase as part of a losing plan. It may instead restrict later action or revise the operative model. These responses address different commitments and have different epistemic costs; the arithmetic does not select a unique repair.

## What a finite observation can distinguish

The coherent history also constrains how its empirical consequences should be described. For any fixed finite collection of bounded measurable outcome criteria and any positive tolerance, suitable ordinary finite-anchor laws approximate all those probabilities or expectations arbitrarily closely. Each tolerance condition holds on a set of radii belonging to the fixed nonprincipal ultrafilter. Their finite intersection still belongs to it and contains radii above any chosen bound. Uniform approximation by finite-valued functions transfers the event identity to bounded expectations. This is a direct consequence of the accepted construction. The radius can depend on the selected criteria and tolerance; there need not be one radius for every finite test, eventual convergence along all radii, or an effective way to compute a suitable radius from an arbitrary measurable specification.

A specified measurable observation or intervention protocol can be included in those criteria, with its transcript, stopping rule and side information retained. A finite output alphabet then permits approximation of its entire output law. A countably infinite transcript requires more care: matching each finite list of events does not establish small total variation over all events. This is not a general theorem that finite experiments cannot distinguish finite additivity.
