
## Electronic pages 2–4 (continuous-first-pass)

The received text opens with its title/author, basmala and authorial preface. Decree and wisdom are presented as integral to justice, praise, monotheism and law, rather than decree isolated from normative purposes. The initial epistemic hierarchy privileges revelation as elucidated by prophetic speech, companions and followers; denunciations of rivals are the author's polemical classification, not independently verified histories of whole schools.
The text sets up two opposed reductions: making human action independent of divine will/creation, versus denying the creature's action, power and voluntary distinction altogether. The latter view is explicitly an opponent's position. On e4 the reported collapse of will, love and command allegedly licenses moral indifference; a reported exchange with Ibn Taymiyya rebuts loving what God condemns. This report is not a second independent source. The same polemical bundle denies powers in fire, water, medicine and perception, purpose, real good/bad distinctions, enduring accidents and bodily natures. Ibn al-Qayyim is attacking these denials. Thus genuine created causality and moral differentiation are controls present before his detailed positive argument. Do not cite the water/fire denials as his thesis. Textual defects include repetition «لا يقدرون يقدرون» and awkward «ولا اختيار»; they are retained unchanged and not used as precise doctrinal premises.

## Electronic pages 5–9 (continuous-first-pass)

The preface finishes the anti-reductionist polemic, gives thirty chapter topics and explicitly marks divine decree's study as practical necessity. The planned structure includes nonmetaphysical-seeming but integral concerns: daily and embryonic determinations, how decree motivates effort, rival dialogues, satisfaction with decree, and innate disposition. It announces both causality and morally purposeful acts; this is authorial program, not proof of its fulfillment. The opening also attributes success to God and fallibility to the writer, asking readers not to reject unfamiliar reasoning through partisan dislike. Corrupt poetic fragments and the chapter 17 title «الكتب» (not silently corrected to a familiar alternative) remain witness defects.
Chapter1 argues from reported prophetic traditions for determination before heaven/earth and priority of throne over pen, combining temporal clauses rather than assuming every 'first' unconditional. The written decree is correlated with realized acts, but the reports are received religious testimony, not independently authenticated cosmology. The Ibn Abbas instruction pairs asking God with the bounded ability of all creatures to benefit/harm only within decree. Abu Hurayra's celibacy/castration-related narrative is reported, not an endorsed practical recommendation. Chapter2's funeral narrative answers the inference from predestination to inaction with 'work': facilitated action is the route to the decreed result. The text includes interpretive disputes over pronouns, variants in reports about drinking and accepted prayer versus repentance, and the author's correction against falsely attributing a report. Covenant/offspring/Adam-David traditions begin; individual wealth, poverty and gratefulness are connected by the reported divine answer. These distinct narrative and isnad claims require attribution rather than flattening into one philosophical premise.

## Electronic pages 10–14 (continuous-first-pass)

The covenant dossier is not transmitted uncritically: e10 rejects al-Hakim's asserted Muslim condition for an isnad, quotes Abu Umar on interruption/unknown transmitters, distinguishes the Medina transmitter from the Basran ascetic, and separates corroboration of meaning from soundness of that particular route. Numerous prophetic/companion reports follow with chains; black/white imagery belongs to these transmitted eschatological narratives, not a modern biological judgement. E12–13 then refuses to identify all this narrative detail directly with Qur'an7:172: the verse speaks of children of Adam from their backs, while the reports describe Adam; innate acknowledgment and messengers establish the accessible argument, rather than an inaccessible first scene functioning by itself as culpability evidence. This is a significant internal source control.
Chapter3's Adam–Moses debate raises the real danger that decree-as-excuse abolishes prophecy, law and blame. The author's defenses of the report's reception and attacks on rival schools remain attributed polemic. He surveys and rejects readings based solely on parental seniority (truth prevails even against a father/master), different dispensations, mere repentance, or postmortem venue. He also rejects a mystical reading where witnessing decree erases badness and blame, indeed judging that approach worse than opponents' rejection of the report. The Avicenna passage at the end is a hostile reported formulation, not independent verification of Avicenna's complete position. The criterion of truthful argument independent of rank, and the refusal to license wrongdoing through metaphysical contemplation, are important epistemic/moral controls not exhausted by uniqueness arguments.

## Electronic pages 15–19 (continuous-first-pass)

E15 distinguishes truth of universal divine willing from the illicit inference that whatever occurs is loved, commanded or excuses continued disobedience. The same sentence can be true metaphysically yet misused pragmatically against law. In reading al-Ansari, the author refuses the apparent suspension of good/bad judgement and interprets ambiguous utterance against clearer ethical commitments. Al-Wasiti's long mystical-psychological explanation is quoted as a charitable account of temporary narrowed attention, not extinction of obligations: higher absorbed awareness can conceal still-present discrimination, and sobriety restores it. The author's synthesis at e17 seeks simultaneous attention to divine decree and the creature's own action/acquisition. The text announces 'two aspects' but explicitly articulates only the first before referring to both; this received wording is a local incompleteness, not silently supplied text.
E18 credits the calamity-versus-fault interpretation of Adam's answer to 'our shaykh', then offers another possible answer distinguishing a completed repented act from persistence in present/future wrongdoing. Both conserve accountability. Sleep without negligence and optional night prayer provide discriminating counterexamples. E18–19's strong-believer hadith becomes a practical account of excellence: energetic pursuit of the genuinely beneficial, with divine assistance, before the event; acceptance of decree rather than disabling regret after what cannot be recovered. Willing effort, help and realized success are not collapsed, and failure of an outcome does not show absence of effort. The opening of chapter 4 moves to embryonic determination and harmonizing hadith chronology, an orthogonal biological/testimonial topic that remains part of the continuous study. Duplicate clauses on e15 and e18 and awkward spellings remain uncorrected.

## Electronic pages 20–24 (continuous-first-pass)

Chapter4 continues the full report dossier on embryonic writing: forty/forthy-two/forthy-five day and later ensoulment reports are not simply erased into one chronology. E22 proposes distinct commissions for the resident embryo angel and the soul's arrival, allowing more than one recording. This is the author's harmonization of reports, not independently verified embryology; all medical/biological timing remains historical source content. The substantial moral sermon attributed to Ibn Masud condemns false promises, joking lies, slander and neglect, and links repeated truthful/false acts with acquired character. Reports on epidemic relocation, souls' leaves, and children's destiny are also retained: the author's reconciliation restricts testimony about a particular child while identifying groups in prophetic vision, not an independently established judgement on actual children.
Chapter5 distinguishes the annual Night of Decree from the mid-Shaban view and interprets qadr primarily as determination, permitting honour as concomitant rather than sole meaning. Chapter6 presents ongoing daily acts of giving, healing, elevating and forgiving: decree is not depicted as a motionless agency-inventory. The elaborate cosmic-hours tradition is attributed with differing chains; none is independently authenticated here. E24 orders lifelong, yearly and daily determinations as detail of the previous writing, linked to perfect knowledge/power/wisdom and instruction of angels. Chapter7 poses the central practical challenge: if written destiny is fixed, why intermediate action? Its answer begins with the repeated funeral/effort tradition; this opening makes the explanatory status of means, not just human comfort, a live question.

## Electronic pages 25–29 (continuous-first-pass)

E25–26 is a central affirmative causal text: prior decree runs through causes the servant has been empowered and enabled to use; more diligent pursuit can bring the decreed result nearer. Learning, reproduction, farming, hunger/thirst and animal effort supply ordinary differentiated examples. Divine prior giving comprises the end, route and enabling, so secondary agency is real but derivative. The practical theological conclusion is concurrent trust and effort, not suspension of either. This does not yet prove that all possible underived agents must be numerically one: that stronger classification would need more than the examples of dependent animals and people.
Chapter8 treats prior divine favour and an objection about worshiped angels/Jesus/Ezra. It distinguishes grammatical scope from an analogy based on alleged common rationale, then answers by innocence, difference between insentient stones and suffering beings, and the difference between the purported object of worship and demons endorsing it. The account refuses punishment of innocent persons for others' worship; that moral premise matters beyond the immediate polemic. Reports of visions/healing and a camel killing a reviler are transmitted stories, not independently observed evidence. Alternative interpretations of prior writing before Badr are combined as compatible rather than made exclusive. Chapter9 again rejects a weak anti-Qadari report as unestablished, then distinguishes decree-denial from decree-as-excuse. The argument that qadar includes power connects divine acts and creation of creature acts without settling all detailed metaphysics. Chapter10 begins the four grades: knowledge, writing, will, creation. E29 contains «على الرب» where the later explanatory phrase identifies prior knowledge; retain the error rather than silently editing. Repeated phrases in the omnipotence paragraph are also retained.

## Electronic pages 30–34 (continuous-first-pass)

The prior-knowledge chapter treats unrealized conditionals (what children or untested persons would have done), innate disposition and responsibility. E30–31 distinguishes an initial withholding of guidance known fitting to its subject from punitive subsequent misguidance after refusal. This is the source's moral explanatory claim; it leaves a serious question about creation of those initial receptivities rather than resolving it by the word 'fitting'. Multiple exegetical opinions are recorded, not all adopted. E32 supplies a sharp lexical control: Qur'anic/language 'choice' as preferred selection is narrower than kalam choice as bare will; inference from willed sin to loved/approved sin trades on that ambiguity. Religious versus cosmic will and permission are likewise distinguished. The author also asserts that preferential choice involves a ground of preference, which gives wisdom substantive content without yet demonstrating the necessity of every token act.
E33's istikhara exposition separates knowledge of benefit, capacity, facilitation, sustained blessing/growth and satisfaction; enabling capacity alone does not secure actual realisation. These are derivative human conditions, not automatically coequal divine conditions. E34 explains apparently obstructive events through later outcomes: Hudaybiyya as an opening for safe encounter and argument, Joseph's chain of suffering as a hidden route to benefit. These are source interpretations and theological exemplars, not independently verified universal theodicy. Practical patience and gratitude condition the believer's benefit, preserving differentiated response rather than mechanical identical good from all suffering. The work's historical, devotional and psychological materials remain part of the record.

## Electronic pages 35–39 (continuous-first-pass)

E35–36 continues providential narratives but adds an important justice distinction: prior divine knowledge is not itself the creature's deed or ground of deserved punishment. Testing makes known dispositions actual in acquired deeds before praise/blame and reward/punishment attach, with messengers providing a case against the agent. Testimony about the healed leper, bald man and blind man illustrates gratitude, memory and truthful acknowledgment. Birth and nurture are then used to infer creator, perfection, revelation and resurrection, with several bridging evaluative premises ('not fitting to abandon them') that should be separately assessed rather than treated as bare existential consequences.
E37 is an exceptionally explicit secondary-cause control: a benefactor can be part of the cause, but does not independently create; God gives both cause and outcome, can work without that cause, withdraw its causality, introduce an opposing cause, or connect a contrary outcome. This affirms real defeasible causality rather than the e4 opponent's denial, while denying self-sufficiency of received causes. The gratitude argument adds that even acquisition and thanks are given capacities/acts. This is the source's complete-original-giving picture, not by itself a proof that the dependence witnessed among creatures excludes every conceivable plurality of originals.
E37–38 also rejects wealth and power as reliable marks of divine love, election or desert. Competing readings of Qarun's 'knowledge' are evaluated grammatically and morally: personal technical expertise versus presumed divine recognition of merit. Neither makes prosperity proof of favour. E39 distinguishes knowing truth from following it, hence epistemic ignorance from practical misdirection; accepted alternatives are not conflated. Chapter11 begins writing and treats different occurrences of 'dhikr' and 'zubur' by contextual semantics, then reads promised inheritance as a prophetic sign. These historical religious arguments remain attributed rather than externally certified.

## Electronic pages 40–44 (continuous-first-pass)

E40 distinguishes prior decree-recording from concurrent recording of acts and their continuing consequences. The derivative effects of action count toward moral accounting, a real extension beyond immediate bodily acts; walking to prayer is one example, not an exhaustive interpretation. Source-critical restraint appears in the Meccan sura/Medinan Banu Salima narrative: the author prefers application/reminder over assuming a contradictory first-revelation chronology, while leaving alternatives explicit. E40–42 stages both readings of 'book' (Qur'an/preserved register), adduces their strongest contextual cases, then judges rather than flattening the disagreement. Animals' communities and return to God supply cosmic and moral context. Scripture's generic wording may contain principles rather than every individual detail verbatim. Claims of consensus are the author's claims.
Chapter12 opens universal will with a long scriptural dossier. It targets both philosophers denying divine will altogether and those excluding creature acts from it. Its summary distinguishes what actually occurs through will, what does not occur without will, and what could be otherwise had the will differed, including a different decree. Thus one must not infer each actual token effect's eternal necessity merely from total sovereignty. Ordinary natural variation, rain, fertility, hearing, sight, wealth and guidance occur alongside history and salvation; this is deliberately comprehensive governance. These scriptural premises provide a positive theological account, not a neutral independent derivation that has already defeated every common-source alternative. Minor electronic defects include doubled function words and «أذلوا»; no correction is used as evidence.

## Electronic pages 45–49 (continuous-first-pass)

The will dossier moves through sleeping, prayer, dreams, conditional speech and oaths. 'What God wills and you will' is distinguished from subordinate 'then'; the author quotes al-Shafii on dependent human will rather than denying it. Al-Shafii's contextual explanation of the time tradition attributes agency to God without making temporal succession a deity. Reports, lexical claims and authorial explanations must remain separate. E47 treats delayed recollection of 'if God wills' as devotional remembrance, not retroactive invalidation of legal undertakings, preserving a legal/ordinary-life limit.
E48 gives a four-way differentiation: loved and occurring; loved but not occurring; disliked but occurring; disliked and not occurring. Creative will is therefore not normative approval. It interprets the destruction verse's 'command' causally rather than prescriptively, with syntactic and historical arguments; cumulative wrongdoing and a final ratifying act are distinguished, so a final occasion does not erase earlier culpability. This strong punitive causation thesis remains philosophically difficult in relation to initial receptivity, not solved by asserting the labels. E49 restates total effectual will but immediately attacks both uncreated autonomous creature acts and the opposing denial of any creature causal contribution. The latter extreme's claim that acts are literally God's and only metaphorically man's is explicitly rejected as comparable or worse in error. The chapter begins evaluating a weaker acquired-act account through effects on a property rather than existence. This is neither flat occasionalism nor a concession of independent original human sources.

## Electronic pages 50–54 (continuous-first-pass)

E50 offers unusually exact productive distinctions. It reports property-only acquisition, then examines joint effects: cooperation where neither alone can do the whole, alternation, and actual joint carrying where either could alone do the whole are all possible/actual. What it declares impossible is each actually effecting the very product independently; the reason is that each independence excludes the other's doing. This is a narrow actual-whole-alone contradiction. It does not, without an added productive-individuation premise, prove that all nonexclusive double sufficiency is contradictory. The subsequent account rejects making human power an independently sufficient creator even by divine assistance: it is at most a cause or part of the cause. The effectual source is divine will. Be precise about the several quoted school positions and the author's own evaluating voice.
E51–52 explicitly seeks the truth possessed by each opposing party: universal divine power/will and genuine human power, will, action and choice. True arguments for either need not defeat the other. The practical epistemic norm is to judge adversaries justly rather than answer one falsehood with another. E52 separates the act from its product: knowledge, will and creation belong to God; the creature's praying, moving and acquiring really inhere in the creature, and are created effects. E53 refuses reduction of creating to prior knowing/reporting. E54 argues that Creator/created semantic scope does not include the Creator's attributes among 'everything created', and denies both externally autonomous creature action and punishing one for a deed not theirs. Generic possible-action omnipotence is a positive premise, not independently settled by bare necessary being.
Textual uncertainties, unrepaired: e50 'كما يمتنع' in an asserted permissive analogy is suspect; its lead-in says only divine power independent without a clear contrasting human clause; 'على سبيل' ends incompletely before a school attribution; e54 'لا ينبغي أن تكون' appears opposite surrounding explicit assertion that acts are both real human deeds and divinely created. These clauses must not bear a reconstructed proof. The clear enumerated cooperation distinctions and repeated act/product explanation are available without repairing those defects.

## Electronic pages 55–59 (continuous-first-pass)

E55 argues from manufactured shelters, armor and boats that attribution to God's creating includes form achieved by human workmanship, not raw matter alone. It declines the superficially stronger infinitival reading of 'what you make' in Abraham's speech in favour of the contextual relative-object reading, yet still draws creation of worked objects through human action. Divine making of leaders, inspiration and prayer for states of character are explained as more than naming or teaching. E56 reasons from creation to intimate knowledge; the inference that removing one evidence of knowledge leaves none requires care and is not independently established here. The distinction between legislative and creative 'making' blocks counterexamples involving forbidden ritual statuses.
E57–58 separates patience from making-patient, love from making-love, and formed affection from mere exhortation. It argues against making everyone's divine aid identical to public instruction and making reward an autonomous debt owed for unassisted merit. The root of error is again failing to distinguish God's act from creature act: incitement/permission and actual mutual hatred are different causal levels. E59 classifies outgoing actions into voluntary, involuntary and commanded, preserving real modality differences.
Especially important epistemically: E59 expressly refuses use of 'you did not throw when you threw' as a blanket proof annihilating creature efficacy. In its reading, the Prophet really cast, while reaching the enemies at that distance and scope was the distinct divine result; angelic participation likewise limits the soldiers' sole-killing claim. This is productive decomposition of the event, not the assertion that one numerically identical complete act is both independently performed twice. It strengthens the source's affirmative no-independent-creature picture while restraining facile proof-texting. Corrupt/awkward wording at e57 ('غير مقدور العبد') remains recorded rather than adopted as a denial of voluntary power.

## Electronic pages 60–64 (continuous-first-pass)

The created-action evidence distinguishes showing lightning from merely creating a visible thing; seeing is the recipient's real act, making-see the divine operation. Maturation and intentional retrieval of treasure are unlike actions, yet both within will. Created human anxiety is the human's trait, not a trait transferred to its Creator. E60–61 quotes al-Tabari on natural signs and guidance, then treats the 'bird in the neck' as attached action/destiny through linguistic and exegetical testimony. Polemical claims that no predecessor endorsed an alternative are source claims, not verified exhaustive negatives.
E62 critically reevaluates earlier exegetes on the pronoun in 'We thread it into their hearts': continuity of pronouns favours the Qur'an entering without belief, not 'they do not believe in disbelief'. The author harmonizes the older interpretations as accounts of consequence while preserving his literal reading. This shows grammatical scrutiny even when an easier proof text would serve his conclusion. E62–63 distinguishes sending/empowering demons from bare moral command, through ordinary sending and the legally relevant case of releasing a trained hunting dog. Created natures are invoked: releasing what has a disposition to act while not preventing it is causal deployment. Refuge and granting-refuge are distinct acts; both schools can establish the half they affirm without erasing the other.
E63–64 develops patience, firmness and victory as created yet voluntary bodily/argumentative acts. The inattention discussion adds a psychological claim: intentionally making oneself unaware requires awareness of that object, unlike divine making-inattentive. This may require temporal qualification (one can deliberately arrange later forgetting), so it is not a universally settled modern cognitive argument. Will is again differentiated from command in Shuayb's possible-return passage. Textual defects include «وهذا لا ينبغي أن يكون فعلا للعبد حقيقة» against its immediate positive continuation, and attribution 'قول هود وما توفيقي' though the later section identifies Shuayb. E80 answers the justice objection by distinguishing public enabling conditions and intelligible communication from a further gift of effectual aid; those without reason, discriminatory maturity or received message are not punished before a case is made. This is a principled restriction on responsibility, but the causal relation of withheld aid to failure remains a philosophical issue. The author again rebukes determinists for denying causes, powers and the creature's influence, arguing that attacking an opponent with false premises entrenches rather than cures error.
E81 defines the effectual guidance as involving the divine cause and genuinely actual creaturely being-guided. E82–84 analyses seal, covering and hardening vocabulary, rejects reducing divine action to a label or an observed state, and demands lexical possibility, established use and a defeating reason before nonliteral interpretation. ## Electronic pages 85–89 (continuous-first-pass)

Chapter15 presents the opposed strongest objections rather than merely slogans. The Qadari asks whether ordering entrance and then sealing the door, or ordering walking while shackled, could be just; such conduct would be worse for the perfectly self-sufficient merciful source than for a needy human. E86 explicitly concedes that their proposed moral account contains more truth than falsehood: punitive sealing follows actual chosen rejection. He rejects both reducing justice to whatever is possible and removing divine causation to preserve justice. E87 interprets God's straight path as just exercise of sovereignty, including no punishment for another's guilt, no deprivation of earned reward and no imposition beyond capacity; a second reading as unavoidable return/judgement is distinguished.
E88 reasons that conceding divine creation of punitive heart-states undermines a universal exclusion of creature acts from divine power. Satan's bounded agency is exhortation/beautifying motives, not sovereign production of the heart's ultimate state. Particularly valuable is the rejection of a false binary: a sura need neither independently originate disbelief nor merely coincide with it. Its arrival becomes a genuine cause through the hearer's believing or opposing response. This is an explicit intermediate causal relation, compatible with different outcomes given differing receptivity. E89 then separates ugly creature acts from the wise creation of them; act/product and decree/decreed remain distinct.
The claim that only coercion could convert a settled rejecter is rejected: divine creation of willing love would be voluntary belief, not externally forced speech. But the author answers why not give that aid by fittingness, diversity and self-created receptivity, saying the recipient, received capacity and reception are all God's effects. This is stronger than merely citing an external constraint on God, yet the moral rationale of creating a nonreceptive subject is not made transparent by the typology alone. Preserve that hard question rather than treating the author's dismissal of it as independent closure. Electronic 'درائهم' and other malformed words remain unamended.

## Electronic pages 90–94 (continuous-first-pass)

E90–91 develops habituation and unequal temporal control: early resistance is possible, while repeatedly cultivated passion or disease becomes entrenched and eventually beyond unaided reversal. Entrapment and disease are analogies for self-mediated loss of access, not a denial of all initial capacity. Yet divine release remains possible even after sealing; the author's contrast with fixed-destiny theories must not be collapsed into a claim that true divine knowledge becomes false. The causal point is differentiating the accessible means of requesting/taking remedy from the cure not directly commanded by the patient. A genuine tension is preserved: seeking release is said possible, while complete sealing can block even aversion and supplication. The text qualifies scope to particular persistently refusing persons, since many actual converts were once unbelievers. It does not license diagnosing any present individual as irremediably sealed.
E92 inventories distinct heart, ear, eye and speech impediments and argues that heart-life/death, sealing and blindness are real in their appropriate subject, not merely unreal metaphors because not wax/iron or ocular lesions. The heart-as-ruler model grounds embodied action; analogous terms vary by bearer without erasing reality. E93 rejects interpreting 'wrapped hearts' as self-praise for full wisdom, using usage, contextual parallels and the difference between original incapacity and punishment after chosen refusal. E94 treats invisible barrier as literally unseen rather than converting passive morphology to active, and carefully distinguishes light passing cloud over the heart from dense accumulated rust. Moral habits are causally consequential: chosen deeds cause a later state not itself under direct control, while both are created. The objection beginning e91 is visibly incomplete after 'prior ... acts'; preserve the syntactic gap, not a reconstructed wording. The disease analogy offers no current medical prescription.
