## First pass E135–144
END first-135-144 was visible, untruncated; SHA 256 183aaa5b74cb812d615309e225001c4010c94f96b48341c62584742bde37accf. Actual pages: E135, E137–144. E136 is not a credited distinct actual page.

- E135 title; E137 begins treatise 5: sincerity of religion to God is the root of beneficial knowledge and right action. Opening evidential modesty matters: the forty-mornings report is mursal; the alternative Anas chain has a severely weak transmitter; Ibn al-Jawzi treated it as fabricated. Ibn Taymiyya does not simply canonise the report, or declare either Sufi practitioners or critics wholly right. Each can possess partial truth and error (E137–138).
- E138–140 distinguishes the independently warranted value of sincerity from the specified forty-day period, whose support is only proposed by corroborating scriptural occurrences of forty and a disputed mursal. This analogical corroboration is weaker than direct attestation. The practical/legal conclusion also distinguishes lawful i'tikaf from invented conditions: perpetual silence, hunger, sleeplessness, prescribed food, obligatory initiating shaykh, and abandoning congregational/Friday worship. Pre-prophetic retreat at Hira is not itself post-revelation normative authority.
- E140: the Ghazali story has an expressly unknown isnad; its teaching can be right even when historicity is unestablished. Sincerity aimed primarily at obtaining wisdom is not sincerity to God as ultimate end. This separates moral/epistemic content from provenance.
- E141–143 reports rival views: Mu'tazilites and allied theologians deny God is lovable for himself and restrict ultimate reward to creaturely enjoyments; some vision-affirmers separate pleasure from vision of God; philosophers reduce vision to intellectual cognition. These are the author's characterisations, not independently verified histories. E142 editorial correction marks corrupt original wording. Prophetic prayer for delight in seeing God is the positive scriptural counterexample.
- E143 objects that philosophical universals exist qua universals in the intellect; enjoyment of universals is not thereby encounter with external divine reality. Distinguish this narrower conceptual claim from the polemical judgment that no philosopher rises above Mu'tazilite vision-theory.
- E144 advances an objection to the ordering: love presupposes cognition, so how can divine love be the origin if prior inquiry needs a prior desired object? First answer: basic recognition of God is fitri/necessary, not universally the product of inference, although corrupted dispositions may need inference. This is an epistemic starting-point defence, not yet a derivation of all theological predicates.

## First pass E145–154
END first-145-154 was visible, untruncated; SHA 256 44fb283636f1559a38254c99b3abd84e2f8250425f393f170e538d39e6a76223. Actual pages E145–154.

- E145–146 second answer: cognition/love can begin generally, then become determinate. A seeker of truth as truth is said implicitly to seek God, the pure truth sustaining all creatures. Explicit necessary-being move: total reality cannot consist entirely of originated possibles; at least an ancient necessary existent must be admitted. The further identification of every sincere truth-search with God-directed love is an extra bridge, not simply the quantificational existence claim.
- E146–150 moral epistemology: sincerity plus maximal effort yields guidance; intense love or aversion generates strategies for obtaining/avoiding its object; partisan desire instead selects conclusions. The psychological observation about motivated inquiry is intelligible independently; the universal guarantee and classification of all deviation as desire need the theological premises.
- E148 linguistic criticism of Ibn Arabi's hawā passage: if hawā means the desired object, every such object is desired and the supposed privileged status of desire disappears; if it means the act/state of desiring, worship is confused with the object worshiped. Keep this as an act/object distinction, not an independent proof of monotheism.
- E149–151 scriptural network connects guidance with obedient action and sincere worship, and unity of the prophetic religion with social concord. The author criticizes correct conclusions reached only because they happen to fit one's desires: agreement is not identical with truth-directed motivation.
- E151–153 begins systematic reconstruction. Voluntary action has an originating side (awareness, love/will, capacity) and a terminal side (intended loved object). A finite human cannot originate all his own action-principles: self-causation is circular; an infinity of simultaneously required acts or an infinity of past human acts is unavailable to the finite person. Crucially E152 differentiates complete simultaneous causes from temporally successive conditions, whose infinite succession is disputed; he declines to rest the human-action claim on a general ban on all temporal regress. E153 thinks origination is clearer here than Razi's possibility route. This secures dependence of human action-principles on causes beyond the person, not by itself that one cause owns all every perfection intrinsically.
- E153–154 unrestricted satisfaction of mutually conflicting desires undermines desired benefit; social coordination needs binding norms. The analysis is not confined to direct combat: failure to cooperate, failure to defend, mutual obstruction, and competition each destroy goods. E154 explicitly allows humanly enacted laws as social necessities before differentiating their ultimate adequacy. This is a legal-political coordination argument alongside metaphysical reasoning.

## First pass E155–164
END first-155-164 was visible, untruncated; SHA 256 4a232d4075988c498f6116ecadecf553e21ae7a87faa10c2abb05b373f620e66. Actual pages E155–164.

- E155: laws range from minimal worldly existence through worldly welfare to both worldly and otherworldly welfare; otherworldly welfare without any worldly rectitude is denied. Worldly rectitude is not luxurious consumption. Unrestricted whim is a self-defeating ultimate policy because obtaining some immediate wants frustrates the durable interests that motivated acting.
- E155–157 two senses of an invalid ultimate end: one should not rationally intend it as final, and it cannot actually deliver the complete sought good. People can nonetheless perform actions for failed ends, just as they can hold false beliefs. The legal analogy to invalid worship/contracts concerns failure of the intended legally recognised effect; it is not proof that an event with a failed end cannot occur.
- E157–160 strongest positive synthesis: the human is really an agent with power, will, belief, and intended benefit, but is not an independent creator of those principles; likewise the human receives genuine welfare and delight without being the absolutely ultimate object of worship. Efficient and final dependence are compatible with derivative agency and beneficiary status. God as creator/ultimate end must not be used to negate these. The author places some Sufis, Mu'tazilites, and Ash'arites in opposing partial accounts, but these are polemical classifications.
- E160–161 goodness/badness are linked to fittingness, harm, benefit, love and aversion, hence final causality. He rejects both denial of rationally knowable outcomes and an unqualified transfer of creaturely normative details to God. The positive moral realism is teleological; the passage does not solve all conflict between proximate and remote goods merely by naming them goods.
- E161 manuscript blank after a sentence, reported by editor. E162 another one-word lacuna interrupts the transition; register rather than silently reconstruct.
- E162–163 transient pleasure is a real subordinate final factor and desire a real originating factor, but neither is an ultimate self-sufficient principle. Analogy proposed: as an originated cause needs an originator, a vanishing end needs an abiding further end. The symmetry is an argument that requires defence, not an identity of concepts.
- E163 author explicitly says the divine-end claim has not yet been realised adequately and starts a third argument. This self-assessment matters: earlier passages should not be treated as a completed proof on his own account.
- E163–164 absolute nothing is neither producer nor objective; making something merely to erase it is pointless or concealed pursuit of another objective. Legal application: prohibited stratagems cannot be rendered innocent by ignoring intentions; deeds have their character through intentions. Admits actual human inconsistency of intentions just as in beliefs. Strong reading concerns coherent ultimate ends, not denial that people actually pursue contradictory aims.

## First pass E165–174
END first-165-174 was visible, untruncated; SHA 256 15ca6b3cc4428fef7639e1b2d03ce2d3157210f414550ffe6f98d0128ca5c078. Actual pages E165–174.

- E165 interprets the causal bā' in creation 'with truth' broadly enough to include both efficient cause and final cause. Arabic uses 'because of' both for an agent and for an objective. Rightly observing lexical breadth does not alone establish all substantive teleological claims. The author proposes rational/foundational knowledge of return as of origin, against a revelation-only theory.
- E166 criticizes philosophical exceptionalism: law admitted necessary for the populace, while philosophers exempt themselves from its truth and discipline. This is a consistency challenge conditional on the law's actual benefits and authority, not a stand-alone argument for revelation's every content.
- E166–167 crucial end-premise: a good that comes then disappears without producing any enduring good leaves the post-state equivalent to the pre-state and so cannot be a rational ultimate aim. Charitable interpretation: sustained total well-being cannot be secured by a policy of only vanishing gratifications. Stronger literal inference that a finite realised good has no noninstrumental value does not follow merely from its nonexistence later. This is a substantive value premise, not supplied by the necessary-being result.
- E168–169 interprets First/Last both temporally and as first intention/ultimate end; explicitly admits firstness of intention is not the hadith's sole apparent meaning. 'Last' is said not to require all other beings annihilated. Thus abiding finality is not straightforward universal creature-annihilation.
- E169–172 epistemology of ends and law: consequences can corroborate scriptural evidence about which actions are good, obligatory, or mistaken. The author applies this to virtues of early caliphs and the first civil war. These historical judgments are authorial evaluations, not independently checked facts. Detailed legal distinction: Qur'anic fighting of an aggressing faction is situated after conflict and attempted reconciliation, not a general license to initiate warfare for all prior injustice. A textual lacuna occurs E171 before continuation.
- E172–173 religious-intellectual desire can be worse than worldly desire, so the injunction is not merely ascetic control of appetites; sincere criticism must be possible also in doctrine and worship.
- E173–174 final-cause regress: an intended object is either desired in itself or for another; cycles give reciprocal explanatory dependence; endless instrumental ends would demand an infinite intended order in finite cognition, or simultaneously completed results. This at best targets complete/end-conferring explanation, not automatically every evolving project with indefinitely deferred goals.
- E174 relies on an inherited exhaustive classification of motion (forced, natural, voluntary), takes natural motion as restoration after external displacement, and concludes all motion ultimately derives from volition. This is a further cosmological premise, not analytic from motion alone. Combining it with the transient-end premise yields an eternal ultimate loved object. The argument would need renewed defence of the classification and of why no natural process is basic.

## First pass E175–184
END first-175-184 was visible, untruncated; SHA 256 671f989e7d6030932c9ec4bcd569574c88b99d3a787f896519a978b954752fd6. Actual pages E175–184.

- E175–177 extends the source/end parallel: a creature's very self is neither its own made product nor the ultimate ground of its own being/desirability; creaturely love, pleasure, and efficacy are derivative. Love of another is represented as exchange, compassion that relieves the helper's distress, or orientation to God. The claim that all non-divine interpersonal love reduces to a further benefit is asserted with a broad psychological generality needing support.
- E177 distinguishes the loved existent, which precedes action, from new contact/pleasure sought through action. The objection that an intended end need only exist in cognition beforehand is stated explicitly. Reply: desire needs an objectively desirable reality rather than conferring desirability on itself circularly. This is a stronger argument for a non-arbitrary ground of fitting desire than the bare claim that every prospective object must already exist externally. The inference from objective fittingness to an already existing final object remains to be earned.
- E178–180 the author explicitly criticizes kalam readings of Qur'an 21:22 limited to competing efficient makers: mutual prevention would predict no act, whereas the verse says corruption of an existent order. He intends a final-cause/welfare argument, with misdirected worship analogous to nourishment by glass or poison. Thus one must not replace his intended proof with only a two-wills stalemate. Conversely the verse's use of corruption is not alone a neutral derivation of his entire theory of value.
- E180–182 nonduplication is applied symmetrically to complete efficient and complete final causes: one numerically same effect cannot belong wholly and independently to two complete producers; one act cannot be wholly and finally devoted to two independent ultimate ends. Mixed worship is not a distributable valid share for God because it fails its condition of sincerity. He **expressly allows valid human partnerships** (E182), so this is not a universal prohibition of joint action or multiple subordinate motives. The analogy with a spoiled mixture illustrates a claimed religious condition; it is not itself proof that every mixture is spoiled.
- E183 extends the no-priority-cycle argument to a cause's constitutive part or prior condition: a thing cannot already supply what must precede its own origination. Source versus effect and priority versus simultaneity matter here.
- E184 immediately limits that prohibition: reciprocal conditions may coexist when their cause is other than either (dawr ma'i); prohibited circularity is priority/derivation (dawr qabli). Cooperators can each perform an action conditional on the other's, giving an outcome available only through their combined contributions. The precise central sentence in the electronic text is textually awkward ('مستفيدًا لا يحتاج فيه إليه'); do not silently repair it. Its elaboration prohibits the same enabling assistance being supplied in a derivation loop. Ordinary assistance with instruments is expressly excluded from that prohibition. Additional power generated by cooperation is attributed to the joint activity, not independently to either member. Thus there are two legitimate levels: possessed contributing capacities and a jointly realised strength/product.

## First pass E185–194
END first-185-194 was visible, untruncated; SHA 256 8aa7f1b8b5d32f607563f3fae4e428b28a60f14dd97af52c846ea02016e65063. Actual pages E185–194.

- E185 is the decisive cooperator passage. Each partner has power, awareness, intention, and a sought object; what each has on the originating/final sides is not simply borrowed in a closed loop from the other. Yet the sought result is obtained only by conjunction of their two intentions and activities. Reciprocal exchange can therefore be real without being reciprocal origination. The text calls the need one of conditions/conditioned items rather than causes for effects. Avoid forcing 'independent' to mean either physically separable in every possible circumstance or able alone to produce the joint product. Neither is asserted here.
- E186 generalizes even within one agent's use of multiple causes: plants through rain, rain through clouds, children through parents. Two items cannot each be the prior efficient cause or complete reason-for-the-other; they can share an external cause and end, and can coexist such that one is **with**, not **through**, the other, and one end is **with**, not **for the sake of**, the other. These are explicit modal/relational permissions. 'May have a common cause' is not in this sentence a newly demonstrated necessity that every coexistence has one unique source. E186 has a one-word manuscript lacuna.
- E186–187 moves to universal creaturely praise and worship, distinguishing that from merely indicating a creator by their existence. The inference invokes the broader final-cause argument and scripture, rather than being entailed by the cooperator distinction alone.
- E187–191 substantial objection: reciprocal love exists, including intensely noninstrumental-seeming love. Response first reduces legitimate marriage to each partner's enjoyment and shared reproductive/bodily goods; then admits that creatures can in fact love another absolutely, but calls this a faulty intention. E191 explicitly states the prohibited circle is impossibility of reciprocal ultimate love **with correct intention**, not impossibility of psychological occurrence. This blocks an easy but mistaken objection that any observed mutual affection refutes his theory. It also reveals that normativity is doing substantive work.
- E188–190 independent argument about afterlife pleasures: pleasure is positively experienced, not merely the absence of pain, even when pleasure and relief coexist. Worldly pains and physiological ends need not be essential prerequisites in another order; not observing an instance is different from knowing its impossibility. The argument is a rebuttal to a necessity claim, not empirical proof of an afterlife. It also shows the author accepts multiple possible causal arrangements; worldly production conditions do not define every realizable source-mode.
- E190 legal marriage is treated as reciprocal obligations. E192 the healthy-versus-disordered-taste analogy supplies a model of objective fitting desire: mere preference does not settle goodness. Application to every case of supposedly ultimate creature-love still depends on the disputed standard of health and fittingness.
- E193–194 love for God grounds legitimate love of other people. The editor flags one transmitted report as munkar in one chain with support disputed elsewhere, and cannot find the attributed Tirmidhi source of another. Do not treat all cited traditions as equally established or silently turn modern hadith judgments into the author's own claims.

## First pass E195–204
END first-195-204 was visible, untruncated; SHA 256 193a6d4d7c31721c6b41a8a280f2540151a6a5679a79b521aeadb43c3668d9b0. Actual pages E195–203; E204 not credited as a distinct actual page.

- E195 love of the prophet is derivative from love of God; the latter is asserted to be the practical monotheistic root. E196 restates expressly that joint participation in a shared cause/end is permissible, while each one's being the other's complete ultimate cause/end is not.
- E196–198 renewed synthesis: originated things need an originator; motions need an end; forced and natural motions are subordinate to voluntary motion. E197 supplies the physical examples and identifies angelic movement scripturally. Thus the universality of volition is not solely a result of excluding efficient regress. E198 again combines origin-priority and end-posteriority in self-reference; interpret against E191/200's later explicit distinction between ontic impossibility and corrupt human intentions.
- E199 reaffirms the condition/cause distinction within human activity. One act can be conditioned on another without either being its own prior originating condition. Some real causes are from humans and precede their effects. This prevents reading the theology as a denial of all creaturely efficacy.
- E200–201 central clarification: animals may pursue proximal pleasure without intending the reproductive/bodily wisdom intended by their creator. Lack of the correct higher human intention does **not** prevent the act occurring; the intended conclusion there is its corrupt or harmful character. Ontic occurrence depends on God's creating and enabling causes, whose own wisdom is separately asserted. Human agents need not explicitly know God empowers them in order actually to act through that empowerment.
- E201–202 develops the previously underexplained final object: fittingness to be desired must not be generated by the very desire it explains. Cognition of absence is derivative from positive objects; complete absence has no differentiating content. Mecca is distinguished from a pilgrim's later arrival and rites. These illustrate a prior loved thing plus later realisation, but do not establish that every prospective end has exactly that object-structure. The text's strong claim is broader than its examples. A one-word manuscript lacuna occurs E201.
- E203 concludes treatise 5. It describes the argument as newly clarified after prolonged searching; this is an authorial assessment, not a reason to waive appraisal of its bridge premises.

## First pass E205–216
END first-205-216 was visible, untruncated; SHA 256 5a53141ea86016763c4e8120a5f3d3744eaab258e2d40772f0e1ca7ed7a09889. Actual pages E205, E207–216; E206 not credited as distinct.

- E205 title; E207 begins treatise 6 on willing, causing, and accounting for absence/destruction. Cognition of a nonexistent compound is built from known positive components (a ruby mountain, second deity, future prophet, etc.). The author distinguishes this from direct primitive cognition of bare nothing. God's lack of knowledge of an alleged existent implies its nonexistence only because divine knowledge is exhaustive (E208). This is not a license to infer nonexistence from any human failure to know.
- E208–210 reports views opposing ability/will directed at absence: no thing needs no maker; no divine willing of existence is sufficient to explain nonexistence. Mu'tazilite 'annihilation' as an event/substance in no subject is criticized; rival continuance theories explain perishing by withholding renewing accidents or continuance. These are opponent reports followed by criticism, not authorial commitments to subjectless annihilation or universal momentary accidents.
- E210–211 alternative analysis distinguishes an uninitiated nonbeing from the prevention/destruction of something with an operative tendency or actual existence. In the latter case, defeating conditions call for explanation. A tendency produces only with enabling conditions and without impediments; non-occurrence may arise either through absent enabling cause or present blocker. This is a practical causal analysis, not reifying nonbeing as an independent actor.
- E211–213 resolves disputes by disambiguating 'cause': complete necessitating total condition versus incomplete productive tendency. The former contains everything required; the latter can coexist with impediments. The author states God's will is the sole individually complete cause and his loved/approved end the unrestricted complete final end, supporting this with scripture and fitra. These theological identifications are not freshly demonstrated by the preceding terminological distinction alone. Care is needed with the biconditional wording: absence of a particular sufficient factor need not exclude all other sufficient routes unless 'complete cause' here means the exhaustive full explanation.
- E213 legal 'specification of the cause' is permitted for a contributory cause, impossible by definition for a complete condition set. Rational/legal, natural/revealed settings share the distinction; merely calling something a rational cause does not eliminate possible blockers.
- E213–214 the 'knowledge causes being a knower' debate is located within the contested theory of ahwal. Those rejecting a real distinction do not posit two entities linked causally. Thus logical predication must not be silently treated as efficient production.
- E214–215 explicit causal realism: God produces **through** created means, not merely at their occurrence. Rejecting independent nature or independent human origination does not require denying natural/human causal contribution. Scriptural causal bā' supports this; the text openly opposes occasionalist formulations as he describes them.
- E215–216 linguistic objection from transitive/inchoative pairs: 'burning' or 'breaking' may denote just attempted/contributing activity, or the total act-plus-reception that includes the result. 'I taught but he did not learn' exposes the ambiguity. If reception is built in, the whole is composite. This is a particularly strong local bridge for distinguishing intrinsic active contribution from realised effect under product conditions.
- E216 contrasts efficient/final causes outside the product with material/formal constituents within it; acknowledges the latter terminology as a philosophical convention rather than ordinary Arabic. The discussion of end first in intention/last in occurrence must be read with E177/202's prior-object/later-attainment distinction.

## First pass E217–226
END first-217-226 was visible, untruncated; SHA 256 cbf0bd210a65b6019e4736223661761688fd664f7586a220e12d6bac1628b618. Actual pages E217–219, E221, E223–226; E220 and E222 not credited as distinct.

- E217 juristic 'cause' may mean an activating condition (ownership threshold for zakat, offense for liability) or the lawgiver's purpose (deterrence, justice, worship). Beneficial consequences may accrue without the agent anticipating them, which aligns with E200's distinction between proximal actor-intent and broader objective wisdom.
- E218–219 distinguishing a sign/evidence from a productive cause resolves another absence dispute. Negative facts may be evidence for positive or negative conclusions. No bare absence efficiently produces an existent, but absence of an impediment can be a component of the full condition set or a condition of a contributing cause. Ordinary legal language attributes the result to the disputed missing impediment because the positive basis is already agreed, not because nonbeing makes things. Removing harm can be a derivative final purpose while sheer nothing cannot be the first aim. This finishes treatise 6.
- E221 title; E223 begins treatise 7. Islam is analysed as submission and exclusive direction to God, with intransitive/transitive uses. 'Submitting one's face' stresses sincere intention and is joined with doing well to include right action. Semantic context matters more than imposing one flat definition on every occurrence.
- E224–226 real inward submission entails a basic degree of faith but need not entail its full obligatory realisation. Outward social 'Muslim' status can include hypocrisy. Qur'anic 'not yet' is read as anticipatory rather than permanent exclusion; denial of complete obligatory faith does not require denial of every fragment. The four-hearts report and great-sinner discussion support graded/mixed states. These are scriptural/legal-theological classifications; avoid collapsing them into empirical psychology or treating every use of 'faithless' as total unbelief.

## First pass E227–236
END first-227-236 was visible, untruncated; SHA 256 da15a572cded558c4fcc47d60a44b161cf4f060154ec9de0858ca45299b11ef7. Actual pages E227–236.

- E227–228 Islam has two opposed failures: worship-sharing and refusal to submit. The adhan's tah­lil and takbir counter these. A qualification follows: shirk may encompass pride, while humility can coexist with shirk. These are conceptual dimensions, not two necessarily disjoint sociological populations.
- E228–231 scripture supplies typologies of Christians, Israelites, Pharaoh's people. The author's generalizations are religious polemic, not independently established descriptions of all members of those communities. He combines scriptural motifs to argue arrogant refusal of one ultimate end still leaves another, possibly the self.
- E230–233 positive psychological argument: agents always have work and intention; refusal of God as ultimate object is still directed toward some final loved object. Social arrogance also depends on approval, supporters and reputation, exposing the proud person's dependence on others. This is perceptive as a diagnosis of some pride; the universal identification of every non-theistic ultimate concern with idolatry relies on the expanded functional definition of worship.
- E233 brings in the classical juristic categorization of non-People-of-the-Book in jizya discourse. E234 interprets absolute obedience in permitting/prohibiting as taking authorities for lords; the modern editor notes weakness/unknown status in the reported chain. This claim distinguishes the practical function of authority from merely naming a divine being. The electronic sentence about fighting for 'religion and obedience to God alone' under an absolute ruler looks contextually problematic; no silent correction is credited.
- E234–235 justice is both equal treatment of equals and proportionate distinction of unequals. Shirk equalizes what is maximally unequal; pride elevates a person over an equal without warrant. This yields a unifying normative conception rather than defining wrong only by injury. Its application still needs a warranted ranking, especially the divine/creature distinction.
- E236 contextual aim: understand historical and present actors to act justly toward them and learn from predecessors. Qur'anic punishment narratives are used illustratively. No independent historical verification of those applications is claimed.

## First pass E237–246
END first-237-246 was visible, untruncated; SHA 256 07f5b8ed3e2f9ae7283b98dcd925061a69974ccf1a409b072c8aeeaea7647ea2. Actual pages E237–246.

- E237 contains a hostile account of Mongol obedience and an allegation about Chinggis Khan's mother and ancestry. This is the author's unverified polemical allegation, not reliable historical evidence simply because embedded in the edition. Its gendered generalizations are likewise not adopted. This material is orthogonal to the valid local causal distinctions and should not receive inherited authority from them.
- E238 abrupt short proposition: shirk is said to entail practical disbelief in the afterlife; unrestricted divinity is contrasted with qualified creaturely lordship. Read 'every polytheist disbelieves in the afterlife' as an authorial theological inference, not the empirically false claim that all polytheistic traditions deny all postmortem existence.
- E238–244 main problem: how is shirk an injustice to God if God cannot be harmed? Extensive scriptural collection separates God's invulnerability/self-sufficiency from creatures' violated duty and self-harm. God's intrinsic claim to worship need not imply dependence on creaturely supply. This is directly relevant to source-mode perfection: entitlement, love and approval are not automatically deficiencies seeking compensation.
- E244 argues internally against a definition of wrong solely as unmerited harm: opponents already allow a rational duty of gratitude to a God who gains no benefit, so their own premises need a category of violated due right without divine injury. He rejects the further move of mapping divine duties exactly onto creaturely duties.
- E245 candid concession: rival predestinarian theologians possess substantially more truth than their opponents on divine knowledge/will/creation yet err by denying intelligible wisdom, intrinsic wrong, or rational obligation when there is no harm to God or superior commanding him. Authorial evaluation is nuanced within a polemical hierarchy; do not present it as simple blanket condemnation.
- E246 distinguishes the fact God wills from a denial of purposive wisdom. Neither 'only to expose the creature to meritorious reward' nor 'sheer will without intelligible suitability' exhausts the explanation. Editorial note suspects 'التجوير' rather than the copied 'التجويز'; register the variant without resolving it from context alone.

- E247 invariant moral-religious fundamentals shared by prophets are distinguished from varying ritual forms, quantities and penalties; Meccan/Medinan organization illustrates roots versus changing laws. Thus recognising moral reason does not imply every detailed legal rule is independently recoverable or immutable.
- E248–249 competing definitions of injustice: misplaced allocation, withheld right, and unmerited harm all require knowing the relevant due. A broad definition can be sound despite needing contextual specification. Injustice can include omission and overstepping; pure nothing without a standing entitlement is not itself an injustice. Lexical haqq spans descriptive truth/existence and normative rightful/beneficial realisation. The electronic phrase E249 'وهو المانع' is contextually suspect; no visual correction was made here.
- E250–251 expands the argument beyond harmed subjects: a poorly fitted beam, wall, garment, meal, or crop can fail the due proportion/function even though the artifact does not suffer. The beneficiary suffers the resulting failure. Legal validity is similarly success in attaining the intended normative effect. This gives a coherent model of right allocation not reducible to felt injury by the immediate recipient, while the application to intrinsic divine right still needs an independent theological premise.

- Print 248 begins the remaining Nabigha verse about a deprived basin. Bread placed beneath filth is wronged in a sense even if it cannot feel damage; wrong harms the disposer.
