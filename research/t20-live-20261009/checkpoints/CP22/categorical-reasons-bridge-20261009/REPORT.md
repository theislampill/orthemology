# Categorical reasons: the agency bridge after Railton

Bounded T20 research assessment. No integration, closure, protected conclusion change, empirical certification, or historical-time claim.

## Result

**Smith supplies a substantive route to the exact desire-independent-reasons target that Railton leaves open. His 2025 formulation strengthens the 2024 case by assessing robust agential capacities directly, rather than only compliance with theory-relative reasons. Even that stronger route does not independently establish the necessity of its preferred idealization and universal protective desires.** The remaining burden is now precise: establish that normatively ideal advice must be fixed by maximally robust lifelong agency, and that the latter requires the specified intrinsic concerns rather than merely being well supported by them.

The strongest positive result is conditional. If an independently justified account of ideal agency requires concern for every agent, and ideal advice correctly determines the non-ideal agent's reasons, then current indifference does not remove those reasons. This is a genuine categoricity result within that account. It neither grants anyone legislative authority nor proves that every actual agent will be motivated.

The 2024 paper reserves best-conception status (p. 27). The newer 2025 chapter supplies a stronger attempted necessity argument, which was subsequently read in full and is assessed in section 4a below. Stopping at the earlier reservation would understate the current case. The cited fuller 2020 chapter is not credited as read; the access ledger records the preview-only routes inspected.

## Source capsules (modern primary sources; combined under 180 words)

Michael Smith, “Rethinking The Moral Problem” (2024), pp. 7–32, rejects his earlier convergence argument as circular. He proposes socially situated, self-controlled ideal agency, assessed by parsimony and robust reason-responsiveness. Necessary convergence is limited to certain concerns; personal ends may differ. Best-conception status is assumed, and weights remain partly open.

Smith, “The Ethics of Agency: An Alternative Approach to Normative Ethics” (2025), author manuscript pp. 1–20, strengthens the case through robust knowledge-acquisition and desire-realization. It argues that protection against intrapersonal and interpersonal vulnerability requires intrinsic helping and non-interference concerns. It permits moral/non-moral trade-offs, distinguishes patients from reason-holders, and expressly leaves sanctioning authority as a further question.

Michael Bukoski, “A Critique of Smith's Constitutivism” (2016), pp. 116–146, distinguishes excellent agential functioning from fully rational advice and challenges their normative identification, the extension to other-concern, and moral content. His critique targets earlier formulations. The later repairs require fresh assessment.

## Own analysis

### 1. What would count as repairing the gap?

The inherited Railton assessment already separates objective moral appraisal from a reason binding every rational agent regardless of contingent ends. This report does not rediscover that separation. A criterion can evaluate a wrongdoer's conduct accurately even when the wrongdoer has no desire to meet it. Conversely, the presence of such a desire is not its justification.

The new task is therefore not to show once again that present desires can be mistaken. It is to defend a standard under which some ends themselves merit correction, and to show why that standard determines the reasons of the person being assessed. Those are two substantive questions. A theory might successfully identify better agential functioning without thereby settling everything worth doing. Or it might identify justified advice without showing that all ideal advisers necessarily agree on other-regarding concerns.

We should also keep three further distinctions explicit:

- A categorical reason can be pro tanto. Independence from contingent desire does not entail decisive priority in every conflict.
- A reason can be available to a capable subject without actually moving that subject. A correction mechanism explains uptake only given the relevant recognition and operation.
- A reason for respecting another's agency does not by itself give that person, or a third party, a power to issue binding specifications.

The target includes reflective reason-holders. It does not indiscriminately attribute normative reasons to every creature whose behavior admits a belief/desire explanation. The source's broad use of “agent” must be read with its competence restriction (Smith pp. 19–20). Moral patienthood is a separate issue.

### 2. A real independent consideration: fixed-standard correction

Consider a subject who knows an available, justified means to an end but whose performance can be disrupted by a competing impulse. Hold fixed the end's justified status, the relevant information, and the circumstances. A reliable capacity to detect and correct the disruption improves the subject's performance under nearby perturbations. This is an intelligible excellence independent of whether that particular end is moral.

That comparison supports a stronger account of practical competence than possession of true beliefs plus an otherwise harmonious desire set. It also explains why merely observing unmotivated assent does not settle whether a subject has a reason: recognition, motivation, correction and successful action can come apart.

The scope is important. This argument establishes the value of correction **relative to a warranted standard that remains fixed**. It does not yet establish that a lost intrinsic end should be restored, rather than that the subject's practical reasons have changed. Nor does describing a conceivable correction mechanism establish a realised naturalistic mechanism or a complete reduction of agency. The latter empirical and metaphysical questions are not resolved here.

This is the positive force I retain from the comparison at Smith pp. 21–23. Rejecting it simply because the paper aims at naturalism would be unwarranted.

### 3. The discriminating test: change an end, not just its implementation

Now change the intervention. Remove an intrinsic concern for another's agency while leaving information and instrumental organization intact. Ask what would count as recovery.

Under an account where reasons track the relevant idealized contingent ends, losing that concern may alter what the subject has reason to do. Under an account where concern for others is constitutive of correct idealization, losing it leaves the reason intact and creates a defect to be corrected. These are different standards of success. The second account can consequently classify restored concern as successful self-control where the first cannot.

That classification may be correct. But the local correction argument cannot establish it unaided: it requires that the disputed end already qualify as one which ought to survive the intervention. Otherwise a comparison of robustness quietly changes both the agent and the target whose robustness is measured.

This is not an accusation that every use of normative vocabulary makes the theory viciously circular. A shared, initially schematic concept of a reason can legitimately be filled out by competing explanatory theories. Nor is it enough to invent a contrary value assignment. The pertinent question is whether the proposed account has independent explanatory and normative advantages sufficient to justify its standards of correction. In this case, robust preservation and exercise of agential capacities is a genuine candidate advantage. What remains unearned is its identification with the appropriate standard for all practical reasons, together with the universal other-regarding restriction.

Smith's opening comparison at p. 25 requires the alternative account of reasoned criticism to be independently as credible as its rival. That is a substantive gate, not something secured merely by calling the new account more robust. His worked comparison at pp. 26–27 makes especially clear why the gate matters. The strongest fair verdict is therefore **an incompletely defended comparative premise**, not a demonstrated formal inconsistency and not an unqualified claim that he only assumed the conclusion.

### 4. Why social surroundings do not finish the inference by themselves

Whether an agent can exercise a capacity often depends on others. An isolated account that ignores obstruction, assistance and dependence can badly describe human practical excellence. Treating supportive social surroundings as relevant is therefore a serious improvement over imagining an ideally efficient solitary will.

There are nevertheless two directions of explanation:

1. Others' appropriately restrained conduct protects this person's opportunities.
2. This person has an intrinsic reason to protect everyone else's opportunities, including opportunities whose loss does not threaten this person's own success.

The first does not by itself establish the second. General reciprocity, role symmetry, common vulnerability or universal functional standards might supply a bridge. Each requires an argument specifying why it reaches the powerless case as well as mutually advantageous coordination. Merely requiring that every inhabitant of the ideal environment already possess the preferred concern makes the environment beneficial; it does not independently prove that this concern is constitutive of each inhabitant's ideality.

This objection must meet the strongest reply. The proposal is not that cooperation happens to benefit an egoist today. It evaluates agents across circumstances and places their capacity to respond correctly within a social setting. A selfish disposition can be a fragile basis for coexistence even when it succeeds locally. That provides a positive reason to investigate universal concern, and defeats the simplistic response that one fortunate exploiter is an ideal-agent counterexample.

Still, modal range and common standards must be specified independently of the desired outcome. A local advantage is not a universal agential necessity; conversely, a described exploiter is not a proved metaphysical possibility. This assessment establishes neither a possible ideal amoralist nor an impossibility theorem for one. It identifies the precise comparison the argument has to win.

The present result is stronger than the complaint that “self and other are different.” It tests whether an improvement in everyone's operating environment is being credited as an independently justified intrinsic end for each person. That issue remains after the older simple symmetry argument is replaced.

### 4a. The 2025 necessity argument changes the assessment

The newer return matters because it supplies a more independent success metric: knowledge acquisition and desire realization, rather than simply doing whatever each theory already labels reason-supported. This weakens the cross-target objection in section 3. A critic who repeated that objection unchanged would miss the improvement. The remaining objection is about the strengthened idealization and its proposed necessary implementation, not the mere use of the word “reason.”

First, being well placed to advise a subject and being a maximally robust exerciser of capacities across one's entire life are distinct specifications. Accurate advice may recommend giving up an opportunity to acquire knowledge in order to complete a worthwhile project. That trade-off is a substantive practical question. One cannot settle it merely by including lifelong robust possession and exercise of maximal capacities in the definition of the ideal adviser. The relevant challenge does not deny that dependable capacities are ordinarily good, nor demand an adviser vulnerable to ignorance. It asks why the further lifetime robustness condition belongs to rationally correct advice rather than to one kind of functional excellence.

Thus the purported contradiction for the Humean has a conditional form. It targets the conjunction of unrestricted intrinsic ends **and** the strengthened robust-agency benchmark. To reject Humeanism itself, an argument must show why the Humean must accept that benchmark as the correct analysis of ideal advice. Calling the second component a restatement does not establish the equivalence. A functional assessment could be genuinely objective while the normative identity remains disputed. This is the precise relevance of the earlier critic's kind-ideal/rational-ideal distinction after the new formulation.

Second, concede the stronger benchmark for the next test. Stable capacities need protection from earlier interference and other agents. A policy of helping and non-interference, supported by self-control, offers an intelligible mechanism. It gives the account a positive explanatory rationale. It is stronger than choosing altruistic desires arbitrarily.

But an effective, straightforward mechanism is not yet a uniquely necessary mechanism. The source initially allows protection to come from the agent or the environment, then requires particular intrinsic desires (manuscript pp. 11–12). To earn that necessity, the account must exclude relevant robust realizations through other structural, corrective or environmental supports, or show that all such realizations count as the same intrinsic concern under an independently defensible account of desire. Merely describing another mechanism would not prove it metaphysically possible; equally, calling the preferred mechanism most straightforward does not prove every alternative impossible. The outcome here is a located unproved necessity premise, not an exhibited possible ideal amoralist.

That test has strict admissibility conditions. A proposed structural safeguard is not a counterexample merely because it prevents interference. It must preserve genuine action and the same feasible desire-realization capacities, remain effective under the same relevant nearby-world perturbations, and avoid solving the problem by deleting the very options whose availability the argument assumes. No such fully qualified counterexample is claimed here.

The capability metric also needs a fixed domain. Capacity to realize any realizable intrinsic desire cannot silently become capacity to realize only antecedently permitted desires. Yet it would equally be unfair to demand simultaneous realization of incompatible desires or demand protection of interference that the account expressly excludes. The strongest reading retains the qualification that protected exercise must not itself constitute interference, and permits balancing against other reasons. The precise question is then whether the knowability, realizability, conflict and neighborhood restrictions can be specified without already installing the preferred moral ranking. If they can, the robustness argument gains independent force. If their specification depends on that ranking, the necessity argument cannot independently establish the ranking by appealing to those restricted capacities. This is a scope test, not a declaration that the source is inconsistent.

An implementation-neutral repair is available in principle: argue that any sufficiently robust agential system must instantiate a functional disposition whose content is universal protective concern, even when its physical implementation differs. That would avoid demanding one particular psychology. But it requires content-individuation and necessity arguments. Reliable non-interference alone does not establish a motive of concern, and an intrinsic concern is not identified by a theorist's favorable label. The earlier allowance for reflex-like self-correction makes it especially important to keep successful corrective operation distinct from the particular intrinsic ends attributed to it.

Third, the interpersonal inference still needs universal scope. Dependence on protection by others supports the importance of their dispositions. Showing that every properly situated agent needs such protection is a promising symmetrical argument. It does not yet show that each protector's ideality requires concern for every possible patient, including those outside the protective dependencies relevant to that protector's capacities. The account can answer through a genuinely universal functional standard; it cannot obtain that standard solely by requiring its ideal community to contain it already.

The resulting comparative verdict is substantive and defeasible. A community of agents who protect each other's capacities is a compelling model of secure agency and has recognizably moral value. The inspected arguments do not yet show that this model alone fixes all ideal advice or that its psychological content is forced by every robust realization of agency. The 2025 paper materially improves the argument without removing those two burdens.

Finally, its explicit authority discussion (manuscript pp. 16–17) confirms that wrongness, permission to sanction and an obligation to sanction differ. This is a source-supported boundary, not an external demand imposed to obstruct the account. No inference to legislative title is gained from the categoricity claim alone.

### 5. The critic must be updated too

Bukoski supplies a useful distinction, but his 2016 verdict cannot simply be transferred to a revised theory. Three safeguards matter in assessing the current argument:

- We must not construe every agential concern as absolutely overriding. Allowing trade-offs blocks an immediate refutation by a case in which one relinquishes a capacity for some other important purpose.
- We must not replace advice to the actual subject with imitation of an ideal subject. Different costs or limitations can rationally change what should be done without changing the underlying reason.
- We must not require a complete first-order code as a condition of any objective or categorical norm. Partial indeterminacy leaves room for real constraints. The question is whether later specification is constrained by independently justified reasons rather than unconstrained agreement.

These are substantive improvements, not evasions by definition. They remove easy versions of absolutism and exemplar objections. They leave the more basic problem intact: why does the selected standard of agential excellence authoritatively assess a subject's ends? The current answer is an explanatory proposal about capacities and robustness, not a reuse of the rejected convergence-from-equilibrium argument. Its credibility must be evaluated on that new ground.

Likewise, dissatisfaction with some first-order consequence is a defeasible objection, not an automatic disproof. Familiar moral judgments can be corrected. But once the theory deliberately departs from ordinary moral concepts, it must show that its replacement preserves the normative problem we needed solved. Ensuring favorable conditions for agency may be an important good while leaving other moral content or justified priorities unresolved.

### 6. The earned conditional transport

A faithful conditional application needs all of the following, rather than “ideal agents agree” alone:

1. There is a warranted, applicable idealization of this reason-capable subject; it is not merely an internally consistent description with no established admissible realization or reference.
2. Every eligible ideal adviser under that account has the relevant concern regardless of the subject's initial contingent preference. Unique agreement on every personal project is unnecessary.
3. Correct advice from that ideal standpoint really determines a reason for the non-ideal subject in this circumstance. Relevant costs, capacities and competing reasons are retained.
4. The reason under consideration is identified correctly; a shared concern need not select the same act in different circumstances.

Given these conditions, a subject cannot remove the relevant reason simply by ceasing to care. The inference is valid and addresses Railton's open target. Its warrant is only as strong as the idealization, applicability and advice premises. The paper provides a candidate substantive rationale for those premises, not a completed independent demonstration of all of them.

This transport also clarifies the target change. The theory does not establish that Railton's particular criterion of moral rightness secretly has a categoricity property Railton declines. It supplies a different account of reasons and a different moral identification. One can compare those accounts, but success for one cannot be transferred to the other without a further equivalence argument.

Even if the conditional is established, it does not show that nature is ultimately independent of God. A naturalistic normative identification and an ultimate metaphysical explanation of the relevant nature are different questions. Conversely, failure to complete this identification supplies no proof that only a personal creator could ground categorical reasons.

## Exact increment and limits

New within the bounded records checked: complete primary readings of Smith 2024 and the supplied 2025 chapter; a complete reading of the directly relevant 2016 critic; correction of older objections against the revised view; and a two-stage assessment revised in light of the stronger capacity-based argument. The resulting open premises concern ideal-advice equivalence and necessity of universal protective concerns, rather than a generic “naturalism cannot give ought” claim.

The argument earns serious comparative support for socially situated, correction-capable agency and a faithful conditional route to categoricity. The later source prevents resting this verdict solely on theory-relative compliance or the 2024 reservation. It still does not independently establish the universal concern/ideal-advice identification needed to repair the whole gap. That is the present obstruction. It does not license a contrary metaphysical model merely because one can write it consistently.
