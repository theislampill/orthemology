## 1–10 (continuous-first-pass)

The opening quotes the Imami opponent denying some divine abilities and then accusing most rivals of divine wrongdoing, causally inert human agency, absence of purposes and an undifferentiated will. Ibn Taymiyya replies by separating doctrines about predestination from imamate, tracing claims to different schools, and distinguishing actual Sunni positions. Sect genealogies and named Zaydi subgroups remain his historical assertions, not independent findings.

His positive agency account at L6 asserts genuine human action, ability and causal efficacy, including natural powers: God creates causes and effects, each creaturely cause needs a cooperating cause and absence/removal of obstacles. This is expressly different from the view attributed to al-Ashari that effects occur at rather than through created causes. L7 affirms purposive wisdom and creaturely benefit but rejects a simple transfer of identical moral standards between divine and creaturely agents. L7–10 distinguishes cosmic/decretive willing from religious willing, love and approval. He also separates willing one's own act from willing another's act: divine command need not entail the granting of effective aid. Oath-law examples and Abraham's intended sacrifice support distinctions rather than mere terminological claims.

Orthogonal material: detailed Zaydi taxonomy, election through two electors, lesser versus greater candidates, oath exceptions, abrogated fifty prayers. Electronic wording at L9–10 is suspect: L9 says command 'does not entail the second rather than the first', while surrounding explanation points the other way; L10's examples 'كالكفر والفسوق والعصيان' sit awkwardly after commands to what God loves. Record exact text without treating the problematic wording as a settled doctrinal statement.

## 11–30 (continuous-first-pass)

The opponent's injustice objection (L11) likens punishment for created unbelief to punishment for involuntary color or height. Ibn Taymiyya first distinguishes two Sunni accounts: wrongdoing intrinsically impossible because all is God's property versus possible but never divinely done (L12–13). He reports the first without making it his sole defense and favors a differentiated account. L14–17 invokes the ordinary difference between voluntary action and involuntary condition, causal formation of moral qualities, and reciprocal incoherence of invoking predestination only to excuse one's own aggression. The Adam–Moses report is construed as appeal to predestination about ensuing calamity after repentance, not permission to sin; these interpretations and report assessments are the author's.

L17–20 ties consequences to acts by created causal relations, like poison and antidote. Moral benefit/harm and perfection/defect are distinguished from the disputed rational knowability of blame and punishment. The causal argument at L19 requires a complete determinant: an occurring contingent act cannot be explained by a still-indifferent condition; regress among insufficient determinants will not actualize it. Complete motive plus ability entails action while both are created. He explicitly rejects denial of the creature's causal power. The strength here is a stated explanatory argument, not merely an appeal to predestination; its adequacy depends on the completeness and necessity premises.

L21–25 distinguishes the created act's harmful character for its human agent from God's wise creation of it, and general wise ordering from individual benefit. L22 expressly warns that human judicial analogies are not identities with divine acts: God creates choice itself rather than coercing an unwilling subject. L23–24 denies divine need for servants or compensatory exchange, while affirming benefit of commands and differentiated assistance. Crucially L25 leaves the particular total wisdom of these occurrences beyond obligatory human knowledge and refers fuller treatment elsewhere. This is a limitation of the present defense, not a demonstrated detailed theodicy or full-source sufficiency proof.

L26–30 distinguishes capacity grounding obligation, available before and without actual obedience, from complete concurrent ability sufficient with its conditions for the act. Hajj and piety commands support genuine ability before performance. The opponent's equal-assistance thesis is denied through scripture about specially given faith and through the causal demand for a discriminating determinant (L30). Orthogonal material: practical repentance and consolation, doctors and patients, judges and mistaken evidence, compensation, rain damaging some houses, psychological habituation, reported ascetic antinomian sayings. No such report has been independently authenticated.

## 46–60 (continuous-first-pass)

L46–50 supplies ten distinct replies to the alleged silencing of prophets: practical reciprocity; warnings still convey consequences; prophetic responsibility is conveyance; divine wisdom and absence of divine need distinguish creating assistance from commanding beneficial action; actual desire plus ability yields action; and both determinist and anti-determinist interlocutors lack the proposed selective excuse. L49 importantly refines terminology: the preferred linguistic usage makes capacity the enabling power, while entailment follows from that power together with will; will need not be part of the word 'capacity'. Thus the earlier two-capacity taxonomy must not be hypostatized into two independently existing agents.

L50–53 credits the opponent's Abu al-Husayn al-Basri-related school with a real convergence on complete motive plus ability. The author explicitly says truth should be accepted from any speaker and that one should not rebut error with error. His stronger condemnation of antinomian determinism than of moralistic denial of predestination is a significant internal qualification of polemical alignments. L54–57 interprets Adam–Moses through calamity versus fault, assesses rival interpretations and cites reports without our independent authentication. L58–59 discusses 'kalam' and 'jadal' as sometimes the less honorable species named by the genus, then the report of Ali and Fatima being urged to night prayer. L60 opens the opponent's next objection: divine purposelessness allegedly permits rewarding Satan and punishing the Prophet and destroys rational motivation for worship. That is the opponent's reductio, not an authorial concession. Orthogonal material includes commercial debts, warning analogies, sensory motivation, prayer practice, and a threefold taxonomy of predestination errors.

## 61–80 (continuous-first-pass)

L62–68 answers the reward-reversal objection by distinguishing power, bare permissibility, known nonoccurrence and actual promise. Some theologians know outcomes only through truthful report; others also infer them from wisdom, justice and mercy. Khadija's assessment before knowing Muhammad's prophethood is an example of character-based expectation. Moral symmetry among relevantly similar cases and asymmetry among unlike cases are defended from scriptural rebukes. Optional worship and earning rather than receiving an inheritance illustrate that benefit obtainable by another route does not make a reliably beneficial act foolish.

L69–70 quotes the opponent's attestation objection: purposeless divine action and permitted divine wrongdoing would undermine both miracle-as-confirmation and reliability of the confirmer. The reply distinguishes several routes rather than affirming them all as equally final. L73–74 denies that miracles are the sole route to recognizing a prophet, demanding evidence for that exclusion. L75–77 presents necessity-of-recognition, power-to-attest, and performative commissioning rather than truth-evaluable assertion as alternative school arguments. L78 offers intended concomitance rather than intended final purpose as a possible defense of those rejecting purposes, but explicitly calls their purposelessness view weaker and says it is not his aim to vindicate it.

His own positive boundary is clearer at L79: creating a human's blameworthy act is not itself committing that act; divine attestation of a liar would itself be lying, a defect incompatible with divine perfection. This is a substantive perfective/truthfulness premise, not derived simply from omnipotence. L79–80 also admits some predestinarian schools cannot adequately establish exclusion of defects and exposes the circularity or redundancy in proving truthfulness through consensus when exclusion of defect is itself known only by consensus. He distinguishes revelation whose meaning some humans do not know from God speaking without meaning anything. This directly preserves the attestation difficulty while refusing to conflate the author's defense with every Sunni-labelled answer. Orthodox/polemical superiority claims remain authorial claims; no external report authenticity is certified.

Will is a condition of an act's existence, not of its being obligatory (L93). Scriptural inability to hear is interpreted as intense aversion rather than missing physical power. The Abu Lahab puzzle is attributed to a stronger opposing school and called a weaker view, while noting that merely asserting rational ugliness does not refute people rejecting that moral premise.

It distinguishes God's creating from the created effect and a human's action from God's own intrinsic action (L99), refusing to predicate of God what he creates in a creature. L100 rejects mere concurrence as an adequate explanation of the distinctive role of power: life and knowledge also concur; it also challenges the division in which human power affects only an act's description rather than its existence. These are positive discriminations among causal models, not generic determinist slogans. Natural causation, growth through water, creaturely strength and acquired/inborn traits remain significant orthogonal examples. ## 101–120 (continuous-first-pass)

L101 reiterates that no creaturely cause is independent: it requires aid and can be impeded, and God creates the cause, aid and removal of opposition. The involuntary/voluntary distinction is expressly accepted. L101–105 develops a demanding anti-indifference argument: a newly actualized act, willing or becoming-agent requires a determinant, and merely citing a standing created capacity does not explain the temporal difference. Ibn Taymiyya criticizes al-Razi and others for using the argument against human indeterminism but exempting divine action through an unchanged eternal will. Necessary intrinsic attributes are not thereby posited as separately subsisting sources.

L105–110 differentiates regress in agents or completion of causal efficacy from succession among effects. The former is rejected; the latter is disputed and allowed in the author's preferred account. The inference from uninterrupted divine activity to an individually eternal sphere is expressly denied. If successive effects are possible, prior created conditions can precede the heavens without any particular effect being beginningless. L108 states the distinction between the necessary/self-sufficient and contingent/dependent case, affirms intrinsic successive divine acts and eternal capacity/perfection, and denies that an individual effected thing is coeternal with its agent. L109 separates a concurrent necessary condition from an efficient agent. These are substantial positive resources for the wider inquiry; they are not a completed proof that the whole divine source has no unexplained determinant. Polemical claims about Plato/pre-Aristotelian philosophers and all earlier religious authorities remain unaudited historical assertions.

L111–116 answers the claim that creation of good and bad acts erases gratitude and blame: sharing a creator does not erase their many other differences, benefits or harms. Human and divine gratitude are both possible; attributing the benefit to one need not exclude the other. The author distinguishes legal-prophetic sending from providential permitting/empowering of wrongful rulers or demons. Specific social applications and statements about rulers are his text, not present advice or independently verified history. L117–118 quotes a tripartite responsibility argument attributed to Musa al-Kazim as a child before Abu Hanifa; L120 challenges its missing chain and consistency with the figures' reported doctrines. The investigation has not independently adjudicated the anecdote.

## 121–140 (continuous-first-pass)

L121 adds chronological and doctrinal objections to the al-Kazim anecdote, while quoting Abu Hatim's favorable appraisal of him. These are the author's historical/report-critical claims. L122–127 turns to the substance: 'from whom' is ambiguous between the subject in whom an act inheres, the voluntary agent, and the creator of that agent and act. Different relations of attribution do not make two co-agents sharing the same role; a child's being from its mother and created by God is an analogy. The divine act/created effect distinction is essential, as is refusal to transfer a created quality or its creature-specific consequences to the Creator.

L123–126 treats scriptural attribution of evil: inclusion in a universal, reference to a proximate cause, or suppressed agent, rather than an isolated divine name of evil. It distinguishes blessing/calamity in 'what befalls you' from acts one performs, and cites fallible legal opinions of Ibn Masud, Abu Bakr and Umar as explaining 'from me and Satan' normatively rather than denying creation. L128–130 challenges an unqualified analogy between human and divine duties: foreknown misuse of a gift or weapon and divine creation of unbelievers create difficulties for the opponent too. The argument is reciprocal and role-sensitive, not a claim that every divine act is wise merely because a human analog is imagined. The final causal argument rejects a human agent's creating its own becoming-agent through circularity or a regress within finite created acts.

L131–138 quotes and answers the objection that unbelievers obey God's will and prophets oppose it. The response separates command-conformity and decretive willing, then gives the author's preferred twofold account of religious will/love and cosmic will/creation. L139–140 compares schools collapsing love/approval into willing, insisting scriptural hatred of sins and love of obedience are genuine discriminations. The transmitted joy-over-repentance, beauty, concessions and communal advice examples are retained as attributed reports. L139's movement between named groups is compressed and may contain missing/garbled wording; do not silently repair it into a neat quotation. Orthogonal themes include legal fallibility, linguistic attribution, divine names, parenting/entrustment, and affective devotional vocabulary.

The account discriminates maximal compatible benefit from indiscriminately obtaining every individually desirable item; it does not establish that any named evil is indispensable without further premises. ## 191–210 (continuous-first-pass)

L191 begins source section006 by seamlessly completing the rejected overextension of 'God cast': creation does not make God the one walking, praying or fasting. The Uthman stoning anecdote is attributed, not authenticated. L192–195 returns the enabling-known-falsehood difficulty to the opponent, distinguishes what power permits from what is known not to occur, and gives an a-fortiori perfection argument for divine life, knowledge, power and truthfulness.

L196–200 separates school accounts of speech. Under the nonvolitional account, truth is linked to knowledge as a necessary concomitant of the necessary eternal self, so its negation is impossible; this is a reported school route, not an argument for an independently subsisting necessary attribute. Under the volitional account favored by the author, intrinsic speech is wise, and a lying speech-act would be an intrinsic defect. Creating lying in another does not entail such a defect. L201–202 separates providentially 'sending' a liar from accrediting that liar and distinguishes genuine truth-entailing prophetic signs from an unusual occurrence. Extraordinary acts can accompany false claimants with counterindications; a genuine evidence of truth on a liar would be contradictory by definition. This blocks equating every wonder with attestation, but application still needs substantive criteria for recognizing genuine signs. L202 again states multiple routes to knowledge of prophetic truth beyond extraordinary signs.

L203–210 answers the alleged collapse of sanctions. Decreed past acts cannot be undone; sanctions aim at future acts, and successful prevention shows the prevented event was not actual divine decree. Medicine, food, walls and defensive measures exemplify countering one ordained cause by another. L209 distinguishes an incomplete cause from a sufficient one: a deterrent may exist without achieving deterrence, as danger need not culminate in death. Thus creating a deterrent and an undeterred act is not creating contradictory outcomes in the same respect. Orthogonal themes include preventive and remedial care, legal oaths, prophecy versus professional expertise, semantic scope of sending, and practical resistance to wrongdoing.

## 251–265 (continuous-first-pass)

L251–257 continues the hierarchy of theological schools and the critique of abstract necessary-being uniqueness proofs. L258–265 is the already inherited cooperation/tamanu discussion, now encountered in the whole-volume flow, not newly discovered. The author differentiates his preferred development from both later weak presentations and the specific Quranic divinity argument. He rejects mutually originating capacities through prior explanatory circularity; contrasts creaturely cooperation, in which independently supplied partial powers precede and combine, with two allegedly self-necessary creators whose powers cannot be obtained from a third; and rejects co-production where each is independently the complete sole producer of the identical effect. L262 explicitly calls the necessary creator's power an inseparable attribute of its own self, not a further self-standing being. The argument's important test point is how necessary agreement of wills is being construed. The author's route treats lack of unilateral action as external enabling, prevention or partial capacity; applying it to a model of intrinsic concord without such external sourcing requires preserving those premises rather than assuming them from the mere word 'agreement'. The text supplies affirmative anti-circularity and role-sensitive cooperation resources but does not by itself license confusing original productive accounting with external efficient creation. It says nothing here restricting a modern occurrence/contribution universe to finitely many types; finite-control interfaces require their own analysis. Historical rankings, anecdotes of interlocutors' conversion and claimed school consensus remain authorial assertions.

## 281–295 (continuous-first-pass)

L281–282 revisits the idols verse with grammatical attention to the circumstantial waw and polemical purpose of the sentence. Ibn Taymiyya again accepts the opponent's contextual reading and then argues that creating the crafted object's actual form includes creation of the causally inseparable human work, and that 'creating you' includes creaturely accidents. He explicitly argues that assigning reciprocal concomitants to independent ultimate producers would produce dependence. This is an additional inference, not a direct lexical identity between idols and actions.

L283–293 shifts to divine vision. The opponent attacks seeing without spatial relation and extreme occasionalist possibilities; Ibn Taymiyya distinguishes the shared affirmation of vision from particular Ashari accounts denying elevation/confrontation. He uses parity and selective-inconsistency arguments without making every school claim his own. The reasoning differentiates impossible characterizations from merely extraordinary departures from accustomed perception. L292–293 importantly separates ability, possibility, actual occurrence and present uncertainty: admitting that God could produce unseen things does not mean believing armies and mountains currently occupy one's surroundings unnoticed. He continues to reject a merely occasionalist account that removes the eye's powers and every real relation of cause/obstacle. Claims about hadith tawatur, the four schools, prophetic earthly vision and the limits of ordinary vision remain source-attributed and uncollated.

L294 opens the opponent's attack on eternal commands addressed to nonexistent people, illustrated by calling to future slaves twenty years before purchasing them. The source's development now moves from predestination/cooperation to speech; all of this remains part of whole-volume reading, including apparently orthogonal visual epistemology and grammar. Several typographic anomalies and misassigned surah labels occur; no silent correction or print-page claim is made.

## 296–310 (continuous-first-pass)

L296–299 reconstructs why a particular Kalami combination leads to eternal commands: rejecting intrinsic successive states while requiring speech in the speaker rather than in another. Ibn Taymiyya's methodological point is that inconsistency in the pair does not identify which premise to reject; the opponent cannot assume the shared premise is correct. The bearer/producer distinction is repeated for speech, life and movement. L300–303 argues positively for unlimited intrinsic voluntary speech, past as well as future, as perfection; absence of an individually eternal utterance does not imply prior total inability to speak. He notes that many arguments excluding such succession are disputed or weak, while referring full proofs elsewhere. L303's text around which two propositions are valid is compressed and needs collation before exact formal reconstruction.

L304 distinguishes speech to a mentally specified future addressee, conditional on existence, from pretending someone is presently there. Wills, future trustees, unborn children, Ali's reported address to Husayn, the Dajjal warning and prayer greetings are examples. L305 expressly disclaims making every sense of 'the Quran is eternal' the target he defends; uncreated divine speech and eternal generic capacity/voluntary speaking differ from an individually coeternal voice. L306 exactly repeats the last sentence of L305, an electronic duplication retained in coverage. Orthogonal topics include testamentary law, language of Quraysh, grammatical interpretation of 'be', the mihna, scriptural silence and changes in school doctrines. No manuscript/edition adjudication follows from the present transcription.

L307 shifts to the opponent's sweeping claim that only Imamis/Ismailis affirm prophet/imam infallibility and that rivals authorize utterly wicked leaders. L309–310 answers by distinguishing preserved transmission of revelation, correction of an initial error, disputed minor/major sins, and fallible initial judgment from what is finally affirmed as binding. The author's target is reliable final teaching, not denial that any disputed action or correction can occur. Source claims of consensus and polemical descriptions remain attributed.

These are Ibn Taymiyya's accusations and sectarian applications of scriptural passages, not neutral historical findings or present judgments about communities. The continuous reading preserves them without repeating them as verified fact. L315–316 attacks the asserted practical necessity of an available infallible imam by asking what instructional/governmental benefit an inaccessible figure actually supplies. The claimed historical comparison between early caliphates and Ali's conflicts is integral to his argument but unaudited. L317–325 moves to number and qualifications of rulers. ## 326–340 (continuous-first-pass)

L326 closes the ruler reports with the qualification about maintaining religion. L327–335 rejects the opponent's caricature of unrestricted obedience to anyone receiving one person's pledge: Ibn Taymiyya distinguishes establishment of authority from obligatory obedience, and obedience is only within right conduct, even under an otherwise just ruler. A wrongdoer's true statement or right command does not become false or forbidden merely through its speaker. He debates invalidation of a corrupt judge's every judgment and evaluates removal through greater expected harm. The anti-rebellion teaching and particular hadith readings are historical legal-theological positions in this work, not present instructions or independent verification of political facts. L333 contains a probable inversion in one shorter greater/lesser-harm sentence alongside clearer surrounding formulations; retain uncertainty.

L336–338 compares the epistemic demands on rulers and witnesses. A witness's unknown factual report may require investigation, whereas the content of a command can often be independently judged; apparent uprightness suffices for appointment rather than inaccessible impeccability. He argues wrongdoing does not annihilate every good act, citing reciprocal compensation on judgment day and scriptural retained brotherhood despite fighting. Reports about al-Walid and Ali's deputies illustrate fallible delegated administration even under a supposedly infallible leader, without independent authentication. L339's impossible-demand analogy warns that requiring unattainable perfection may end in worse practical dependence. L340 opens the opponent's attack on analogy, opinion and the four schools as later additions. Orthogonal themes include consultation, public evidence, institutions versus officeholders, moral mixedness, compensation and overstrict asceticism.

## 341–355 (continuous-first-pass)

L342 denies that accepting analogy tracks Sunni versus Shi'i affiliation cleanly, naming rejecters and supporters across the divide. L343 distinguishes a proved transmitted text, probabilistic reason and ignorance, refusing the unsupported attribution of every imamly or juristic statement to the Prophet. Its comparisons of individual scholars remain source assessments. L344 is a hostile catalogue of alleged allegorical interpretations; the claims about who held each reading are not independently checked.

L345–353 distinguishes later named schools from deliberate invention of a new religion and collection/attribution from origin of contents. Different dates, independent inquiry, internal correction and plural transmission explain later schools. L348's distributed correction thesis claims the whole community preserves truth while individuals err; it does not establish every unanimous claim without historical evidence. L351–352 retains David/Solomon and Banu Qurayza prayer examples distinguishing mistake from culpability. L353 expressly denies that agreement of the four imams alone is infallible or that truth is confined to those four; a contrary reasoned judgment from other jurists remains assessable on evidence.

L354–355 differentiates sound analogy from analogy overriding stronger text or failing relevant similarity. The burden lies on identifying the causally/relevantly shared ground of the ruling, not surface likeness. Removing a relevant difference can establish parity even if the exact common basis is not identified; converse analogy separates outcomes when the determining basis is absent. This is a useful orthogonal epistemic method: refuting many bad instances does not refute the class, and similarity only licenses transfer where the operative respect is shared. It has possible relevance to source-model analogy but is not automatically a theorem about the modern model.

## 356–370 (continuous-first-pass)

L356–358 quotes a long opponent catalogue of alleged scandalous rulings involving sexual prohibitions, penalties, paternity, intoxicants, ritual validity, stolen property and recreation. L360–362 distinguishes false attributions, minority rulings and rival-school counteraccusations. These historical legal positions and polemical claims are not modern legal advice, present endorsements of punishments or independently verified descriptions of communities.

L363–365 examines how different legal consequences of kinship need not track identically: inheritance and prohibited marriage have different scopes. It distinguishes eliminating a fixed penalty on a theory of contractual doubt from declaring the underlying act permissible. The account of distant spouses explains an attributed Hanafi legal allocation of paternity/inheritance despite absent biological generation; the author rejects the doctrine but refuses to portray it as belief in impossible physical conception. L365 also observes that the opponent's argument from common intoxicating effect itself uses analogy despite his general attack on analogy, and prefers direct textual proof where available.

L366–367 separates water's legal name and scope from an analogy about hardship, distinguishes a changed/purified ground from praying directly on unremoved impurity, and treats tanning disputes with detailed textual/report qualifications. The source notes that a report in Muslim can still be disputed by particular earlier critics; this study does not adjudicate the reports. L368–370 treats changed stolen goods as a dispute about survival of ownership, compensation, option and added workmanship, refusing the allegation that the owner's blood simply becomes unprotected. It separates mistaken interpretation, judicial resolution and deliberate manipulation. The admission-versus-testimony issue concerns the interaction of evidential thresholds, not unrestricted exemption. ## 386–400 (continuous-first-pass)

It is therefore not treated here as undisputed merely because invoked. Shared versus peculiar views must be mapped issue by issue. The author then argues for unity and moderated positions, but the associated sectarian superiority and population-wide accusations remain source claims.

## 401–415 (continuous-first-pass)

L401–404 continues the sect dossier: rival Abu Hashim successors, transmigration, al-Mughira, Abu Mansur, competing Mahdi identifications, Ismaili/Sevener accounts, Fathiyya, Waqifa and further succession variants. These are reported heresiographical claims, not independently checked group histories. The author exposes a selective numbers argument: invoking greater numbers within Shi'i subdivisions would also permit a rival appeal to the larger Sunni population. His own widespread historical accusations are not established by that logical observation.

L405–415 turns to the third claimed ground: confidence of salvation supposedly makes a guide preferable to one admitting uncertainty. The Baghdad–Kufa path analogy is quoted, followed by an apparent correction in wording at L406 whose speaker/editorial status should not be overclaimed from the stripped body. L408–409 tests the proposed criterion against rival confident political loyalties. L410 separates two indispensable warrants: existence of an infallible authority and reliable access to what that authority actually said. Fallible later books and teachers cannot acquire guaranteed reliability merely by attribution to a distant or absent authority. L412–413 uses mutually incompatible confident teachers to show the criterion is self-defeating.

L414–415 is a strong affirmative epistemic passage. A confident guide may deceive or be ignorant; a cautious one may rightly return the traveler to examination. Other possible paths must remain open. Bare assertiveness establishes neither knowledge nor truth, and suspending judgment until the evidence is clear is identified as rational practice. This is directly relevant to keeping the modern sufficiency question genuinely open rather than choosing the loudest source-claim. It does not imply skepticism about every result: the distinction is warranted evidence versus confidence alone. Orthogonal themes include scams, spiritual authority, promised rewards, fallible transmission, competing decision rules and gradual certainty.

No line beyond this volume boundary is credited. This finishes the continuous first pass of the selected electronic body, not certification of its printed apparatus or every historical claim.

## 236–246 (bounded-reflective-reread)

Reread confirms a three-part distinction often flattened by polemical summaries: (1) real but dependent creaturely efficacy, (2) complete determination by intrinsic power and will, and (3) the rejected eternally fixed necessitator of an individually coeternal world. The author explicitly accepts complete-determinant necessity (L240–242) while rejecting will supplied from outside the necessary being (L239) and allowing renewed intrinsic divine exercise (L244–246). Therefore 'necessity' is not the objection by itself, and an intrinsic attribute is not another self-subsisting source. The contingent sphere's need for another actualizer cannot be exported unchanged to the necessary being after L246 expressly excludes that export. The author's assertion still leaves a modern explanatory inquiry: what warrants treating the entire source's intrinsic exercise as fully accounted for? That is a separate bridge, not answered just by changing the graph label from effect to intrinsic act. Typographic instability in L243/L246 remains localized and is not silently repaired.

## 266–276 (bounded-reflective-reread)

Reread confirms that the attack on agreement is not merely 'two agents might disagree'. It expressly examines fixed agreement, fixed disagreement and possible alternatives, then anchors the rejection in productive conditions and complete determining source. L272–273 offers the narrow bridge in explicit form: if each will/action is part of what makes the other will/act, the cycle remains even if each is only part of the determinant. A mutual relation without this productive role is not the same premise; fatherhood/sonship are treated separately. Likewise an actually distinct contribution is not the identical intrinsic act of two bearers. Foreknowledge cannot be used as an already available external coordination device where the event known depends on the wills needing explanation. This is stronger affirmative evidence than an argument from bare modal coexistence, but interpretation must still establish when intrinsic agreement entails the alleged dependence. The author also claims universal observed interconnection against separate domains; that empirical/metaphysical premise must be retained rather than smuggled in. Nothing here refutes a universally quantified interface merely by mentioning finitely many illustrative occupations or partners.

## 194–202 (bounded-reflective-reread)

Reread confirms separate affirmative routes for truthfulness rather than a single inference from effective power: perfection excludes an intrinsic lie; the reported nonvolitional-school argument links truth to necessary knowledge; the author's preferred volitional account links truthful intrinsic speech to wisdom. L202 explicitly permits extraordinary acts by false claimants when counterindications prevent their being genuine truth-signs. 'Evidence entails what it proves' blocks a contradiction, but identifying an observed wonder as genuine evidence remains a substantive epistemic task; otherwise one could conceal the whole attestation problem in the term 'sign'. The author acknowledges diverse routes beyond miracles and refers fuller discussion elsewhere. Creation of lying in another does not constitute the creator's own speech, and necessary knowledge/truth is not an independently subsisting agent. These distinctions strengthen a source-based appraisal without warranting an automatic transition from necessary existence or efficacy to reliable revelation.

## 410–418 (bounded-reflective-reread)

Reread confirms that reliability of an ultimate authority and reliability of a received attribution are distinct premises (L410), and that confident claims do not establish either. The travel analogy explicitly allows investigation of a third route rather than forcing choice between the two advertised options (L414). L415 commends suspension until evidence, while L416 distinguishes a settled conditional from uncertain membership of a particular person in its antecedent. This supplies a constructive discipline for the larger inquiry: necessary-being acceptance, adequacy of a causal account, source independence, truthfulness and our attestation access must each retain their own warrants.
