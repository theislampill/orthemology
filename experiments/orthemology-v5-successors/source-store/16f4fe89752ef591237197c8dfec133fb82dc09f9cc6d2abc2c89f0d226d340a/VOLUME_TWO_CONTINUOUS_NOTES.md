Reader-facing study record, 7 October 2026. The interpretations and reading claims below belong to the recorded study and its bounded rereads. This edition of the notes adds no source-reading or proof credit.

## Segment 1: chunks 1–5 (002:1010 onward; see manifest)

Face 60 begins by alleging that the reason/revelation opposition cannot establish the creator it professes. The long quotation introduced as Abū Ḥāmid distinguishes three obstacles in philosophical emanation: the agent lacks elective will, the eternal world is not a temporally produced act, and the simple one cannot produce multiplicity under the one-from-one axiom. The quotation debates whether natural causation is genuine action or a transferred use of the name: burning fire, cutting sword, satisfying bread, quenching water, and the human who casts someone into fire. Its attribution must not be erased. Ibn al-Qayyim resumes explicitly with قلت and moves to the allegedly impossible, wholly negative abstract existent. Reports of adversaries and religious communities are polemical claims, not independently established history.

Face 61 gives a particularly important anti-equivocation argument. تركيب may mean assembled by another, previously separated then joined, or separable; these are denied of God. It can also be stipulated to mean a determinate essence and distinguishable attributes; those meanings are affirmed regardless of the label. افتقار can mean needing an outside producer (denied), or a thing being inseparable from its own essence and an attribute inhering in its bearer (affirmed). غير can mean separable in entity/place/time (not divine attributes), or cognitively distinguishable (affirmed). Mutual entailment of essence and attributes does not require an external producer. Necessary self-subsistence excludes need for what is outside the self; it does not imply featureless simplicity. The bearer called God is the essence qualified by full knowledge, power, life, will, and other perfections, never a bare substrate.

His critique of kalām targets a chain through accidents, motion/rest, atomism, no beginningless events, accidents lasting no two times, and homogeneity of bodies. He alleges it denies real divine activity by equating act with product, renders prophecy merely a nonpositive relation to eternal speech, and distorts resurrection into restoration of numerically identical destroyed atoms and even times. His preferred alternative is not yet fully stated here: preserve critique separately from any positive reconstruction.

Faces 62–66 attack reversal of clear/ambiguous authorities and insist on unpacking unfamiliar ambiguous terms before either acceptance or denial. The psychology is substantive: intimidating technical language, group shame, habituation, fear of appearing unintelligent, and sunk commitments lead students to accept premises and then their unwelcome consequences. He explicitly allows that some investigators initially intended knowledge but chose the wrong route. The recommended method is shared, intelligible subject matter under affirmation/negation, not word contests.

Faces 67–69 interpret Iblīs through nested syllogisms, then reject both ranking by origin and the specific superiority of fire. Orthogonal social argument: believers among slaves/non-Arabs outrank unbelieving aristocrats; virtue is not lineage, ethnicity, colour, or material origin. The lengthy earth/fire comparison combines natural observations (soil multiplies seed, fire needs a substrate, earth extinguishes fire), necessities of life, providential symbolism, ritual associations, and moral temperament (steadiness/humility versus restless destructive upwardness). It acknowledges good jinn and evil earthly humans, so material origin is not a simple moral determinant. The textual claim of prophetic human superiority over angels is specifically his school-position assertion.

Face 70 distinguishes not knowing a positive attribute from knowing its negation: lack of evidence is not evidence of absence. Rational denial requires the incompatible state established. Positive route: God is absolutely more complete than any other bearer; every revealed attribute is, he argues, a perfection whose possession is better than its absence. He explicitly recognises disagreement about which properties count as perfections. The giving-of-perfection argument says any creaturely perfection involving/implying no defect belongs more fittingly to its giver. This restriction is central, not disposable. Body/composition inferences are challenged link by link; even a conceded terminological consequence need not carry the next ontological consequence. Natural comparisons (musk/filth; fire/water; throne/earth; angel/demon) show shared generic predicates need not imply equality. His examples include power, elective activity, speech, knowledge, mercy, perception, rule, and bodily-scriptural predicates, but the inferential force of each requires separate inspection rather than assuming all are alike.

Faces 71–72: ليس كمثله شيء, no equal, and no namesake are praise because of positive unsurpassed perfections, not a blank absence shared with nonbeing. Negative scriptural predicates indicate positive fullness: no sleep entails perfected life/sustenance; no fatigue perfected power; no wrong perfected justice/self-sufficiency/mercy; no forgetting perfected knowledge; no spouse/child/partner/helper/superseding intercessor perfected independence, dominion, and sovereignty. Not being encompassed by sight does not mean not seen. Ṣamad gathers complete qualities and creaturely reliance on him; the no-hollowness gloss is claimed compatible. Ontological thesis: all perfection is existence, all privative lack defect; permanent necessary being owes its existence to no other, whereas every other existent depends on it. Do not flatten this into bare cosmological dependence: affirmative value/perfection and exclusive incomparability do major work. Last visible heading jumps from 72 to 79 in this transcript; retain numbering anomaly, do not silently repair.

## Segment 2: chunks 6–9, completing source 002 assigned interval

Face numbered 79 offers the strongest explicit unshareability argument so far: two cannot share the absolutely highest exemplar, because equality leaves neither higher than the other, while inequality leaves only one highest. Parallel absolute subjugation: if another equal resists, the claimant is not absolutely subjugating; if subdued, the other is not equal. The argument deserves its full stated force, but its premises include strict unsurpassability/exclusive dominion, not merely necessary existence. Whether “highest” excludes jointly maximal equals is an inferential issue, not something a modern conditional verification settles. The author expressly calls this an argument from positive perfection against assimilation. Four levels of the highest exemplar are carefully distinguished: divine perfections in themselves regardless of human knowledge; their cognitive presence in worshippers; verbal mention/report; and love, worship, trust, return to their bearer. This is not identifying the divine bearer with human representations or acts. Creaturely devotion is response to recognised perfection.

Faces 80–85: affects and interests shape interpretation; opponents allegedly resent unwelcome scripture. Specific reported anecdotes include recasing the Moses speech verse, a disputant’s visible disgust, resistance to hearing the closing theology of Bukhārī, and suppression of books. Keep “بلغني” reports and anonymous claims distinct from eyewitness “جرى بيني”. Four/five avenues of evasion are catalogued: denial, concealment, distortion, politically useful imagining, or unknowability. Five rival authorities are reason, juristic opinion/analogy, mystical experience, politics, and esotericism. He reads divine adjudication and the book’s detail as incompatible with those authorities overriding its import; revelation still must be interpreted, and later examples show how. His treatment of Jewish scripture here affirms relevant preserved attribute testimony as corroborating prophecy, contrasting earlier polemical allegations of degrading portrayals; neither description is a neutral historical census.

Face 86’s legal/hermeneutic material is extensive and important. Companions asked to reconcile apparent conflicts: reckoning versus interrogation; entering fire versus passing it; promised pilgrimage without specified year; consequences of wrongdoing in this life versus next; absolute injustice as shirk versus degrees of wrongdoing and corresponding security; inner thoughts, burdens, mistakes and forgetfulness; deceased persons distressed by mourners versus innocent persons punished for another’s guilt. The distinction between unrestricted perfection and a degree/instance appears explicitly: المطلق للمطلق والحصة للحصة. Examples of censuring opposition include barring women from mosques and challenges to commendation of modesty. The silverware/usury dispute is nuanced: Muʿāwiya’s allowance is read as limiting the text because workmanship’s value is separate, a permissible field of interpretive judgment, but his manner of replying suggested overriding it. Likewise Muslim inheritance from a non-Muslim is reconstructed as specifying belligerent unbelievers, compared with homicide law’s protected non-Muslim distinction. Thus not every legal disagreement equals reason defying text; charitable interpretation, scope, and manner matter.

Faces 87–88: radical defeasibility concern, that allowing unspecified rational defeaters undermines settled assent to revelation even where none is known. Long polemical intellectual history traces Iblīs; early Shīʿa, Khārijites, Qadarites, Murjiʾa as still text-appealing; Jahmī emergence, Jaʿd and execution by Khālid; translation and al-Maʾmūn/mihna; Qarmaṭians/Fatimid rule; restoration under Nūr al-Dīn/Ṣalāḥ al-Dīn; Mongols/al-Ṭūsī; and Ibn Taymiyya. Causal moral claims that every calamity springs from overriding revelation must not be promoted into verified historical explanation. The phrase calling Ṣalāḥ al-Dīn Nūr al-Dīn’s nephew is a conspicuous transmitted/historical issue to preserve rather than normalise. Execution/political claims, especially about al-Ṭūsī, need outside historiographic assessment not supplied by this authorised continuous study.

Face 89 repeats both decisive positive premises: God owns all strength, knowledge, beauty and perfection; complete perfection cannot be shared by two. Examples of cumulative creaturely beauty remaining negligible relative to divine beauty emphasize degree and asymmetry rather than denying creaturely beauty altogether. Scriptural readings, grammar of أن القوة لله جميعا, universal auditory cognition, sight, inexhaustible speech, and inability fully to praise develop that thesis. Epistemically, known truth need not await refutation of every imaginable doubt: this applies to sense and reason too. He cites changing souls/accidents, an-Nazzām’s leap, and monism as allegedly absurd theories with elaborate arguments. His positive certitude is not offered as a neutral premise accepted by every rival; report the doctrine and reconstruct the warrant separately. Face 90 orders knowledge (reason/hearing/fitra), guidance through messengers, and revealed book as nested ranges, then challenges opponents for revelation, prophetic learning, or a rational argument with accepted sound premises.

## Segment 3: chunks 10–15, source 003 continuous opening

Face 90’s continuation distinguishes mistaken followers, arrogant misleaders, and opportunistic worshippers. It separates disease of knowledge (falsehood/confusion) from disease of intention (knowing but not following), and assigns critical discernment to doubts and sound reason to desires.

Face 91 explicitly distinguishes a truth’s existence in itself from our knowledge of it. Reason may establish our knowledge of revelation without causing revelation’s actual truth. A sufficient sign entails its signified but does not always conversely follow from it: creatures entail their creator; absence of creatures does not entail his absence, and his existence does not entail theirs. Bilateral entailment gives contraposition both ways, but its application to knowledge depends on the stipulated unique epistemic route. He permits absent expected transmission to disprove an event when strong incentives to transmit would have existed; not every absence is inconclusive. Source contains an apparent duplicated contradictory clause concerning “القدح في الشرع” near this argument; no emendation is silently made.

Faces 92–97 deploy rival philosophers’ and theologians’ mutual refutations, then quote Ibn Sīnā’s Aḍḥawiyya at length. That quotation claims philosophical incorporeal unity could not be taught openly to a mass audience; scriptural depictions therefore educate imaginatively, and the same move could explain corporeal resurrection. It concedes some attribute passages cannot plausibly be ordinary Arabic metaphor, contrasts hands-above-hands and neglecting God’s side with enumerated eschatological coming, and invokes widespread Hebrew scripture as not wholly forged. Ibn al-Qayyim’s reply distinguishes an argument binding on an agreeing adversary from a demonstrative argument: agreement by negationists cannot oblige outsiders to accept the premise. He agrees revelation does not teach the philosophical negationist doctrine, taking that absence as disproof given prophetic completeness. His critique of allegedly secret elite teaching appeals to ʿAlī’s denial of a secret doctrinal deposit and the legal contents of his sheet (blood money, captive release, Muslim/non-Muslim homicide).

Faces 98–100: fitra’s aptitude for truth is compared with digestion’s receptivity to food and senses’ differentiation. Technical indoctrination is contrasted with native discrimination. A deliberately polarised positive-versus-negative divine portrait is offered as a “trial” before healthy reason. The ensuing long contrast of allegedly virtuous affirmationist lineages with vicious negationist lineages is a moral-historical polemic, not sufficient independent proof of every theological proposition and not an impartial classification. Preserve named traditions and polemical exclusions as the author’s.

Faces 101–109: cumulative epistemic and rhetorical consistency arguments from scripture’s descriptions as guidance, cure, light, proof, knowledge, truth, clear sign, tranquillity, criterion, blessing, infallible/straight, and judge. Guidance supplies the route; happiness is the end; healing removes obstacles while guidance supplies the positive means. He charges reversal of scripture’s own communicative function if it systematically teaches apparent falsehood to be negated by specialists. Important corrective within his school: “the predecessors merely recited unintelligible attributes” is rejected; they are said to understand meanings while denying creaturely assimilation. He expressly distinguishes arguing to those who accept prophetic authority from arguing to deniers: for the latter, demonstrate faulty rival premises and positive rational support, rather than simply quoting what they reject.

Language excursus at face 108: parses يبغونها عوجا by circumstantial accusative, omitted preposition, or semantic incorporation of “make crooked”/“impose”; he prefers incorporating a meaning that explains both seeking and distorting. Face 110’s sustained pilgrimage-guide analogy tests whether deliberately misleading instructions can count as benevolent guidance merely because overcoming them stimulates thought. It distinguishes obedient followers, rival route-finders, paralysed sceptics, warring groups, and noncommittal bystanders. The social-epistemic point includes superficially reconciling mutually incompatible revered authorities without understanding any.

Faces 111–118 enumerate threatened consequences: prophecy’s knowledge, eloquence and sincerity; special statuses of Adam, Abraham and Moses; interpersonal love, desire and sight of God; and genuine divine agency distinct from its products. The author does not accept the alleged impossibility of love for an eternal being or desire except for nonexistents. He restates prophecy’s double end, knowledge of the worshipped and love/obedience, considering knowledge higher. Apparent contradiction between known true revelation and known true reason is impossible; epistemic disputes instead challenge prophetic truth, transmission, or intended meaning, rather than two already conceded contradictory truths. The opponents’ possibility of unknown subtle rational defeaters becomes an argument for endless instability under their rule. He contrasts rational speculation’s scepticism with unregulated mystical discovery’s excessive affirmation/union, cites reported late returns to revealed teaching by named thinkers, and begins a four-way analysis of how to receive an apparent teaching before checking every rational objection.

## Segment 4: chunks 16–21

Faces 119–125 analyse belief’s conditions: acceptance only after an independent reason/dream/disclosure is held not to be trust in the messenger as messenger. Long direct quotation from al-Rāzī’s Nihāya distinguishes (1) prerequisites of validating revelation, unavailable from it without circularity; (2) contingent unseen particulars available from testimony (crow on Qāf); (3) matters available rationally or textually, where an unknown rational defeater allegedly prevents textual certainty. The quoted answer rejects objective moral obligation on God and denies deception where interpretation remains possible. Ibn al-Qayyim’s following indictment should not replace the quote’s own more differentiated position: it does not literally deny every possible use of testimony, but he argues its rule effectively excludes doctrinal learning from prophecy. Face 123 affirms reported divine perfections as necessary and defects as impossible; this concerns perfection/defect predicates, and must be coordinated with his later free temporally specific acts rather than generalised to necessity of every product.

Face 124 critiques overextension of expertise: competence in mathematics/other sciences does not guarantee divine science; followers infer more expertise than established, then force incompatible texts away. Face 125 distinguishes unconditional affirmation/obedience from conditional conformity to personal reasoning or desire and gives a graded judgment of religious standing, rather than one undifferentiated category.

Face 126 reconstructs a detailed Muʿtazilite-style chain: truthful prophecy needs miracle; miracle needs impossibility of divine support for a liar; that needs moral judgment, freedom from need, incorporeality, and the accidents/temporality argument. He criticizes affirmationists who accept that chain’s premises but denounce its conclusions without resolving the inference. He distinguishes those calling the method essential, al-Ashʿarī’s purported “dangerous/forbidden though not false” stance, and his own “false and self-defeating” stance. Consequences catalogued include denial of attributes, act/product collapse, impossible transition from timeless inability to capacity, and finitude or frozen terminal states of paradisal life. Alternative routes: human development, animal/plant/celestial order, and sensory/testimonial prophetic miracles. Quoted al-Khaṭṭābī calls a miracle-first route sufficient for limited intellectual capacity; Ibn al-Qayyim expressly upgrades it to among the strongest because sensory and rational evidence combine. These are claims about signs’ entailments whose factual premises still require verification; ordinary natural signs and transmitted miracles are distinct evidential classes. An extended continuous Qurʾanic natural-theology sequence covers rain, winds, clouds, seed/soil and differing fruits irrigated by one water, bodily development, sleep, languages/colours, conjugal affection, food production, waterways, navigation, solar/lunar order, and revivification. Read all, not merely title-level counted.

The inherited “end part three/begin part four” marker occurs inside chunk 19, not at the modern volume boundary. Face 127 acknowledges adversaries can excel in general rational sciences while being wrong about theology; do not caricature this as denial of all scientific ability. Faces 129–130 distinguish report/knowledge from command/desire; linguistic excursus on ولا تلبسوا الحق بالباطل وتكتموا الحق compares conjunction and “wa” of accompaniment/diversion, favouring an analysis stressing mutual entailment of concealment and mixing.

Faces 131–132 are crucial scope discipline. Shared general content is not enough to transfer distinctive consequences from creature to Creator. Any attribute has entailments qua that attribute (life involves activity/perception; knowledge reveals known objects), entailments qua divine (necessary, eternal, comprehensive), and entailments qua created (contingent, originated, created, limited, removable). Only the first general content is shared; neither bearer’s distinctive mode is transferred. “Above” entails a higher/lower relation, but not universally being supported, enclosed, or dependent on what is below: even created skies/earth show counterexamples to that universalisation. God’s specific relation to throne is sustaining it, self-sufficient toward it, encompassing it, and not encompassed or sustained by it. Thus the divine bearer and creatures are sharply distinct while ordinary semantic content remains intelligible.

Face 133 defends multiplicity of attributes/names and renewed affairs in the same bearer as necessary to genuine lordship/creation, not a defect. Fātiḥa is read dynamically: praise for acts, governance, freely bestowed mercy, judgment, guidance, blessing, anger after its cause exists. Especially explicit distinction: divine هدَاية is his act; consequent اهتداء is “their act” (فعلهم), analogous to response-verb formation. This is genuine creaturely doing, not merely the divine act with a creaturely label. For “Be, and it is”, temporal markers and conjunction indicate willing/speaking/produced existence in sequence; divine takwīn inheres in him while the product follows. Do not conflate necessary possession of active perfection with a necessitated eternal particular cosmos.

Faces 134–140 criticize those allegedly unable rationally to exclude defects while rationally excluding positive perfections. His answer to the “nonreceptive subject is neither blind nor seeing” defence is a graded perfection ordering: ability to receive life/perception is more complete than a stone incapable of either. So denying receptivity is, on his perfection metaphysics, a further descent, not successful transcendence. He expressly says he will not attribute what he merely infers as another’s doctrine and invites checking their books. This principle must constrain our reporting of his own charges too. Face 137 records universal agreement, as he claims, on an eternal necessary existent alongside vastly conflicting predications; this itself makes clear that bare acceptance of necessary being does not settle attribute disagreement.

Faces 141–142 quote Ibn Kullāb via Ibn Fūrak and Aḥmad’s anti-Jahmī text. These support divine height, ordinary upward-directed prayer, and a purported exhaustive disjunction of creation within/outside the Creator. Polemical scenario: indwelling creatures in God or God in polluted places versus distinct creation. The disjunction’s exhaustiveness is asserted as rationally immediate, not independently established merely by the quote. Preserve Ibn Kullāb/Aḥmad attribution and transmission route; the author’s ensuing explanation distinguishes subsistent bearers from inhering attributes.

## Segment 5: chunks 22–29

Face 142 continues extensive attributed documentation from al-Ashʿarī (Mūjaz/Ibāna/Maqālāt), al-Bāqillānī and others. Interpretation distinguishes elevation from ubiquitous control; if istiwāʾ merely meant control, the special throne reference would lose its contrast with all controlled things. “From behind a veil” is read as a real relation with removable barrier, distinguishing earthly from postmortem vision. These historical attributions need textual checking if used as claims about the quoted authors; their presence here is not independent verification.

Faces 143–146 criticize partial adoption of opponents’ premises, compare intellectual seduction to roadside traps, and list doctrinal areas where rival allegedly rational claims differ. The scepticism catalogue covers time, space, existence/essence, atomism, body homogeneity, events in God, prophecy, ethics, soul, speech and vision. Long reported repudiations of kalām include al-Shāfiʿī, Mālik, Aḥmad, Abū Yūsuf, and then al-Ghazālī’s nuanced account: speculative theology’s practical benefit is defensive protection of received belief, prescribed like a hazardous medicine in the right case and dose; dangers include doubts and hardened partisan attachment. Ibn al-Qayyim explicitly goes further than this prudential critique, alleging some methods/premises false in themselves. He denies that predecessors banned correct rational inquiry, sound argument, unfamiliar technical words as such, or debate to guide a seeker or defeat falsehood. He distinguishes solitary inquiry and debate, each good or bad by path, aim, and audience. The text includes coercive historical recommendations and threats against designated heretics; record as historical polemic, neither endorsement nor modern prescription.

Face 147 supplies thirty numbered subsidiary routes for divine elevation/separateness. Fitra is receptive, reason discriminating, revelation clarifying/completing. The author claims agreement across revelation and ordinary human cultures, and compiles reports from al-Ashʿarī, Ibn Kullāb, Ibn ʿAbd al-Barr, al-Sijzī, al-Ṭalamankī, Naṣr al-Maqdisī, Abū Nuʿaym, the Qādirī creed, Maʿmar al-Iṣfahānī, Abū Ḥātim/Abū Zurʿa, Ibn Qudāma, al-Qurṭubī, Qutayba, Isḥāq, al-Awzāʿī, Abū Ḥanīfa, Ibn al-Mubārak, Aḥmad, al-Shāfiʿī, al-Bukhārī, and Ibn Rushd. The corpus has diverse transmitted wordings (including settling/sitting in certain reports), and explicit denials of need for throne, fatigue/rest, contact, mixture, or known modality in others. Preserve those speakers and qualifications, not one flattened quote. Political history around Maḥmūd, Ibn Fūrak and al-Qādir includes “it is said” and variant assassination allegations, not verified narrative.

Thirty-route architecture, kept at full conceptual scope:
1. Given spherical world and real separation, absolute outside is above; below belongs inside. This relies on his cosmography and dichotomies.
2. Upward prayer is claimed innate and universal rather than learned.
3–4. Choose the perfection rather than its opposed defect: life not death, knowledge not ignorance, separation not indwelling, above not below; surrounding the world entails superiority of location, while even flat-world supposition retains the nobility-of-above argument. He explicitly answers “merely rhetorical” by claiming the evaluative premise is rationally immediate.
5–9. Subsistence, external existence, nonidentity, noninherence and separation are linked in dichotomous arguments; abstract mental existence is not the real Creator. An outside-minds analogy extends to outside-world. These are real ontological arguments, but the move from distinctness to spatially locatable separateness is substantive and must not be hidden in formal notation.
10–14. Denying elevation is distinguished from rejecting food/sleep/offspring. Shared height need not mean shared essence; even created levels differ. “Body” must be disambiguated (mere height, atoms, matter/form, distinguishable features). Ibn Rushd’s Aristotelian place as inner containing surface is used: outside the cosmic containing bodies need not be place in that sense. A nonbodily entity above is argued less unintelligible than one neither within nor outside.
15–18. Sensory reference, determinate essence, finite/infinite alternatives, and graded receptivity to elevation. Conceiving abstract alternatives does not establish external possibility. Three ranks: incapable of a perfection, capable but deprived, possessing it.
19–20. Those affirming supremacy in power/status must explain why their anti-bodily argument spares these but eliminates essential height. Al-Rāzī is quoted accepting inseparable attributes resting in necessary essence without an outside cause; Ibn Sīnā’s intelligible forms allegedly concede corresponding plurality. This is an adversary-relative consistency argument as well as affirmative metaphysics.
21–24. Self-subsistence is positive perfection, not merely the negative “no substrate”; qiyūmiyya is standing by self and sustaining others. Necessary independence entails subsistence; subsistence with another subsistent supposedly entails distinct separation. A uniquely determinate essence cannot be only negation. The independence route is explicitly distinguished from the subsistence route because it starts further upstream.
25–26. Possibility/actuality of beatific vision and divine seeing both allegedly require real relational separation. More complete existence is more eligible for sight; present nonvision reflects weakness of the beholder, not deficient object. Mirror example distinguishes represented image from actual face; the optics is historical, not current scientific evidence.
27. Relational attributes (knowledge, power, will, mercy, sight/hearing) differ from nonrelational perfections (life/beauty). Their objects are distinct from attributes, and attributes inhere in their bearer; separation is attributed through the bearer, not independent attribute-agents.
28. Composition senses: existence/essence, existence/necessity, essence/attributes do not imply an external cause; atoms and matter/form are separate disputed analyses. A necessary being need not be stripped of positive content.
29–30. Mere conceptual difference (apple flavour versus colour in one apple) does not rule out indwelling/identity. Divine separateness is claimed maximal, across essence, attributes, acts, necessity, priority and ultimacy. Alternatives union, indwelling, and neither-in-nor-out are compared with distinct transcendence. This establishes what the author claims by mubāyana; it cannot simply be replaced with generic causal independence.

At the end, he explicitly counts these thirty subsidiary routes into the main argument and resumes at 178; do not invent faces 148–177 as an unrelated missing section. Face 178 attacks simultaneous distortion of revelation, reasoning, fitra and language; “above them” and the explicit visual comparison with clear sun/moon are said to resist transfer to honour/rank or bare knowledge.

Face 179’s substantial Ibn ʿAqīl quotation extends criticism to mystical authority and political expediency. Its resident physician versus itinerant quack analogy differentiates slow safe remedies from immediately gratifying but harmful intervention; soap cleans but wears fabric. It values productive livelihood over idle self-styled spiritual communities, warns both scholars and tradespeople against their characteristic temptations, and explicitly denies that Sunnism consists merely in partisan loves, ʿĀshūrāʾ practices, or conspicuous ritual gestures. Tradition and juristic understanding are complementary; inherited sectarian custom is not proof. Preserve quotation boundaries and allegations of sexual/music abuse as polemical reports.

Faces 180–183 move from prophetic prudence and reported community welfare to superior cognition, then scripture’s sufficiency. Face 183 begins a sustained account of the heart’s constitutive loving need: worldly gains do not satisfy ultimate longing; one may possess a disposition without consciously recognising it. It distinguishes dependence on God as maker/sustainer/healer from dependence on God as beloved/end. This second, teleological/devotional dependence is additional substantive material beyond a common-source thesis.

## Segment 6: chunks 30–35

Faces 183–186 continue the psychology of love and its two obstacles: ignorance of the beloved’s perfection and corrupted preference for another. Divine maker-dependence and beloved/end-dependence remain distinguishable. “Not estimating God’s due worth” differs from impossible exhaustive estimation: people can fulfil their proportionate duty while never comprehending infinite greatness. Power to send revelation, transform the cosmos, and defeat idols is treated as constitutive of due honour. Foreknown/written determination and actual creating/disposing are distinct levels of qadar; the actual product follows rather than becoming identical with the knowledge.

Faces 187–190 reprise incomparability as positive fullness, then develop difference among grandeur of essence, attributes, acts, speech and meanings. The same predicate is modulated by its bearer: all are claimed of God according to his mode, not only “valuable in our minds.” Created interior/exterior completion examples include Ṭālūt’s knowledge and bodily stature, Joseph’s beauty and chastity, Gabriel’s strength and splendid appearance, paradisal radiance and joy, clothing and piety. These examples support the author’s value scale but are not independent proof that every bodily-sounding feature transfers to God. “God is greater” ranges over essence, rank, meaning, might, majesty and acts in his usage.

Face 191 is a substantial Arabic philology excursus. Dhāt begins as feminine possessive dhū; what follows can be an inherent attribute (power) or separate owned creature (throne). “In God’s dhāt/side” in poetry and devotion means his cause/path rather than a physical part or metaphysical essence. The author corrects the claim that “knower of dhāt al-ṣudūr” simply means knowing chests; it means their concealed contents. Absolute definite al-dhāt as entity’s essence is judged later coined Arabic, comparable with māhiyya/kayfiyya/kammiyya; he does not therefore prohibit using it if rightly understood. Neologisms versus native expression, analogy, and adverbial “by himself” receive discussion. This is evidence against treating all later vocabulary as intrinsically illegitimate.

Faces 192–195: disputation with divine teaching, coercion and inducement, alleged defeat of argument followed by persecution, and the vulnerabilities created by inconsistent shared premises. The text’s universal providential account of calamity and human fault is a theological claim; no inference about a particular contemporary victim is authorised. Multiple readings of “no path for disbelievers over believers” are set out, including proof, afterlife, nonlasting domination, and believers’ own self-caused openings. Face 195 is a long archive of anti-Jahmī reports, mostly via al-Bukhārī’s Khalq afʿāl al-ʿibād and other named collections: speech, Quran’s uncreated status, height/vision, stories of Jaʿd/Jahm/Bishr, reports of denunciation, marital/prayer/testimony/inheritance sanctions, and violent prescriptions. These must be attributed, not endorsed; chains and compiler statements do not alone authenticate their contents. The archive also contains positive reports of divine call heard near/far, angelic response to speech, martyrs’ postmortem dialogue, judgement without interpreter, and devotional approach through scripture. The author claims the transmitters satisfy knowledge and justice conditions for testimony; this is part of his justificatory strategy. His contrast of religious sect origins is strongly polemical and not a verified history of present communities.

Face 196 uses bad downstream consequences to interrogate first principles. “Only one comes from one” allegedly requires featureless source and, by recursion, a chain unable to yield observed diversity; real extra aspects reintroduce multiplicity, merely privative ones cannot cause real plurality. This is an independent anti-emanation criticism, not by itself the positive proof of a unique source. The “every determinate property needs an external determiner” rule similarly threatens the determinate necessary source itself. Repeated distinction: voluntary intrinsic act is not identical with separate product. Qadar criticism targets an absolute exclusion of God’s willing/creating creaturely acts, because then creaturely wills themselves originate without a creator; this does not yet erase human action, expressly affirmed earlier.

Faces 197–199 trace a methodological cascade from unstable conjectural premises to interpretive rejection, polemical labels, and hostile loyalties. Knowledge and certainty cannot be secured by technical vocabulary alone. Moral/affective consequences of denying attributes include undermining love, praise, joy, repentance, confidence, and vision; reducing love to bare willing would identify everything divinely willed as loved, a distinction he rejects. He connects moral wisdom with prophetic evidence: allowing arbitrary support of deceptive claimants threatens inference from miracle to truth, while moralist rivals face a duty-to-inquire circle if duty itself depends on prior inquiry. Face 200 opens a new important critique of the rule excluding mercy/love/joy/anger because they supposedly entail pleasure/pain or appetite/aversion; keep following attribution and the author’s response distinct.

## Segment 7: chunks 36–41, face 200’s thirty replies and 231–237

Face 200 uses contextual disambiguation of appetite, pleasure, pain, aversion, affect and “influence.” Animal need-based appetite is excluded; willing/loving is not excluded by redescribing it with an unseemly word. Desire for learning, meeting someone, pilgrimage, medicine or martyrdom shows ordinary “desire” exceeds sensual appetite. Learning opponents’ technical meanings is explicitly legitimate and needed, compared with learning another script/language and translating local drink terminology. Shared semantic content plus differing distinctive content must both be retained. God has the most complete form of every affirmed attribute, without creature-specific defects. Fear is excluded because it implies defective knowledge/power or involuntary vulnerability; anger against evil need imply none of those.

The cited anti-pleasure proof: an omnipotent deity would have to create the enjoyable object eternally (impossible) or, if later, could have created it earlier and should have been compelled by enjoyment to do so. The author replies in thirty stages, later counted into the running nominal total (200→230). Important distinctions:
- Same challenge would undermine admitted will/love/mercy unless they are distinguished by mode and object.
- “Pleasure/pain” are unscriptural ambiguous terms, neither unconditionally affirmed nor denied. Scriptural joy, love, patience under offense, and anger are affirmed; external injury and acquisition of missing perfection are denied. Offense (adhā) is explicitly not damaging harm.
- A being loving good and hating evil is intrinsically more complete than one indifferent to both. Negating grief, remorse and distress is based on full knowledge/power, unlike stripping moral affection altogether.
- Self-knowledge and self-love of already complete intrinsic beauty provide an object independent of created goods. Divine self-praise exceeds every creature’s praise, because self-knowledge is fullest. Perfections given to creatures are loved there, and even more fittingly loved in their divine source.
- Pleasure is not simply cognition of an agreeable thing: thing, cognition, and consequent joy are distinct. Reflexive love or cognition does not require two numerically separate bearers; one self can stand under different respects, as self-correction and self-knowledge show.
- Temporally responsive delight in repentance is real. The traveler recovering his lost camel/food/water supplies an a fortiori comparison of joy, not equality of need or degree. Forgiveness and repentance require prior fault, so an ordered good cannot be realised before its prerequisite.
- “Influence” distinguishes an external supplier improving a deficient deity (denied) from creatures performing acts that occasion approved/angry divine responses (affirmed). The latter’s agents, conditions and occurrences are themselves created by God’s wise will, so no rival independent external source supplies divine perfection. Prayer, rescue, hearing and vision already involve real responsive relations. This does not make creature acts unreal.
- Complete decisive motivation plus complete power entails action, but mere available enjoyability does not itself entail a decisive motivation. Even humans can decline additional pleasure without harm; God does not suffer deprivation.
- Successive events cannot all occur simultaneously. Their time, quantity, attributes and prerequisites are part of what makes them intended/loved. Desire for all temporal goods does not entail creation of all goods at an impossible single eternal instant. His divine case cannot be settled by importing a creaturely need-based compulsion.
- An opponent who allows eternal will with only renewed relations can give the parallel defence of love/joy, although this is a dialectical fallback, not replacing the author’s own real renewed-acts account.

Replies 20–30 examine praise ethically and liturgically. Al-Rāzī’s description of praise as reporting deserved enjoyable treatment threatens divine praiseworthiness if joy is then impossible; al-Āmidī’s reduction to approval supplies another partial account. Author’s synthesis: praise includes true cognitive report with love/approval, blame true report with disapproval; blessing and curse primarily request, with information following. It is not merely an inert proposition nor merely appetite. Witnessing corresponds to truthful report, intercession to merciful request; habitual slander/curse compromises these roles. Extensive reports on prayer, opening praise, bowing/prostration, final testimony, intercession, dhikr counts, gratitude, answered supplication, and hope supply the lived devotional stakes. These reports vary in cited source/status; “in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Ḥākim” is the book/source claim here, not our independent authentication. The moral argument rejects both equalizing human and divine obligation and stripping acts of intrinsic morally relevant properties. His position combines real fittingness/unfittingness, complete divine wisdom, and lack of external coercive standard. God does not do what deserves blame, though adversaries may falsely insult him; offense does not lessen him. Love of excusing oneself through messengers combines with love of praise, thus punishment after warning is presented as vindicated justice.

Faces 231–235 return to communicative intent and semantics. A text cannot systematically mean what its words cannot convey, especially their contradictory opposite, while claiming clear public guidance. Rejecting scriptural love/mercy but retaining will often imports exactly the dependence/composition objections supposedly escaped. Most important face 234 rejects both “divine predicates literal only in creatures” AND “literal only in God, metaphorical in creatures.” Life/knowledge/power genuinely apply to both, without equal realisation. Pure homonymy would undermine intelligibility; shared meaning or analogical graded predication is preferred. Tashkīk is explicitly a species of tawāṭuʾ, whose instances may differ in priority/fullness. Creaturely attributes and efficacy must therefore not be demoted to empty metaphors. Faces 236–237 appeal to exemplary prophetic lives, practical fruits, tranquillity and relief from interpretive distress; those claims have their own historical/psychological premises and cannot simply prove every detail to someone not already sharing them.

## Segment 8: chunks 42–46, final received body

Face 237 parses Qurʾan 4:65 through ten intensifications: oath, negation, temporal act, ḥattā, universal relative clause, absence of distress, indefinite in negation, general adjudication, positive submission beyond no distress, and cognate accusative. Face 238’s anti-taqlīd discussion opposes assigning all doctrinal inquiry to theologians and all legal inquiry to later school specialists such that scripture becomes useless. When prophetic instruction is unknown, a scholar’s opinion may permissibly be followed; it does not become intrinsically compulsory, especially against a known text. This is graded epistemic recourse, not denial that expertise assists interpretation.

Face 239 separates three denials (transmission, meaning, authority) and three counterarguments (rival text, reason, inherited authorities). It discusses the risk that a reporter hears only a quoted statement after missing its introduction, and rejects using that bare possibility indiscriminately against a broad transmitted corpus. Important for our own method: author-versus-reported-speaker boundaries are exactly at stake. Examples include an alleged reclining report, a Zubayr attribution story, al-Zamakhsharī on throne-bearers’ faith, a Jonah comparison, alleged “where” denial, and no-encompassing-vision versus positive seeing. The author’s authenticity judgments are reported, not independently re-proved here. Face 240 explicitly acknowledges that a valid entailment argument depends on its entailment premise: he argues settled belief in an infallibly truthful informed speaker excludes denying the established intended assertion. It does not by bare logic settle which assertions have that status.

Face 241 opens the completion-of-religion verse with Friday/ʿArafa reports, then the inherited electronic text jumps abruptly to “as transmitted from him, I concede…” and Iblīs’s seven questions. This is a visible textual seam; do not manufacture its missing introduction or treat the unattributed new voice as Ibn al-Qayyim’s assertion. The reported story is associated in the passage with an interpreter of the Gospel. Crucially, Ibn al-Qayyim explicitly says it is Israelite transmission neither affirmed nor denied and probably a constructed debate placed on Iblīs’s lips. He answers the arguments regardless of historicity. Questions concern creation despite foreknown evil, imposing obligations despite divine self-sufficiency, particular prostration, punishment, access to Adam, asymmetric temptation of descendants, and delay of destruction.

Competing replies are kept separate: naturalizing philosophers dissolve Adam/Iblīs/angels into psychological forces and stable species processes; voluntarist predestinarians deny purposes/moral fittingness; Qadarites deny divine production/willing of human evil, with old foreknowledge denial distinguished from later foreknowledge-plus-trial explanations. The author rejects all three as incomplete: genuine natural powers/causes, purposes, moral differences, and creature agency must remain together with comprehensive divine knowledge/will/power. He explicitly censures denying “causes, powers, natures, wisdoms and praiseworthy ends.”

His own replies 1–15 (received text stops during 15’s natural-theology elaboration) distinguish seeking understanding from hostile accusation and limit a creature’s grasp of exhaustive reasons. General justified knowledge of perfect wisdom need not await knowing each detail; the craft-expert comparison is epistemic humility, though its application requires the asserted perfect-expert premise. Command/ban benefit the dependent creature, not a needy God; inner nourishment and health parallel bodily nutrition. Bare “because ownership allows anything” is not his substantive resolution. Most forceful positive synthesis is reply 14: God does not acquire completeness through action; his actions issue from complete attributes, while creatures may be perfected through their actions. Names such as Creator, Provider, Forgiver, Wise entail manifested effects appropriate to them; wisdom ranges over every created/decreed/commanded/forbidden thing, as knowledge over all knowables and power over its appropriate objects. This is a strong necessary expressive-efficacy claim at the attribute/kind level. It does not automatically fix one particular product eternally or make all temporal effects simultaneous; face 200 explicitly forbids that flattening.

Reply 15 moves into broad natural observation and arts: bodily design, camels, birds, domesticated animals and stored resources; proposed derivation of steelyard counterweights from the camel’s balanced neck/head and arches from its back; visual benefit of sky/green-water viewing; alternation of sun/day and dark/night for activity/rest/temperature; seasonal plant life, cloud formation and wind-functions; calendar times for contracts, pilgrimage and lifespan; moonlight for nocturnal agriculture, navigation by stars; planetary versus common celestial motions. These are historically situated explanatory claims, not modern scientific verification. Specific claims such as no animal/plant life where polar daylight is extreme and moonlight’s cooling/hardening are not to be treated as established modern facts. The author attributes real cooperative roles to heat/cold/light/dark, winds, animal anatomy and human inventive reasoning; these are genuine explanatory intermediaries under divine wisdom. The final received sentence ends “حسبنا الله ونعم الوكيل,” matching the supplied printed-end boundary; no later missing promised major section is reconstructed.
